## How to Write a Strong Literature Review WITHOUT A.I. (2024-02-29, https://youtu.be/JRyVJKj4z7E)

- **Core thesis**: A good literature review is not a list of prior research; its one defining quality is "evidence of an organizing mind at work." AI tools are "language producing machines," not thinking machines, so they cannot supply synthesis, prioritisation, or judgment. To write a good review you must become a scholar before you become a researcher.
- **Named concepts / frameworks**
  - **Researcher vs. Scholar**: *Researchers* (typical grad students) are eager to run their own study, know the field only shallowly, and write the lit review afterwards as an afterthought/catalog. *Scholars* know the field in depth, see connections, shortcomings, limitations and implications, and write the lit review *before* designing the experiment, because the review is the basis of quality research. Slogan: "In a world of researchers, be a scholar."
  - **Literature review as methodology**: just as a study's method must fit its research question, the review's method of gathering and synthesising must be chosen deliberately.
  - **Types of literature review**: (1) **Narrative** – most common; writer selects whatever research is relevant, loose on coverage, organised into a coherent pattern. (2) **Systematic** – strict, states selection criteria like a methods section so another writer would reproduce it; reduces author bias (e.g. citing friends); common in health sciences. (3) **Meta-analysis** – pools data from studies on the same question and uses statistics to draw findings across them. (4) **Focused reviews** – *methodological* (designs/methods used before; reveals holes and needed new methods) and *bibliographic* (ensures in-depth field knowledge before dissertation; makes students scholars first).
  - **"Evidence of an organizing mind at work"** – the single quality all good lit reviews share.
- **Process / workflow**
  1. Decide what kind of writer you are (aim for scholar); write the review before designing the study.
  2. Plan the review's scope decisions: only recent studies vs. exhaustive; whether to cover controversies; critique each study vs. list findings; layout (chronological, by viewpoint, by country, etc.).
  3. Choose the review type that fits.
  4. Draft with synthesis, priorities, and cross-disciplinary links; check against the five peer-review criticisms.
- **Rules of thumb / diagnostics** – five real peer-review criticisms to self-check against:
  1. Merely lists papers without signalling relative importance, so your perspective is unclear.
  2. Chronological order – fine for a textbook, not for introducing research; selection/description must be driven by your hypothesis and themes.
  3. Theory section and recent empirical section lack a clear connection/coherence.
  4. Reads like a collection of separate summaries; needs an overarching evaluation that interconnects studies.
  5. Stays within disciplinary limits; draw links across disciplines.
  - **Three pro tips**: (1) Don't just summarize, synthesize (analyse, connect studies to each other and to your study). (2) Set your priorities – make value judgments about more/less important research. (3) Push the boundaries – look outside your discipline.
- **Examples**: montage of AI-tool hype clips; his university asking grad students for a bibliographic review before the dissertation; supervisor asking for a methodological review.
- **Stance notes**: Against outsourcing lit reviews to AI and against influencers promoting it; says editors can tell "glib" AI text. Concedes AI offers conveniences. Against treating the review as a catalog/afterthought and against chronological organisation.

## English Professor Breaks Down Grammar Nazis (2024-03-08, https://youtu.be/-mEdJPYahxo)

- **Core thesis**: He cares about "good and bad rather than right and wrong." Many prescriptive "rules" are historical accidents; the sound basis for preferring one sentence over another is the effect of structure on the reader's mind (cognitive linguistics). Learn the rules (your tools) so you can break them properly.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Cognitive linguistics basis for style**: ask what effect a sentence structure/pattern has on the reader's psychology and processing; this gives a foundation to say one sentence is *better* even though, as descriptivists say, language evolves.
  - **Prescriptivists vs. descriptivists**: "grammar Nazis" = prescriptivists who recite rules without knowing their history.
  - **End of sentence as "prime real estate"**: the position before the period (or comma) gets emphasis/"oomph"; give it to an **open-class word** (content: noun, adverb, direct object) rather than a **closed-class** grammatical word such as a preposition.
  - **Synthetic vs. analytic languages**: Latin expresses grammar by word-form changes; English by word order – so Latin rules (no sentence-final prepositions) do not transfer.
  - **Nominative vs. objective case** (I/me, she/her, who/whom).
- **Rule-by-rule verdicts**
  1. *Double negative* – not a real rule. "Aren't any" and "aren't no" are equally double negatives; the London dialect won because grammar books were written there. Useful deliberately to avoid the positive form ("it wasn't uninteresting").
  2. *"Me and her" vs. "she and I"* – real rule. Diagnostic: remove the other person and see if the sentence still works ("me went to the store" fails). People who know it sometimes break it to avoid sounding pedantic.
  3. *Ending a sentence with a preposition* – not a rule (Latin-enamoured 17th–18th century grammarians, Bishop Lowth). But in formal/academic writing he recommends following it as *style*: (a) keeps the preposition tied to its object; (b) saves the sentence-final emphasis slot for a substantive word. Natural in speech.
  4. *Run-on sentence (comma splice)* – real, but a punctuation matter: joining independent clauses with only a comma. The comma has "no grammatical power"; historically marks a breath pause for reading aloud. Meaningless for speech.
  5. *Subject–verb agreement* – real. Compound subject joined by "and" takes plural; but if the items refer to the same entity, singular (Lolita / light of my life / fire of my loins = one person).
  6. *Who/whom* – same nominative/objective distinction.
  7. *Oxford comma* – often redundant, but sometimes omission creates ambiguity; his rule: be consistent, so use it always.
  8. *Dangling participle* – real and tricky. A participle (-ing) phrase set off by a comma modifies what it sits next to. "Watching from the ground below, the birds flew higher" is wrong; fix by making the watcher the subject.
- **Examples**: College Humor sketch; Mick Jagger ("can't get no"); Benjamin Dreyer's Oxford-comma example where a list could be read as describing one person; Nabokov's *Lolita* opening; "he is a person I have great respect for" vs. "for whom."
- **Stance notes**: Against blind rule-following and rule-citing without knowing origins. Sides *with* the prescriptivists on sentence-final prepositions in formal writing, for reader-effect reasons, not because it is a rule. Closing maxim (attributed to the Dalai Lama): learn the rules so you know how to break them properly.

## Big Writing Mistakes Freshmen Must Stop Making (2024-03-20, https://youtu.be/sHclRP3xJK8)

- **Core thesis**: University writing is not better high-school writing; it is a *different task*. High school rewards writing well about what you know (summary); university demands analysis, i.e. an **argument**, written to invite critique so a community can find truth together. "Writing is thinking."
- **Named concepts**
  - **Watch analogy (inverted proverb)**: "If someone asks the time, don't tell them how to build a watch" – in academic writing the opposite holds: don't just say what something is (parts of the watch = summary); write about *how it works* (analysis).
  - **Flashlight in the dark room**: each person sees only part; we share partial knowledge to see the bigger picture, and the vehicle for sharing is argument.
  - **Argument as peer review**: like the scientific method – publish so others try to break it; whatever survives attack becomes accepted knowledge. For questions science cannot test (good/bad, meaning of art), argument is the only method.
  - **Three crucial pieces of an argument**: (1) **Claim** (a truth claim) with three features: *significant* (develops understanding), *non-obvious* (reader wants to see how you got there), *debatable* (someone could disagree). Test: reader should say "Hm, that's interesting, I'd like to know more"; "water is wet" fails. (2) **Evidence and reasoning** backing the claim. (3) **Limitations, objections, counterarguments** – acknowledge and respond.
  - **Hedges and boosters**: hedges (sometimes, usually, generally) lower certainty; boosters (absolutely, strongly, certainly) raise it. Careful writers calibrate claim strength.
- **Process / workflow**
  1. Before your first paper, book the university Writing Center.
  2. Unless the prompt says "summary," write an analysis/argument.
  3. Form a claim passing the three tests; support with evidence + reasoning.
  4. Anticipate critics; use the template: "Of course my critics would argue X, but what they fail to understand is A, B and C; therefore my claim stands."
  5. Adjust claim strength with hedges/boosters; bring your writing into conversation with others.
- **Examples**: his first university essay came back with "Who are you?" beside an "I think" – lesson that personal opinion without argument carries no weight. Science students say they publish for prestige; he reframes it as inviting critique. "It seems as though water could most probably be wet" as parody of over-hedging.
- **Stance notes**: Blames high-school teaching (feedback on spelling/organisation/vocabulary; content irrelevant). Admits heavy hedging makes weak reading, but defends it as a necessary consequence of argumentative writing; overly strong claims get rejected or attacked. High school: write what you know; university: write to discover.

## Literature Review SECRETS from Hollywood Writers (2024-04-06, https://youtu.be/rC2O0J5k2eM)

- **Core thesis**: A literature review must tell a *story*, not just present a *plot* (organised list). The goal is persuasion: make your research look like the missing piece of the puzzle, and build a likeable, trustworthy authorial persona.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Plot vs. Story**: *Plot* = the events seen on screen (in a review: the studies, even when organised by date/country/field). *Story* = the larger narrative including backstories and motivations that deepens the plot. "Organization is not a story; it's just the plot."
  - **Casting your literature review**: **primary studies** = main characters (most relevant/impactful; drive the plot; bring out key insights). **Minor studies** = supporting characters; not given their own paragraphs, probably no *integral citations*; positioned beside major studies to reveal meaning by comparison/contrast. Sort into **allies** (similar methods/findings) and **adversaries** (contradict or complicate). Cut any source that does not contribute to the single story.
  - **Forest vs. trees**: trees = all the research; the forest = the pattern that makes your study the missing puzzle piece.
  - **The "linear straitjacket"**: organising sources first in a grid/spreadsheet/Zotero matrix imposes grid thinking and hides the forest.
  - **Mind map technique**: write the topic in the centre; add "hooks" (big topics that come to your mind); rearrange the research around them – reveals major/minor characters and where your study sits.
  - **Author function vs. flesh-and-blood author** (credits Eric Hayot): "I" pointing *inside* the text ("I'll explain later", "I argue") vs. "I" pointing *outside* to a real person with experiences. The second adds a story layer/persona.
- **Process**
  1. Gather research; delay the matrix. 2. Mind-map with hooks. 3. Cast primary vs. minor, allies vs. adversaries; cut non-contributors. 4. Find the pattern positioning your work as the gap. 5. Write with deliberate word choices and personal pronouns to build a persona the reader likes and trusts.
- **Rules of thumb**: A review whose story is "first this study, then that one, now mine" is only information and cannot show value. Minor studies appear alongside majors, less dominantly. Avoid "I" deliberately only when you risk sounding self-congratulatory or when a controversy makes naming people unwise.
- **Examples**: *The Avengers* (plot vs. backstories; Captain America/Thor vs. Hawkeye/Black Widow); a mock "Tesseract" research matrix; Wall Street Journal review (textual "I") vs. New Yorker review (writer sitting in the theatre, jokes, pop culture) – the latter creates the second layer and is more persuasive. Meta-point: he opened with the Avengers rather than "three tips" because story makes readers receptive.
- **Stance notes**: Against list-like reviews and grid-first organisation (though he likes having a filing system). Against the blanket "never use I" rule and the belief that academic writing must be formal and impersonal; cites research that informal, personal articles get more citations.

## You're NOT a Bad Researcher! How to Easily Boost Citations (2024-05-12, https://youtu.be/kTl79dinMBM)

- **Core thesis**: Valuable research goes uncited because writers blindly follow academic-style "rulebooks." Drawing on an analysis of 30,000+ high-impact articles, three style choices correlate with publication and citation: simpler syntax, personal language in the front matter, and present tense for your own research.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Syntax as the DNA of an idea**: syntax is the code that replicates the idea in the reader's mind; complex code causes "mutations." Two elements: **content words** (nouns, verbs, adjectives) and **function words** (prepositions, determiners/articles). Many function words make a sentence heavy with branches.
  - **Make the reader feel smart** (not yourself): not dumbing down ideas, but packaging them for easy processing.
  - **Front matter vs. mid-section (empirical section)**: abstract + introduction exist to *motivate* the reader; methods + results exist for scrutiny of details.
  - **Divorce tense from time**: tense is not the same as time; no rule requires past tense for past research.
- **Three tips**
  1. *Reduce complexity*: fewer articles and prepositions in the introduction correlates with more citations. Readers will only do so much work to unpack a message; easily grasped ideas get carried and passed on as citations.
  2. *Use personal language (personal pronouns, active voice) in abstract and introduction* – captures interest, and owning ideas makes you seem authoritative and credible. But in methods/results, personal language correlates with *fewer* citations; passive, impersonal sentences that hide the author and highlight the work fit there.
  3. *Write about your own research in the present tense* – seems current, relevant, applicable; past tense reads as old news.
- **Diagnostics**: count function words per sentence, especially in the intro; check whether abstract/intro have a human presence; check tense when describing your own findings.
- **Examples**: Watson and Crick as good stylists whose paper had impact; a deliberately pompous sentence about prioritising cognitive accessibility over the originator's erudition, delivered as a joke; the Oppenheimer study (same essays with inflated vocabulary/complexity were rated worse; holds across admissions essays and research reports); dry Klein-bottle definition vs. an enthusiastic TED presenter; two restaurant reviews, present tense more appetising; "tree falls in the forest."
- **Stance notes**: Against the rulebook claims that academic writing must be complex, formal/impersonal, and past-tense. Nuanced: passive/impersonal is *right* for methods and results. Tease: review articles get more citations than IMRaD reports.

## What ANY WRITER can learn from Naval Ravikant (2024-08-26, https://youtu.be/wXF_O741o6I)

- **Core thesis**: Naval keeps Scott Adams's 250-word post "The Day You Became a Better Writer" open as a template whenever writing matters. Three lessons: simple writing is persuasive (prune), headlines must open curiosity loops, and sentences should match how brains organise ideas (agents as subjects, actions as verbs).
- **Named concepts**
  - **Kill Your Darlings** (his label, not Adams's phrase): writers get attached to early drafts; persuasion = cutting extra words. Gardener analogy: pull weeds/prune so strong branches flower.
  - **Curiosity loops**: opened when readers realise there is something they don't know. "The first line of your piece is not the first line; it's the headline." Titles used as a keyword "parking lot" give everything away.
  - **Four strategies for triggering curiosity**: (1) pose a question or incomplete puzzle; (2) signal that someone else has knowledge the reader lacks; (3) violate expectations to trigger a search for explanation; (4) present a sequence of events with an anticipated but unknown resolution. Adams's title uses expectation violation (past tense "became", addressed to "you").
  - **Syntax vs. semantics layers**: syntax is fixed (subject–verb–object/complement); semantic roles are movable: **agent** (performs the action), **process** (verb), **goal** (receives the action). Default alignment: agent=subject, process=verb, goal=object.
  - **Two problems with the passive**: (1) the goal comes first and the agent is delayed to a by-phrase, so the reader cannot picture the action until the end; (2) the action shifts into a past participle and the grammatical focus falls on a "flabby" *be* verb, draining energy.
  - **General principle**: readers want *characters in the subject position and actions in verbs*; this activates the visual brain. Applies beyond passives: "All brains work that way" beats "That is the way all brains work" (abstract pronoun subject + be-verb).
- **Process / rules of thumb**: Revise by deleting words that do not change meaning ("very happy" to "happy"). Write the headline to open a loop. Default to active voice unless there is a very good reason. Express ideas in short sticky phrases.
- **Examples**: Naval's Tim Ferriss interview; "The boy hit the ball" vs. passive version; David Foster Wallace: telling students never to use the passive is idiotic, though perhaps no more than about 5% of sentences need it.
- **Stance notes**: Explicitly warns against turning "prefer active" into "never use passive." Languages are evolving organisms that do not keep useless forms; the passive is a tool. Favors "active decisions" over useless rules.

## Incredible Books that Were NOT Recommended to Me (2024-09-19, https://youtu.be/IdS73fqqSTE)

- **Core thesis**: Unscripted "unpacking my library" tour (after Walter Benjamin's 1931 essay): pulling books out of their order reveals connections. Little direct method, but several craft practices and values surface.
- **Writing-relevant concepts and practices**
  - **Art as gift vs. commodity** (Lewis Hyde, *The Gift*): the book that "totally transformed" how he thinks about writing; a manifesto against hustle-culture, productivity-focused online writing advice that treats writing as a commodity.
  - **Copy-typing as apprenticeship**: he typed out all of McCarthy's *All the Pretty Horses* on a typewriter in two or three days to feel what makes it tick (inspired by Hunter S. Thompson typing *The Great Gatsby*).
  - **Translation as practice**: Ezra Pound's advice to W. S. Merwin – translate old Italian/Spanish poets to bring their voices into your own.
  - **"Read seeds, not twigs"** (Pound to Merwin): read enduring classics rather than contemporaries. Linked to Taleb's **Lindy effect** (what has lasted will last longer).
  - **Free indirect discourse** (Joyce, *Portrait*): third-person narration whose word choice is flavoured by the character's interiority (childish "moocow" diction, run-on rhythm), contrasted with first person (*Catcher in the Rye*) and camera-like third person (*War and Peace*, McCarthy).
  - **Punctuation/capitalisation as meaning**: Merwin's unpunctuated lines feel fluid and visual; in "For a Coming Extinction" he notes "The End" capitalised but god/him lower-case, enacting human hubris.
  - **Unsummarisable books last** (Taleb): if a book can be summarised people read the summary; *Skin in the Game* is "fractal."
  - **Clear writing is an output of clear thinking** (on Harari, *Sapiens*). Read the author's books, not out-of-context clips.
  - **Comprehensible input**: re-reading a known book (graphic-novel *Sapiens* in Korean) to acquire a language.
- **Other books mentioned**: *Blood Meridian*, *The Road*; Taleb's skin-in-the-game idea (NASA vs. SpaceX, poker without money, public universities); Esther Perel, *Mating in Captivity* (desire needs distance); David Deutsch (problems are soluble); Han Kang, *The Vegetarian*; Wohlleben, *The Hidden Life of Trees*; Richard Powers, *The Overstory* (dramatising nature). Prompt came from a David Perell post.
- **Stance notes**: Against productivity/commodity framing of writing. Defends Harari from guilt by association. Values serendipity over planned reading.

## 3 Simple Concepts that'll Make Your Writing Clear (2024-09-29, https://youtu.be/lvMkX6OX4dg)

- **Core thesis**: Clarity comes from "obsessive focus not on grammar rules but on the reader." Three reader-focused moves: short grammatical subjects, actions in verbs (no zombie nouns), and old-to-new information flow.
- **Named concepts**
  - **The tunnel (hold-your-breath) analogy**: the verb is the end of the tunnel – it tells what is going on and how parts relate. Readers hold their breath from the sentence start until the verb. **Tip 1: keep the grammatical subject short**; get to the verb quickly.
  - **Zombie nouns** (nominalisations): the action – the "soul" of the verb – has left the verb and is packed in a noun phrase. The verb is the "beating heart" of the sentence. Spotting tip: endings *-tion, -ment, -ence* (not exhaustive).
  - **Three-step formula to fix zombie nouns**: (1) Find the actions and put them back into verbs (transformation to transform; detection to detect; "presence" is just *be*, so drop it; make non-finite verbs finite). (2) Decide which character performs each action and make it the subject (if the agent is unknown, the passive is acceptable: "the quarantine zone was destroyed"). (3) Now that you have clauses, decide their logical relationship (cause/effect, time) and join them: subordinators (as, since), -ing clauses, modal verbs ("allowed to" becomes "could").
  - **Flow = old-to-new**: sentences are pieces of a 3-D puzzle; readers need to see how each links to the whole. Like a swimmer pushing off the pool wall, each sentence starts with old information and ends with new; the last words of one set up the start of the next.
- **Process (editing pass)**: 1. Check subject length/verb position. 2. Hunt zombie nouns; apply the three steps. 3. Check sentence openings against prior sentence endings; reorder (passive if necessary) so old comes first.
- **Examples**
  - Overloaded subject with verb "is given" at the very end vs. "This video explains in detail why…".
  - Zombie sentences rewritten: "The virus mutated, causing the humans to transform into zombies"; "As the zombies moved, the humans detected their presence nearby"; "Since the quarantine zone was destroyed, the zombies could infiltrate the safe areas."
  - Aluminum paragraph: the sentence starting "numerous products…" breaks flow; fix by starting with aluminum/"it" and ending with the products (uses a passive: "it is used by many products…"), so that "used/use" echo and "products" pushes into the next sentence.
- **Stance notes**: Flow for the reader produces the psychological state of flow, "the hallmark of good writing." Quietly demonstrates that the passive is sometimes the right tool.

## 4 Writing Hacks that Readers Love (2025-01-13, https://youtu.be/_X0UERlwz7Y)

- **Core thesis**: If readers cannot focus, it is the writer's fault. Good writing is designed: like a programmer who understands machine architecture, the writer must understand how the reader's mind processes text. Four features: topic consistency, old-to-new flow, visual memory, and minimal metadiscourse.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Writing is like coding**: transfer an idea into a processor; abandon surface tricks/memorised scripts and study the architecture. "Brain-optimized" / "user-friendly" writing. **Writing tools, not writing rules.**
  - **The two skis**: readers manage two levels at once – ski 1: what is this *sentence* about (its **topic**, the first thing read, "star of the show"); ski 2: what is this *passage* about. When sentence topics scatter, the skis diverge. Writing is read at several "levels of resolution" (like an impressionist painting); the larger unit sets the context for each sentence, so sentence-level grammar advice is insufficient.
  - **Three steps for topics**: (1) choose sentence topics carefully and make them the grammatical subject; (2) keep topics short, concrete, familiar – picturable; (3) do not vary topics for variety's sake – repetition shows what the passage is about.
  - **Old-to-new framework / relay race**: first words link back, last words link forward with new, unanticipated information. The passive may be the best tool for this.
  - **Memory is many activities**: recalling a fact, a melody, a painting, a childhood home, an abstract definition all feel different; we remember images, human actions and sensory information, not words; the visual brain is older than the language brain.
  - **Three steps for visual/memorable writing**: (1) concrete language and examples (not "I was in Jakarta" but sights, sounds, smells, feelings); (2) flesh-and-blood characters; (3) if you must discuss abstractions, make them characters – subjects of verbs that tell a story.
  - **Metadiscourse / signposting**: writing about writing ("Chapter 2 discusses…", "The preceding paragraph demonstrates…"). Hangover from oral rhetoric ("tell them what you're going to tell them…"). Unnecessary when the path is clear.
  - **Alternatives to metadiscourse**: (a) signpost sparingly, as in conversation – introduce topics with a question ("What social factors make birth rates decline?"); (b) use visual language treating content as happening in the world ("As we have seen…") so writer and reader are the characters, not units of text; (c) movement/co-operation metaphors ("we arrive at…", "let's finish with…").
- **Examples**: Pinker's *The Language Instinct* passage, scrambled so topics jump (biological makeup, children, you, every individual, general abilities) then restored with "language" as consistent topic; Csikszentmihalyi happiness passage where the passive version ("…defended privately by each person") fits because it keeps "happiness" as topic and ends on "each person," echoed by the next sentence's "People who…"; the six "remember…" prompts; a self-parodying metadiscourse paragraph; birth-rate sentences rewritten.
- **Stance notes**: Against "rules-based writing teachers" (names Strunk and White) on the passive, against "avoid repetition," and against heavy signposting. Deleting metadiscourse is not enough; you must organise what remains.

## How to Write a Great Sentence (2025-01-23, https://youtu.be/vFsQgIQFtwM)

- **Core thesis**: Comma-vs-semicolon worries "don't matter"; grammar is necessary ingredients but beside the point. Writers must love their raw material – the *sentence*, not the word – focus on each sentence's intended effect, and practise sentence *forms* daily the way musicians practise scales.
- **Named concepts**
  - **"I just like the smell of paint"**: a painter friend's answer; you become a writer by loving the basic material. That material is sentences, because meaning arises from *relationships between words* (demonstrated by scrambling a sentence).
  - **Sentence bird-watching**: keep lists of *sentences* (not words) with new or startling patterns; get curious about them.
  - **Best-meal analogy**: you remember the occasion and context, not ingredients or techniques; grammar and vocabulary are ingredients, but no recipe yields a great sentence.
  - **Great sentences are successful sentences**: they achieve their intended effect in context and meet reader expectations. Even "bad" can be great in the right context (so-bad-it's-good cover on a radio show about annoying music).
  - **Window vs. window frame**: great sentences open a window on the world; rule-based advice is IKEA instructions for the frame.
  - **Vase and flowers** (Chomsky's "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously"): grammatical form without meaning = vase without flowers; the scrambled version = flowers, no vase. Practise **form not content, structure not meaning**.
  - **Writers' scales = "Great Sentences Mad Libs"**: take a model sentence, keep its structure, swap words slot by slot. You feel which fillers fit ("'twas cold/early/Denmark") and which do not ("'twas wood/the"; "the color balloons") without naming rules. Better than scales because it generates new sentences. He offers a sheet of 10 model sentences.
  - **DeLillo's fresh-page technique**: after finishing any paragraph, start the next on a new page – "no crowded pages" – to see a set of sentences clearly. Writer as sentence sculptor.
- **Process / exercises** (the video promises three daily exercises; identifiable ones): (1) collect striking sentences while reading; (2) imitate via Mad Libs substitution on model sentences; (3) draft one paragraph per page to inspect sentences. While drafting ask: "What am I trying to do with this sentence? What do I want the reader to see?"
- **Examples**: DeLillo's Paris Review interview (constructs sentences, hears a rhythm, words have sculptural quality); Bergen, Norway dinner anecdote (remembers the story and the reindeer, not techniques); Carnegie Hall "practice" joke; Jabberwocky's first stanza as a Mad Libs frame.
- **Stance notes**: Against grammar-first and rule-based advice; against waiting for inspiration ("if you think you must be inspired every time, try this practice"). Reassures that this is not "broccoli in my ice cream" – not a sneaky grammar lesson.

## Cross-video patterns in this batch

1. **Reader psychology over rules (the master principle).** Stated first in the grammar video ("good and bad rather than right and wrong"; cognitive linguistics) and repeated everywhere: "obsessive focus not on grammar rules but on the reader," "writing tools not writing rules," "make active decisions about your own writing." Rules are judged by effect on processing, attention, memory and persuasion. His recurring foil is "the rulebooks" / "rules-based writing teachers" (Strunk and White named).

2. **The core sentence model** recurs in four videos with consistent terms:
   - Characters/agents in the subject slot; actions in verbs (Naval; zombie nouns; abstractions-as-characters in Hacks).
   - Short subjects; reach the verb fast (tunnel).
   - Topic = first position; keep topics consistent, short, concrete, familiar (two skis).
   - Old information first, new last; sentence end is "prime real estate" for emphasis (grammar video) and the launch pad for the next sentence (swimmer / relay race).
   - Fewer function words = lighter syntax (citations video); prune extra words (Kill Your Darlings).
   These fit into one editing sequence: verbs, then subjects/characters, then topic strings, then old-to-new links, then end-weight, then cut.

3. **The passive voice: a developing, nuanced position.** Aug 2024: default to active, because the passive delays the agent and centres a flabby *be*; yet "never use passive" is wrong. Sept 2024: passive used when the agent is unknown and to repair flow. Jan 2025: a full demonstration that the passive is the *better* choice when it maintains topic and old-to-new. May 2024 adds a genre rule: impersonal/passive in methods and results, personal/active in abstract and intro. Net rule: voice is chosen by topic continuity, information flow, agent knowledge and section purpose.

4. **Visual, concrete, human.** "Clear writing is visual"; the visual brain is older than language; people relate to people. 5. **Story and curiosity as persuasion engines.** Plot vs. story, casting studies as characters, curiosity loops, headline as first line, opening videos with analogies rather than lists (he comments on this meta-move himself). 6. **Academic writing as argument within a community.** Claim (significant, non-obvious, debatable) + evidence/reasoning + counterarguments, calibrated by hedges/boosters; lit review as evidence of "an organizing mind" – synthesis, prioritisation, cross-disciplinary links, the study as missing puzzle piece. "Writing is thinking"; "clear writing is an output of clear thinking" (Harari remark). AI tools produce language, not thinking.

7. **Practice and apprenticeship.** Sentence is the unit of craft: collect sentences, imitate forms (Mad Libs scales), copy-type admired books, translate, one paragraph per page, read "seeds not twigs." Writing as gift/art rather than commodity sits beside the engineering metaphors (code, DNA, design).
