## How to Start Writing for Beginners (2025-01-30, https://youtu.be/Tgpx_04p2g8)

- **Core thesis**: The thing blocking beginners is editing while producing. Separate the two processes, practise "non-editing" in short timed sessions, and let ideas grow on the page; then alternate between "working with words" and "working with ideas" so you never face a blank page.
- **Named concepts / frameworks**
  - **"Write with the door closed, rewrite with the door open"** (Stephen King's editor John Gould): writing = telling yourself the story, audience is you; rewriting = focus shifts to the reader (polish, cut, clarify). Keep the two processes separate.
  - **Non-editing**: timed production with no backspacing, no second-guessing, fingers never leave keyboard. "When you're writing you're producing; when you're editing you're perfecting."
  - **Virtuous procrastination**: editing (or "just a bit more research", tea, tidying) used as a respectable way to avoid the discomfort of writing.
  - **How ideas grow (X to Y)**: start a timer writing about belief X; while writing you discover X is shakier than thought; you write about the uncertainty; a new idea Y clicks; you develop Y. "Writing is thinking"; new ideas sound stupid at first because they have never been brought into language. Ideas are like single-celled organisms: give them energy and environment, and stay out of the way.
  - **Working with words**: the messy stream-of-consciousness mode; you do not judge idea quality, you keep words flowing (painter showing up to the studio and playing with paint until patterns emerge).
  - **Working with ideas**: zooming out, reading what you wrote, and writing one-sentence summaries of ideas that stand out; or starting from one intriguing idea and free-writing while keeping it in view.
  - **Kimchi / fermentation ("funkify your ideas")**: words from timed sessions are raw ingredients; the value comes when ideas are brought together on the page and interact. A "too basic" idea A becomes a metaphor for idea B; a "cliche" idea C becomes a lens on idea D.
- **Process / workflow**
  1. Set a timer for 15 min (target ~250 words, about a sentence a minute) rather than aiming at 2,000 words in 2 hours.
  2. Write without backspacing. When stuck, use **placeholders**: type the first word that comes and add a bracket note such as [find a better word]; keep going.
  3. When the inner voice suggests research/tea/chores, refuse it; timer time is writing time.
  4. Remove distractions: plain-text editor (he uses Obsidian, not Google Docs in a browser) plus a blocker app (Opal).
  5. Schedule editing as a separate block (e.g. write in the morning, clean up after lunch).
  6. Alternate loop: 10-min working-with-words session -> zoom out, write one-sentence summaries of standout ideas at the bottom -> pick one idea, set 10–15 min timer, free-write oriented to it -> summarize again -> repeat. Either mode can come first.
- **Rules of thumb / exercises (tips for helping ideas grow)**
  - Talk to yourself in the draft; he uses "you" instead of "I" ("you believe X") to observe his thinking without attachment (borrowed from Krishnamurti's diaries).
  - When a sentence feels wrong, do not stop: write about why it feels wrong. This breaks the barrier between "real ideas" and "good ideas".
  - Do not let beginnings stop you: start with "the thing is…", "what I'm trying to say is…".
  - Pretend you have an audience and 30 minutes to get the point across; start anywhere.
  - If typing is impossible, dictate with voice-to-text under a timer.
  - If it still fails, do not force it: walk or journal, and listen to the resisting part.
- **Examples**: a French teacher in Montreal who interrupted students mid-sentence to correct errors (learners need comfort and confidence in use, not error correction; self-editing does the same to writers); his own high-school photo vs professor now (things grow in stages, acorn to tree); live demo of rejecting the cliche "like a bolt of lightning" mid-sentence as an example of the editor intruding.
- **Stance notes**: Against editing-while-drafting and against waiting for inspiration. Says mainstream advice on grammar and organization misses that readers care about value (teaser for positioning ideas as solutions to reader problems). Note: in the Aug 2025 video he criticises his own early advice like "don't edit while writing" as too generic because he had no specific reader in mind.

## 20 Years of Writing Advice in 52 mins (2025-02-24, https://youtu.be/G-Sl0-PZv2Q)

- **Core thesis**: The biggest roadblock is seeing the elements of writing as "a list of rules rather than a set of tools". He gives 31 tools spanning sentence clarity, rhetoric, narrative, idea generation and reader value. Closing caution: do not try 15 at once; pick one.
- **The 31 writing tools (his names)**
  1. **The locomotive effect**: in a detail-heavy sentence put subject and verb up front; the core clause is the engine and details hitch on like train cars. Bad case: 15 words between subject and verb forces the reader to hold the subject in short-term memory. (Two Ryan Holiday sentences from *Stillness Is the Key*.)
  2. **Deliberate grammatical mistake (he labels it "enallage")**: bend grammar to "wrench readers awake" and make a moment strange. McCarthy's *Suttree* ending ("fly them"); Dylan Thomas's "gentle" for "gently".
  3. **Forget "write at a fifth-grade level"**: language is social, not only informational. Use in-group code words that carry meaning for your readers ("prior" vs "assumption", "stochastic" vs "random" — Hanania's point against Gwern's advice). Do not use puffy words for their own sake.
  4. **Two-parter: don't fear $10 words, don't fear cheap words**. Faulkner vs Hemingway exchange; Mark Manson's title. "There are no universal rules because there are no universal readers"; writers have specific readers, not general audiences.
  5. **Ironic juxtaposition**: juxtaposition + irony; place two things side by side so each changes how you see the other (Eliot's "Prufrock" evening/etherized patient; *Life of Brian* crucifixion song).
  6. **Alliteration**: makes phrases memorable and more persuasive even when the content is nonsense (proverbs; Gatsby's last line; cites Mark Forsyth).
  7. **Ladder of abstraction**: bottom = concrete things you can see, smell, stub your toe on; top = freedom, the American dream. Move up and down; all-top is cloudy, all-bottom is mundane/journalistic (Gatsby's green light).
  8. **Polyptoton**: same word repeated in different parts of speech/cases (Jay-Z "businessman/business, man"; Beatles "please please me"; Jung on torture).
  9. **Antithesis**: formula "X is Y; not-X is not-Y". Take the ordinary and invert it; covers both what is true and what is not (Oscar Wilde). The one ideal place for a semicolon.
  10. **Writing is entering a conversation** (David Foster Wallace; Paul Millerd): the "table of researchers" parable. Bad entry: ignore prior work, present ideas in a vacuum, or elbow in saying all are wrong, or avoid their jargon. Good entry: take interest in prior work, show deference to the "big kahunas", reproduce their speech patterns to show in-group status, disagree politely, present yourself as a helping hand. Writing only for yourself is journaling.
  11. **Synesthesia**: describe one sense through another to make ideas vivid and concrete (Chandler's smell/Taj Mahal line; *Apocalypse Now* napalm; "cold stare"; David Abram's *The Spell of the Sensuous*).
  12. **Diacope**: word or phrase repeated after a brief interruption ("verbal sandwich"). Variants: vocative, elaboration, extended ("Bond, James Bond").
  13. **Epistrophe**: repeat the same phrase at the end of successive clauses, like a chorus, to keep the core message in focus while adding detail (Tom Joad's "I'll be there").
  14. **Prolepsis**: open with an ambiguous pronoun that points forward, opening a loop (Larkin's "They…", Hemingway's "He was an old man…", "It was a dark and stormy night").
  15. **Congeries**: pile up nouns, adjectives, images to create an immersive, overwhelming mosaic (DFW's cruise-ship essay opening).
  16. **Steal style**: style is "open source", ideas are not. Exercise: ask writer friends whom they admire; pick a passage; read 2–3 times; close the book; write it out from memory; scale from sentences to paragraphs to sections; slot your own ideas into those structures. (Origin: his thesis adviser imitating Walter Benn Michaels.)
  17. **Collecting scraps**: unsystematic note capture (Kendrick Lamar's key words to recover an emotion; Robert Greene/Ryan Holiday note cards). Some notes become orphans, others cluster into constellations. Tool: Obsidian (horizontal linking vs Notion's hierarchy).
  18. **Make main characters the subjects of verbs.**
  19. **Place specific actions in verbs, not nouns.** (Before/after: the nominalized "watching… took place on the part of…" vs "a clever writer was watching…").
  20. **The engine of the story**: one opening moment that raises questions and drives everything (Moss finding the money in *No Country for Old Men*). You should be able to point to it.
  21. **Value / problem statement**: clear, organized, persuasive and useless is still useless. Value = solving a problem readers care about. Never write about "topics". Template to fill at the top of the page (not necessarily published): "I am writing about [topic] because I want to find out [question] in order to help my reader [gain benefit / avoid consequence]". Delete anything not serving it.
  22. **Beware the cliche**: Orwell's "dying metaphors"; writers have a civic duty to keep language alive ("disco rice" as a living metaphor).
  23. **Understate the serious, overstate the light**: Orwell's window pane; *Schindler's List* restraint vs *Saving Private Ryan* explicitness. Let tone match the weight of the subject.
  24. **Collect interesting names** (people and places) and let the name invite the story (Pynchon characters; Saint-Louis-du-Ha! Ha!).
  25. **The Bilbo moment / Bilbo effect**: before the argument, build trust with a simple concrete story the reader can nod along to; then sequence examples simple to complex, literal to metaphorical ("be Gandalf"). Sam Harris's *Waking Up*; Nat Eliason's *Crypto Confidential*.
  26. **Rip a solo** (Frank Zappa): fixed time slot (~20 min), improvise; you have the instrument (grammar, vocabulary), imagination and taste.
  27. **AAB, not ABA**: two similar examples to hammer the point, then a contrasting example that deepens it (comedy's rule of three).
  28. **Analytical, not descriptive writing**: do not name the watch parts; explain the mechanism and pattern. Analysis, not information, creates value.
  29. **Beware zombie nouns**: restore characters as subjects and actions as verbs ("There was worry that…" -> "The students worried the teacher would assign…"). The best writing makes a movie in the mind.
  30. **No long introductory clauses**: move them to the end or split the sentence (startup pivot example).
  31. **Keep the verb next to its object**: move interrupting clauses to the start or end, choosing the position by what the next sentence begins with ("…community engagement. These communities…").
- **Stance notes**: Against rules-based advice, simplicity dogma, "avoid jargon" mavens, topic-driven writing, formal-virtue worship (clarity/organization/persuasion as ends).

## The Silent Dialogue Hack Expert Writers Use to Hook You (2025-03-29, https://youtu.be/30Ic686MRZU)

- **Core thesis**: Dialogue engages when the author steps back and lets readers make the meaning. Three escalating techniques: stop "author-splaining", write lines with multiple meanings, and use silence (ellipsis) where the most charged exchange is omitted.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Author-splaining**: the author butts into a scene to clarify every emotion or thought behind the words (like the Annie Hall subtitles, or a friend pausing the movie to explain). "One of the cardinal sins of dialogue". Cure: as with mansplaining, assume the other person has ideas of their own.
  - **Flat dialogue vs dialogue with multiple meanings**: flat dialogue is just narrative reporting speech; ambiguous dialogue makes readers co-creators who fill emotional gaps and want to discuss it afterwards.
  - **Ellipsis (the "silent dialogue hack")**: strategically leaving the dialogue itself out so the reader stands outside the window and infers.
- **Rules of thumb / checklist**
  - Delete adverbs modifying verbs of speech (any -ly word after "said"). Instead inhabit the emotion and find word choices and body language that flow from it.
  - Delete phrases that name emotional states; show through action, word choice, and what the character does not say, played against context.
  - Ask why the story breaks from narrative into dialogue at this moment; the two modes serve different purposes.
  - Leave intentional gaps or subtle contradictions; watch long speeches that reveal too much; test whether different readers could read a line differently (but not vague or vapid).
  - For embarrassment, shame or vulnerability: characters hide it in life, so describe the scene indirectly, from outside, possibly with passive constructions that enact loss of control, and skip the humiliating moment.
- **Examples**
  - Stephen King's *Carrie* (Sue and Tommy in the car): narration labels every feeling; given as the negative example, while granting King is a master storyteller.
  - McCarthy's *All the Pretty Horses* (father and son in cafe): no authorial intrusion; stirring black coffee carries the subtext.
  - Husband/wife pub exchange ("Will you be long?" / "Well, which is it?"): each line reopens interpretation.
  - Naipaul's *A House for Mr Biswas*, doll's-house purchase: no dialogue, "words were exchanged", passive verbs (accosted, was placed, wheeled); the narrative "looks away" from the money changing hands.
  - *There Will Be Blood* baptism scene: Daniel's inaudible remark to Eli while the congregation sings about power in the blood; his guess is that the line is the film's title.
- **Stance notes**: Against explanatory adverbs and emotional labelling. Trust the reader: "when you step back, you invite your readers forward."

## The Biggest Mistake in Nonfiction Writing (2025-05-04, https://youtu.be/0-YMN4Z-H_0)

- **Core thesis**: Rule books like Strunk and White's *Elements of Style* give harmless style tips but harmful grammar rules; hiding behind rules prevents voice. Replace rules with an understanding of tools, especially the passive voice, the subject-position spotlight, and the stress position.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Rules vs tools** (recurring channel principle).
  - **Subject position = spotlight**; **stress position** = end of the sentence, where emphasis naturally falls.
  - **Five situations for the passive voice**: (1) to focus on the thing acted upon; (2) when the actor does not matter (the couple were married); (3) when the actor is unknown (my wallet was stolen; politicians' "mistakes were made"); (4) to land an important word in the stress position ("the award goes to…"); (5) when it simply sounds better ("rules are meant to be broken").
  - **How to identify a true passive**: grammar = *to be* + past participle; meaning = receiver of the action comes first, doer omitted or in a "by" phrase. Diagnostic question before "fixing": "am I really changing the passive voice here, or is it another false alarm?"
  - **Copy work** (from copywriting): choose exceptional writing in any genre, commit it to memory, write it out from memory (not with the book open) to absorb structure and rhythm.
  - **Split infinitives**: fine when you want the adverb tied closely to the verb; if you want to emphasise the adverb, move it to the stress position ("polarize the public completely").
- **Rules of thumb**
  - Awkward passives are usually awkward because of misplaced emphasis, not because of passivity ("The bill was paid by me" vs "paid for by the last customer").
  - Do not accept grammar-checker or AI "passive voice" corrections blindly; they inherit Strunk and White's confusion.
- **Examples**: Strunk and White's four "passive" examples, of which only the rooster sentence is actually passive (others: existential "there were", plain past tense, adjective "impaired" after intransitive "became"). Their own sentences break their rules (negative form, passive, adjective pile-up, separated related words). E. B. White writes well because he ignores his own advice.
- **Stance notes**: "Omit needless words" is useless to beginners (they cannot tell which are needless); "be clear" is empty; "do not inject opinion" is silly. Strongly against grammar dogma baked into Grammarly/Word/AI tools; "AI cannot think" and cannot make these decisions.

## EXACTLY How to Create Flow in Your Nonfiction Writing (2025-05-29, https://youtu.be/MtbfC3gQNNE)

- **Core thesis**: Flow is a matter of reader psychology, not grammar rules: every sentence must carry both old (familiar) and new information, in that order. The passive voice exists in English precisely to make that ordering possible.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Two goals of the writer**: clarity (the reader understands) and value (the reader gets something new).
  - **Tree of knowledge** (Elon Musk): readers attach new leaves to their existing trunk and branches; writing "flows" when that attachment takes little work.
  - **The battery sentence**: each sentence needs a positive and negative charge = old and new information. All-old sentences are a "closed loop" (boring); all-new sentences are a disconnected tile the reader must leap to (confusing).
  - **Old-to-new principle**: old, simple information at the start of the sentence; new, complex information at the end. The end of one sentence sets expectations for the start of the next.
  - **Clear focus**: when sentence beginnings repeatedly link back to a single entity, producing a coherent unit of discourse.
  - **Agent / goal**: agent performs the verb's action; goal receives it.
  - German proverb gloss: "the beginning and the end shake hands with each other".
- **Process: converting to passive for flow**
  1. Move the goal into the subject position.
  2. Move the agent to the end in a "by" phrase.
  3. Insert *to be* and switch the main verb to the past participle.
- **Diagnostics**
  - For each sentence ask: what here is already known to this reader, and what is new? If nothing is new, cut or enrich; if nothing is old, add familiar context.
  - Check sentence openings: do they pick up something from the end of the previous sentence or the running theme? Put transitions such as "however" at the head of the sentence, between the ideas they contrast.
  - Reversed order makes reference chains "spaghetti with the sauce underneath".
- **Examples**: 2024 US election sentences (informativeness depends on the reader's prior knowledge); the 1977 British Open data string that becomes readable only once familiar context is added; Zebra F-301 pen description rewritten by reordering only (sentence 3 ends on durability and comfort; sentences 4 and 5 open with them; later sentences link back to the theme and end on refills, lengths, colours); Bitcoin paragraph where the active sentence puts "millions of people, from billionaires to students" first, fixed with a passive ("it is used for transactions by millions…"). DFW: telling students never to use the passive is idiotic, though perhaps only about 5% of sentences need it.
- **Stance notes**: Against "never use the passive" (Strunk and White, Grammarly) and against handing sentences to AI tools, which he says create bad habits. "Languages are organisms"; they do not keep forms that serve no purpose.

## The EXACT Writing System to Make Readers Recommend Your Book (2025-07-04, https://youtu.be/MTWhCW33mAw)

- **Core thesis**: Word-of-mouth comes from positioning, not from information or luck. School habits (be nuanced, be objective) hurt shareability; you are in the business of persuasion and need ethos and pathos as well as logos. Use the 3V framework: Vow, Victim, Villain.
- **Named framework: the 3V framework / 3V system**
  1. **Vow** – a promise so clear that wrong readers tap out before buying. Vague promises attract the wrong crowd, who punish you in reviews. Steps: (a) **name the change** – what will be different in the reader's life after the last page; "learning means your behavior changed" (hot stove test); (b) pitch it aloud in one tight sentence to a real person, then stop talking and watch their face; (c) treat every puzzled look as a note on what to sharpen and keep tweaking; (d) when strangers get it first time, freeze that line as subtitle, back cover and Amazon copy.
  2. **Victim** – the specific person in pain ("whose hair is on fire") looking for relief. Do not write for everyone. Naming the pain precisely, in their own language, is itself the persuasion (Hormozi: the pain is the pitch).
  3. **Villain** – pick a fight; a shared enemy creates belonging and shows what you stand for. Steps: (a) find what your specific reader already hates but has not named (a broken system, lazy trend, harmful shortcut); (b) call it out plainly ("throwing stones at their enemies"); (c) show how the book arms readers to fight back; they will reuse your arguments in conversation and carry the book as a badge.
- **Rules of thumb**
  - Recommendation is identity signalling: people recommend books they have not read because the title and stance say something about them.
  - A mass-market framing (Atomic Habits positioned on a near-universal pain, though really about elite performance and deliberate practice) only suits a bestseller goal; for clients/authority, narrow.
  - Better to lose wrong readers than collect bad reviews (he puts "nonfiction" in titles so fiction writers bounce).
- **Examples**: *Bad Therapy* (bold title, polarizing stance, recommended unread); *Value Proposition Design* (~2,300 reviews) vs Hormozi's *$100M Offers* (26,000+), same topic, sharper promise; his own repositioning from "I help writers" to helping experts turn knowledge into a book (lost subscribers, better leads); Harley-Davidson selling rebellion vs Honda/Yamaha; Yeti, Patagonia; his channel's villain is AI "writing slop" that ignores readers.
- **Stance notes**: Against school-style nuance and objectivity when the goal is a shared book; against broad audiences. (Compare the Dan Koe video, where he also asks for qualifiers and balance in voice.)

## 6 Types of Nonfiction Books to Start Writing Today (2025-07-13, https://youtu.be/hWHB2noYItY)

- **Core thesis**: You need a model matched to your goal. Six book types each serve a different purpose, and the purpose dictates audience, length, tone and structure.
- **The six types (his names) with how-to steps**
  1. **The personal brand builder** – for the burned-out professional ~10 years in who wants the same vocation on their own terms. You start with questions, not answers; "writing is the process through which you become an expert"; authority comes from being an author. Steps: (a) **five whys** on a bad day at work until you hit a question worth a book; (b) capture stories weekly or daily (he logs one "teachable moment" per week) and extract lessons from stories, not the reverse; (c) define the audience and bend your language around them (references, vocabulary they recognise); (d) do not wait until you are ready. Example: Ana Lorena Fábrega, *The Learning Game* (references to Taleb, Munger, Holiday, Ferriss, Naval tuned to tech-entrepreneur readers).
  2. **The new authority** – an ideas book that flips the status quo; needs an argument, not advice. Steps: (a) start with the idea that bothers you; Peter Thiel's question about an important truth few agree with you on; (b) do not hedge; make the boldest case and let readers add their own qualifications; (c) build the argument in layers as a bridge of stories, examples, analogies and answered objections; (d) expect and welcome disagreement – "an idea is only an idea if someone else could argue against it"; (e) make it a conversation starter with "bangers" worth highlighting. Example: Aaron Stupple, *The Sovereign Child* (Naval accepted ~90%).
  3. **The lead getter** – short, actionable guide; part lead magnet, part sales rep. Steps: (a) one specific urgent problem, ideally the one just before the problem your consultancy solves, or your full method as DIY; (b) test the method in the real world until proven and repeatable (one-on-ones, workshops, content) – he says his own method is not yet ready for a book; (c) build the book like a product: short (<30,000 words), skimmable, quick win; test = finishable on a short flight. Example: April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* (rejected publishers' push toward self-help, doubled rates).
  4. **The booking agent** – written for reach: podcasts and stages invite authors, not businesses. Steps: (a) position for mass-market usefulness while keeping the core message; (b) distil to one transmissible idea, ideally captured by the title; (c) lead with the book, never pitch; weave philosophy and method in subtly and let motivated readers find you. Example: Chris Voss with ghostwriter Tahl Raz, *Never Split the Difference*.
  5. **The legacy book** – wisdom for family; not market-driven, so form can be quirky and singular. Steps: (a) write for one person; (b) no lesson without a story; (c) let go of publishing. Examples: Pompliano's letters to his children (65 short letters: lesson, story/mistake, advice); Coates's *Between the World and Me*; Marcus Aurelius.
  6. **The gift** – a book that shows readers something true they have not yet articulated; tone is "straight talk", cool authority, no hemming and hawing. Steps: (a) write the book only you can write; (b) focus on the reader's transformation; razor: what should the reader understand, feel or do differently after this section? If no answer, cut. Example: Paul Millerd's *The Pathless Path* and his ~400 "curiosity conversations" with readers.
- **Named concept: an idea has three parts** – claim, evidence, explanation linking the two. Start from evidence and let the claim emerge; claim-first writers force square pegs into round holes or invent flimsy anecdotes.
- **Other principles**: writing as transcription of finished thoughts produces boring prose; expert writing is "thinking aloud", problem-solving that generates new knowledge. A book written without consideration for the reader "is a diary".
- **Stance notes**: Against waiting for permission or credentials; against the kitchen-sink book; against books as brochures; against publishers' mass-market pressure when your goal is leads. Says to hold off writing until you have a blueprint.

## What Went Wrong with Dan Koe's Book (2025-07-22, https://youtu.be/CSkWxm3flEQ)

- **Core thesis**: *The Art of Focus* disappointed fans not because the ideas lacked value but because readers could not perceive the value: wrong genre conventions (tweets vs prescriptive nonfiction), a promise the content did not keep, no structure, and a preachy persona. Four fixes follow.
- **Method**: he read dozens of 2–3 star reviews (ignoring 1-star rants) to find recurring complaints from fans.
- **Fix 1 – Chapter titles as promises.** "A chapter doesn't give readers ideas; a chapter makes a promise." For each chapter ask: what will the reader be able to do, or what new idea will replace which old idea? Turn vague noun-phrase titles into learning goals (his rewrite of "The New Rich": design your life around stillness, not status).
  - **Practical vs conceptual problems**: practical = solved by changing actions; conceptual = solved by replacing an old idea with a new one; conceptual problems sit upstream of behaviour.
- **Fix 2 – Purpose chain (start away from the book).** Readers do not owe you attention (school trained us otherwise). Sequence: (1) writer's goal / definition of success; (2) who must read the book for that goal to come true; (3) what is that person's main problem or pain; (4) design the table of contents as a **bridge** from their current situation (or belief set) to the solution; (5) filter every story, idea and example: does it help the reader solve that problem? That frame is the razor. Editing is reverse-engineering reader needs, not applying the editor's taste. "Books are tools"; without a reader it is a diary.
- **Fix 3 – Structure (coherence and cohesion).**
  - **3D cube puzzle**: sentences -> paragraphs -> sections -> chapters -> book; a misfit at any level of resolution breaks the whole. Each unit must link backward and lean forward or it becomes an orphaned idea.
  - **Grice's maxims**: readers assume sequence is intentional, so they invent links across gaps, misread, or blame themselves and quit.
  - **Colour experiment** (red-yellow-blue vs blue-yellow-red): context first, meaning second.
  - Rules: (1) **index section** at the start of every unit (first sentence of a paragraph) stating the high-level idea, then discussion; (2) one paragraph = one idea; drill: underline the main point, move it to the index position, rewrite the rest to follow; (3) use transitions to label relationships; (4) outline – the chapter needs a "table of ideas"; (5) **think in fractals** – the same pattern at every level. **The uneven U** (Eric Hayot): open with a claim, descend to specific evidence, climb back to interpretation, and end at a more general level than you began, for momentum.
  - Exploratory "pantser" drafting is fine for practice and idea development, not for compiling a book. Give readers "rails to ride on".
- **Fix 4 – Voice and persona.**
  - The author on the page is a persona made of language choices, distinct from the flesh-and-blood writer; readers can like you and dislike the page persona.
  - **Text as a stage**: a paragraph is a scene with 2–3 characters; whatever is in the subject position is the actor in the spotlight. Constant "you" as subject drags the reader onstage and sounds like finger-wagging (or a note to self).
  - Drill: underline every "you" and ask whether it is needed. Rewrite with the idea as subject ("You let your brain form grooves" -> "The brain forms grooves over time", or passive to emphasise grooves).
  - Use qualifiers (might, can, often, in some cases) to modulate claims, without over-hedging; watch dummy subjects ("there is", "it"); be intentional about persona. Aim to sound like "a partner and not a preacher".
- **Stance notes**: Not a blanket ban on "you" or personal pronouns. Here he recommends qualifiers, whereas for the "new authority" book he says avoid hedging; he frames it as balance and genre/audience dependence.

## The ultimate guide to writing WITHOUT A.I. (2025-08-08, https://youtu.be/jmUlhEAS6GE)

- **Core thesis**: AI-assisted writers converge on sameness; the advantage is original thinking. His "expert writing system" (27 steps across phases) uses writing to generate ideas, then completely rewrites for readers around a problem-solution architecture, with flow, value signalling, reader psychology and quality control.
- **Phase 1 – Foundation (steps 1–7)**
  1. **Stop writing like you're in school.** Teachers are paid readers who value easy-to-grade patterns. School hangovers: opening with background/context; narrating your whole thought process with the answer at the end. "Essay" means attempt: state the question and say out loud "I don't know; what evidence would I need?"
  2. **Communication vs results.** Reject the **garden hose** model (ideas piped head to head); adopt **GPS directions / map**: move the reader from point A (where they are) to point B. Stop asking "how do I explain my ideas?"
  3. **Value, value, value.** Clear/organized/persuasive and useless is useless. Ask what readers can do or think differently afterwards. Value is not what you find interesting.
  4. **Know exactly who you're writing for.** Generic readers do not exist; only real people with specific problems. Without a defined reader an editor can only impose pet peeves.
  5. (folded into step 4 in the video) **Anticipate resistance**: nonfiction is persuasion; people dislike changing ideas and behaviour; interview readers, read their online responses, "beat them to their objections" (King: writing as telepathy).
  6. **Learn their secret language.** Take 4–5 prominent texts in the niche, mine **value-coded language**, keep a running document, and mimic it (e.g. "problematize" in humanities; general ones: however, critical, enables).
  7. **Focus on what words do, not what they say.** Functions lie beneath words: "Sales were down 15%" can be problem, evidence or background. Every sentence is a tile on the path; if you cannot name its job, delete it. AI arranges patterns without performing reader functions.
- **Phase 2 – Process: the two-draft writing system (steps 8–10)**
  8. **Permission for terrible first drafts.** The mind is a plate of spaghetti (image from *Gödel, Escher, Bach*); thoughts only cohere when brought into language. Draft one belongs to you: ask questions, think aloud, note contradictions, ignore readers.
  9. **Writing patterns vs reading patterns.** Writer patterns (chronological discovery order, background first, thesis discovered at the bottom) create **interference patterns** that readers report only as "unclear" or "disorganized".
  10. **Completely rewrite.** Open a new document; do not copy and paste. Start from: where is my reader now, what made them pick this up, what gets them through the first sentence and paragraph? Text as guided tour, author as guide not adversary.
- **Phase 3 – Problem-solution architecture (steps 11–14)**
  11. **Start with what's broken**, never background (the recipe-blog grandmother story). The reader's first question is "why should I care?" Use problem language: despite, however, but.
  12. **Organize everything around problems and sub-problems**; sub-problems map to sections and paragraphs. The thesis is your solution. He admits the structure is a "conceit" applied only in the reader draft.
  13. **Disagree without making enemies.** Status quo -> **concession** (what has this belief helped people do?) -> **destabilizing condition** that exposes the problem, where your value begins. Enter the conversation politely. Counter-example: B. R. Myers's *The Cleanest Race*, brilliant but combative.
  14. **Build better problems with real research.** Ask what each quote or citation is supposed to do for the reader. Use sources to create tension ("Smith's findings conflict with Jones's conclusion"), making the situation more problematic, not just more informed. AI research breeds confirmation bias.
- **Phase 4 – Organization / flow (steps 15–17)**
  15. **Keep subjects consistent.** The "avoid repetition" rule is a myth. Pick 2–3 main subjects per paragraph or section and hold the camera on one until you deliberately shift (stage metaphor).
  16. **Train readers in your naming system.** Establish early that variant names refer to the same entity (e.g. "customer retention software" = "these platforms") before using them.
  17. **Choose subjects according to the reader's problems**; order sections by the sequence in which readers meet those problems, with transitions.
- **Phase 5 – Value (steps 18–20)**
  18. **Master words that signal importance.** Three-column list: **opportunity words** (enables, becomes), **cost words** (prevents, disrupts), **urgency words** (critical, crucial), plus community-specific ones.
  19. **Make value obvious in every paragraph.** Readers look for value at the start of a unit (occasionally the end, never the middle). Drill: circle value words; expect them front-loaded and at least one in each paragraph's first sentence; long stretches with none flag a problem.
  20. **Make costs and benefits personal.** Not "this creates inconsistencies" but "this prevents you from retaining your best customers". Same problem, different stakes for different audiences (academic peers vs the community centre).
- **Phase 6 – Reader psychology (steps 21–23)**
  21. **Beat readers to objections.** For each major claim anticipate the three standard objections: other causes; counter-examples / omitted evidence; different definition of terms. Like a sales script, miss one and that is the reader's exit.
  22. **Make readers feel personally invested.** Personal pronouns, humour and anecdotes are fine and persuasive, but do not overdo "you".
  23. **Confident, not arrogant.** Hedges (perhaps, seems, could) suit peer-review cultures and undermine you elsewhere. Make direct claims backed by **claim + evidence + reasoning (warrants)** that your reader will accept.
- **Phase 7 – Quality control (steps 24–27)**
  24. **Audit everything for value.** "True" or "interesting" or "I worked hard on it" are not reasons to keep material; weak content dilutes strong ideas. Ask "why should my reader care?"; rewrite or delete.
  25. **The "so what" test** on every major point: what does this do for me?
  26. **Remove everything that makes reading hard** (DFW via Bryan Garner: good writing minimises the reader's decoding work): strip writer-focused patterns.
  27. **Test with real people.** Reader feedback beats writer-friend or editor feedback; share early work even with a small audience (Millerd's curiosity conversations).
- **Daily habits (bonus)**: before writing ask what specific value you are creating; begin with a reader problem, never background; first draft for thinking; revision = complete rewrite; before publishing circle value words and check for gaps.
- **Success indicators**: readers act on your writing; you can predict objections; people who disagree still find it valuable; you write faster; your work is shared and referenced.
- **Stance notes**: Explicit self-critique – early channel advice (e.g. "don't edit while writing") was vague because he had no specific viewer in mind; coaching conversations changed that. Against AI for drafting and research (he does allow AI clean-up of dictation transcripts in the next video). Against applying fiction's "I never think about the audience" to nonfiction. Warns not to apply all steps at once.

## Writing is a two-player game (here's how to win) (2025-08-14, https://youtu.be/kiIGwqwc49Q)

- **Core thesis**: Writing is a two-player game: you move, the reader responds, you adjust. Draft first for yourself to discover what you think; then plan the reader draft as a sequence of functions anticipating the reader's next move. Templates fail because readers care about relevance, not structure.
- **Named concepts**
  - **The expert's curse / Dunning-Kruger effect of writing**: the more you know, the harder writing feels, because you know your gaps and imagine a respected reader; amateurs are confident because they do not know enough to be scared. Feeling the pressure is a good sign that you are thinking about readers.
  - **Expert writing**: using writing to generate value by thinking thoughts not thought before, as opposed to "pastiche AI writing" that reshuffles others' ideas. The blank page as a **laboratory**.
  - **Two-phase strategy: writer's draft and reader draft**. Getting stuck means you jumped to phase two too early.
  - **The school writing trap**: assignments exist so students can demonstrate understanding to a paid reader with a rubric. Real readers are "busy, skeptical and quick to leave". Distinction: **knowledge vs value**. Trust comes from value, not credentials.
  - **The template trap**: formulas such as "always start with a thesis" and "every paragraph needs a topic sentence" were designed for grading. Readers use a self-centred rubric: their own problems.
  - **Genre and functions (Starbucks example)**: a genre is a cooperative sequence of functions toward a shared goal (greeting, order, confirmation, purchase); surface wording varies, functions persist. "What's your favorite color?" is grammatical but serves no function, so it is bizarre. Same for function-less sentences in an essay (the Truman Show product-placement scene: language addressed to nobody).
  - **Decision tree / dance**: plan the piece as "I say this, they will probably respond with that, so I address it here".
- **Process**
  1. Start writing before you know where you are going: daily 20-minute timed brain dump. Variant of **morning pages** (*The Artist's Way*): write the question you want to answer at the top of the page and free-write 10–20 min with your "center of gravity" on it.
  2. Talk it out with voice-to-text; AI clean-up of the transcript is acceptable to get raw material.
  3. Embrace the mess. Do not even edit the first draft; it goes in the drawer. When it makes sense to you, start a new reader draft.
  4. For the reader draft: define what you are trying to do (in nonfiction, almost always persuade); ask who the reader is and where they are now; begin with the equivalent of the greeting function; map their likely responses, questions, reservations and counter-arguments; meet them where they are and walk step by step to the understanding you reached in free-writing.
- **Diagnostic questions for the reader draft**: Do they already accept the premise? Do they need warming up? Is the idea dear to them, or are they looking for a critique of it? Which counter-arguments must be addressed? To position an idea: what benefit does the reader gain, or what cost does it help them avoid?
- **Examples**: the meditation blogger after one retreat vs the paralysed neuroscientist; searching for how to fix a leaky sink and wading through backstory; painters and potters showing up without a plan and letting the image emerge (nonfiction writers need more of that artistic freedom in phase one).
- **Stance notes**: "Templates are for dummies." Says his position on topic sentences is about function, not rule: here he dismisses "every paragraph needs a topic sentence" as a grading formula, while in the Koe and no-AI videos he recommends an index sentence / value proposition at the top of each unit because that is where readers look. Against AI tools that ignore readers.

## Cross-video patterns in this batch

**1. The master principle: tools, not rules; readers, not templates.** Present in every video. Rules (Strunk and White, "never passive", "fifth-grade level", "avoid repetition", "avoid jargon", "topic sentence") are rejected; each device is justified by its effect on a specific reader. "There are no universal rules because there are no universal readers."

**2. The two-draft / two-mode process is the spine of his workflow, and it evolves.**
- Jan 2025: "door closed / door open"; non-editing; working with words vs working with ideas; editing is a separate later block.
- Aug 2025: hardened into the **writer's draft vs reader draft**, with the instruction to *completely rewrite in a new document* and not edit draft one at all. The earlier "edit later" becomes "rewrite from the reader's position". Timed free-writing (10–20 min), placeholders, dictation and "question at the top of the page" persist throughout.
- The Zappa "rip a solo", painter-in-studio, laboratory and kimchi metaphors all describe phase one.

**3. Writing generates knowledge.** "Writing is thinking" (Jan), "writing is the process through which you become an expert" (Jul), "expert writing" and the spaghetti mind (Aug). Corollaries: start from evidence/stories and let claims emerge; capture scraps and weekly teachable moments; everyone has expertise.

**4. Value = solving a reader's problem.** The identical "clear and useless is useless" passage appears in Feb and Aug. Tools: the problem statement (topic / question / benefit-or-consequence); practical vs conceptual problems; behaviour change as the definition of learning; costs and benefits made personal; the "so what" test; value-coded words. The reader-facing structure is always a **bridge / path / GPS / guided tour / rails** from point A to point B (ToC as bridge; sentences as tiles).

**5. Writing is social.** Conversation-at-the-table parable (Feb) -> concession + destabilizing condition (Aug) -> two-player game and genre functions (Aug). In-group code words (Feb) -> "secret language" and value-word columns (Aug) -> bending references to the audience (Jul). Persona on the page and partner-not-preacher tone (Jul).

**6. Sentence-level mechanics form one coherent system built on position.**
- Subject position = spotlight / actor on stage; keep subjects consistent (2–3 characters per scene); characters as subjects, actions as verbs; avoid zombie nouns and dummy subjects; avoid "you" as habitual subject.
- Subject and verb early (locomotive effect); short introductions; verb next to object.
- Old information first, new information last; the stress position at the end carries emphasis; passive voice is the tool for achieving both.
- The same pattern scales fractally: index sentence -> discussion; uneven U; value proposition at the top of each unit.

**7. Persuasion over neutrality, with a tension about hedging.** For positioning and "new authority" books: bold, no hedging, pick a villain, ethos and pathos. For page persona and claim strength: use qualifiers, be confident but not arrogant, concede before disagreeing. He resolves it by audience and genre: hedging norms are cultural (academia vs elsewhere), and every claim needs evidence and warrant the reader accepts. Similarly, "objective and nuanced" is a school habit to unlearn, yet anticipating all three standard objections is mandatory.

**8. Apparent contradictions / evolution to flag for the system builder.**
- Topic sentences: dismissed as a grading template (Aug 14) but functionally endorsed as index sentences / value at paragraph start (Jul 22, Aug 8). Reconcile as: put the payoff where readers look, because of reading patterns, not because of a rule.
- "You": recommended in private drafts for talking to yourself (Jan); discouraged as a habitual grammatical subject in published prose (Jul); personal pronouns otherwise welcome (Aug).
- Pantsing: encouraged for phase one; rejected for assembling a book.
- AI: consistently the channel's "villain" for drafting, research and grammar correction; minor allowance for cleaning dictated transcripts.
- Channel focus shifts from general and literary craft (rhetorical figures, dialogue, fiction examples; Jan–Mar) to nonfiction experts writing authority-building books (from May, explicit by Jul), and he openly calls his early advice too generic.
- Sequence for books: goal -> necessary reader -> reader's pain -> promise (Vow) -> ToC as bridge with chapter promises -> filter content; he advises not starting to write the book until this blueprint exists, while still free-writing daily to develop ideas.

**9. Imitation as training.** "Steal style" (Feb) and "copy work" (May) are the same exercise: memorise and reproduce admired passages from memory, then pour your own ideas into those structures; plus mining 4–5 niche exemplars for vocabulary (Aug).

**10. Restraint and trust in the reader (literary strand).** Author-splaining, ellipsis, understating the serious, window-pane prose: step back so the reader steps forward. This coexists with the nonfiction strand's demand to make value and links explicit; the dividing line is genre and reader purpose (entertainment/meaning-making vs problem-solving).
