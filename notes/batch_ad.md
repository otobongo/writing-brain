## How Modern Schools Make Terrible Writers (Deliberately) (2026-01-29, https://youtu.be/OHfMQeZqo60)

- **Core thesis**: School writing is optimised for a paid, captive reader (the teacher) and for easy grading. Real readers read only when they perceive value, and value comes from their problems. Five school habits must be replaced with reader- and problem-centred practices.
- **Named concepts / frameworks**
  - **The martini glass** (school essay model): open wide with background/definitions/sweeping generalisations, narrow to a thesis, develop in body, widen again in the conclusion. "Great for teachers, terrible for readers." Two faults: (1) imposes an arbitrary form top-down, when the shape should emerge bottom-up from the needs of reader, writer and text; (2) it is built on continuity rather than problems.
  - **Language of continuity vs. language of instability**: continuity words = and, furthermore, also (signal stasis). Instability words = but, however, by contrast (signal a problem, hence value). Readers scan for the latter.
  - **Reading as an exchange of value**: reader trades attention for value; teacher's value comes from salary ("teachers are hostage"), real readers' value comes from their own problems.
  - **Expert writing = changing the reader's ideas, not self-expression**. Move sequence: start with what the reader thinks ("sit on the same side of the table") -> state the problem (what is wrong/incomplete in that belief) -> state the consequence (cost they pay or benefit they miss; cost may be practical/real-world pain, or conceptual: "you think X, and that stops you answering a bigger question Y").
  - **Two functions / two phases of writing**: Phase 1 writer-focused — writing as an extension of thinking; write about what interests you, scratch your own itch. Phase 2 reader-focused — write a completely NEW draft organised around the discovered idea, putting ideas in the structural locations where readers expect them.
  - **Writing rules vs. writing tools**: "There are no universal writing rules because there are no universal readers." Grammar is a toolset for adapting register to a specific reading community.
  - **Language as in-group signal**: language both communicates and signals social membership; outsider signals distract from the message.
  - **The GAP model** (academic "although much has been said about X, there is a lacuna about Y") — fails because new is not the same as valuable.
  - **Knowledge as puzzle (finite) vs. knowledge as model (infinite)**: in the puzzle view a missing piece is automatically valuable; in the model view a community holds a model used to explain and predict, and new knowledge is valuable only if it fixes an anomaly or improves prediction — i.e. forces an update of a belief the community holds.
  - **Interference**: the language patterns the writer used to think clash with the patterns readers bring to learn. Writer uses the text to think about the world; reader uses it to learn about the world. Value ends up buried at the end of the thinking draft.
- **Process / workflow**
  1. Begin with a problem you do not understand; write iteratively and messily until there is a "click"/epiphany (you produce, not find, the idea).
  2. Do not line-edit that draft; start fresh.
  3. Study prominent articles in your field/genre "like an anthropologist"; reproduce those readers' language patterns (not a template).
  4. Put value up top; place key information where readers expect it.
- **Rules of thumb / checklist (his closing five principles)**
  1. Lead with problems, not background; create instability readers recognise as costly.
  2. Locate problems within a specific reading community; the text should say "here is a problem stopping you from getting X, and here is how my idea solves it."
  3. An idea is only valuable if readers perceive the problem it solves; value does not come from style or credentials.
  4. Focus on function, not form: "What does this piece need to do for my specific reader to reach their goal?"
  5. Expert writing changes how readers think; it is not transmission of what you know.
  - Instead of "nobody has studied this," identify errors in how readers currently think, the frameworks they use, the cost of those, and offer your idea as the solution.
- **Examples**: North vs. South Korean speech (grammatically fine but marks outsider status; he jokingly catches himself opening with background). Researcher seeking a grant to study the Cormac McCarthy archives (Wittliff) whose only value claim was "nobody has explored them" = gap model. School rules cited: don't start with "but", no sentence-final prepositions, no "hopefully", no split infinitives. Comma vs. semicolon / en vs. em dash: "it doesn't matter." Journalism and scene analysis as cases where opening specific beats general-to-specific.
- **Stance notes**: Against martini glass, self-expression as purpose, universal rules, gap model, think-first-write-later. Admits he taught some of these himself. Against gurus' "write about whatever interests you, readers be damned" — valid only for phase 1. "Since AI, readers are drowning in information."

## Watch this if you want to IMPROVE the way you write (2026-02-01, https://youtu.be/LAmzkEyt60E)

- **Core thesis**: Writing becomes unclear when writers vary sentence openings to avoid repetition. Clarity comes from beginning sentences with a small, consistent set of old, familiar, concrete characters and ending with new, complex information.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Movie-poster test**: treat the passage as a movie; who goes on the poster? Readers assume a sentence tells the story of whoever/whatever appears first.
  - **Strategic repetition**: deliberately repeat key characters/concepts at sentence openings; this reads as coherent, not repetitive.
  - **Start of the sentence** = first seven or eight words before the verb (subject plus any opening phrases). Shared concrete characters there give readers a sense of unity / global structure.
  - **Old-to-new information flow**: begin with familiar, concrete, simple; end with new, abstract, complex. End a sentence with the item the next sentence picks up ("leans forward").
  - **Passive voice as a tool** for moving elements into the right position.
  - **Empty verbs** ("resulted in", "occurred"): signal that the real action is buried in a noun (nominalisation). Fix by asking **"who does what?"** and restoring character to subject, action to verb.
  - **Noun phrases as suitcases**: pack a sprawl of prepositional phrases into one noun phrase ("by many tsars before dying" -> "before the death of many tsars, including Peter").
  - **"Since" vs. "because"**: same logic, but "since" marks its clause as linking back to something known, so it lets the old information lead.
  - **Hyponym counts as repetition**: "male primogeniture" refers to the same concept as "law of succession", so it works as a familiar topic.
  - **Signposts for macro-structure**: "for example", "consequently" — use sparingly, only to show how sentences hang together (e.g. flagging that a stretch "double-clicks" into an example).
  - **Non-finite -ing reduction**: turn an effect clause into "-ing" ("making achievement by merit the basis of succession") to subordinate the effect to its cause.
- **Process (diagnostic revision routine)**
  1. Underline the first 7–8 words of every sentence/clause up to the verb.
  2. Ask: from those alone could you predict what the passage is about? Are they abstract? Can you draw them?
  3. Identify the actual cast of characters.
  4. Move characters to clause openings (use passive if needed).
  5. Look ahead to the next sentence; end the current one on what comes next.
  6. Order clauses cause then effect, matching real sequence.
  7. Replace empty verbs; find the hidden action.
  8. Compress strings of prepositional phrases; fix dangling modifiers by placing members next to their category.
  9. Add a signpost where the macro-structure shifts.
- **Examples**: "Mary smashed John in the face with a pie" in four versions, each telling a different story. Full rewrite of a Romanov succession passage (originally openings like "some sort of palace revolt", "achievement by merit", "this"). Revised: reigns "were plagued by" revolt; "Peter terminated the principle of heredity, making…"; "Consequently, successors were not appointed before the death of many tsars"; "For example, Tsarina Anna appointed Ivan VI…, but she was defeated by Peter's daughter Elizabeth, who ascended…"; "Since succession did not depend on authority, the boyars disputed…"; "…when the law of succession was codified by Paul I. But he was strangled by conspirators…". Watermelon/fruit to explain hyponym.
- **Stance notes**: Against "vary your sentence openings" and "never use the passive". Generic reader feedback ("unclear", "dense", "badly organised") describes feelings, not what to do. Transitional phrases are overused. Repeatedly refuses to argue about grammar correctness — the criterion is reader effect.

## Writing Online Is Hard, Until Experts Do This (2026-02-08, https://youtu.be/j1JRSan9CYg)

- **Core thesis**: "Bees make honey" is not inherently better than "Honey is made by bees." The better sentence is the one whose topic position holds what this particular reader cares about. Rule-based advice (shorter is better, avoid passives) produces valueless writing and, worse, stops you thinking about readers.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Topic position**: the start of the sentence; readers move left to right and take what comes first as what the sentence is about.
  - **Translation cost**: a reader who cares about honey but gets a sentence about bees must translate it; that extra work makes the sentence feel less clear, less persuasive and less valuable.
  - **"Writing isn't a thing, it's a function"**: meaning is not in the text like oxygen in water; it arises from what readers do with it.
  - **"There are no writing rules. There are only writing tools."**
  - **The two dangers of rules**: (1) a specific failure — rule-following without value; (2) the habit of thinking in rules, which makes all readers look identical and makes writers suspicious of thinking about readers at all. Rules give an "illusion of certainty" and can be automated by AI.
  - **Reader-question test** (for choosing subjects): treat your sentence as the answer to a question; phrase the question in the reader's voice; whatever is the subject of their question should be the subject of your sentence.
- **Process**: In revision (not the first draft) for each key sentence: guess what the reader cares about -> run the reader-question test -> put that item in the topic position, using passive or a longer sentence if needed.
- **Rules of thumb**: B2B: "don't sell features, sell benefits" applied at sentence level — clients care about themselves, so make the client/business the subject. Even when citing another expert, do not assume readers care about the person; they may care about what was offered or whether it worked. Accept that you are guessing; uncertainty is normal.
- **Examples**: "This new feature permits businesses to…" -> "Businesses are permitted to…". Finance newsletter (he mentions Ben Thompson): "Seasonal volatility of intermediated consumption directly affects price-dividend ratios" — investors care about the ratios, so lead with them. Eric Ries example about Matt Mullenweg offering Automattic staff six months' salary to resign: management readers ask whether loyalty can be tested by paying people to leave -> "Commitment to the company's direction was tested by…"; workforce readers -> "Six months of salary was offered by…"; others might ask "Did his offer work?"
- **Stance notes**: Against "writing mavens" who preach rules they don't follow themselves (they have an internal "alarm bell"; domain experts lack it). He will not meddle if rules work for your readers, but objects to imposing them on others. None of this applies during the first draft (first function of writing).

## Afraid of the EM dash? (2026-02-15, https://youtu.be/FcUtF0UdPsg)

- **Core thesis**: Avoiding the em dash for fear of AI accusations swaps a visible risk for a worse invisible one (less clear prose). The em dash is a legitimate tool whose job is emphasis; use it sparingly and on purpose.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Visible vs. invisible risk** (headlight analogy): turning off your lights to avoid a ticket makes a crash likely.
  - **Syntax vs. semantics of punctuation**: syntax = the mechanics; semantics = what the mark means/does rhetorically.
  - **Syntax of paired em dashes**: marks the end and resumption of a statement; the inserted material is not bound by the grammar of the matrix clause — "injecting a new thought", which suits the free-associative rhythm of speech.
  - **Three legitimate use patterns** found in expert essays: (1) **exemplification** — inject examples without a new clause; (2) **aside comment** — a digression or the writer's opinion; (3) **mitigation** — a hedge that answers naysayers without derailing the main point.
  - **Semantics: the em dash is for emphasis** — it stresses the interrupting clause without bold or italics.
  - **Emphasis hierarchy for interruptions**: em dashes (loudest) > commas > parentheses (quietest, most unobtrusive).
  - **Definitions belong in a non-restrictive relative clause** (", which…"), because that puts the definition in a subordinate position.
- **Rules of thumb**: Reserve em dashes for the clauses you judge most important. Do not use them for definitions. Overuse drains force — same as "however": reserve "however" for major turns in the argument. Test punctuation by reading silently, because your voice supplies the emphasis when read aloud. Em dashes also remove comma splices and syntactic ambiguity (punctuation should give clues to the underlying structure).
- **Examples**: Sentences re-punctuated with dashes (penicillin 1928/DNA 1953 breakthroughs; Sarah opening the university envelope; creative momentum "like physical fitness in an athlete"; "Don't worry about polishing your draft — we'll handle that — just make sure…"). Maria Popova on the ship of Theseus (exemplification), Simon Sinek on adolescence/school (aside), Ryan Holiday on rights (mitigation). Weak definitional uses: Marc Andreessen ("accelerationism"), Gwern ("hikikomori"); Paul Graham and Andreessen named as indiscriminate users. He recommends Gwern's blog regardless.
- **Stance notes**: "No fan of AI writing" but refuses to "cede ground to AI witch hunts". Not laying down rules; describing patterns and effects. The em dash is only a symptom — the disease is the confident-but-hollow AI voice.

## The Top 1% of Experts Think on Paper—Here's How (2026-02-22, https://youtu.be/VkXMlrvq29o)

- **Core thesis**: Valuable expertise solves problems too complex to work out in your head, so experts write to think ("write first, understand later"), then rebuild the text from the reader's starting point. Outlines, templates and prompts produce bland, replaceable writing.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Writing is thinking / writing as an extension of thinking**.
  - **Reading is food, not fuel** (from subscriber Alexi): read when the writing "gets hungry" — when you hit a wall or a gap — ideally guided by a research question so it is not productive procrastination.
  - **Drafts as excavation sites / a lab**: you dig through dirt for treasure; you bring ideas into contact and watch for reactions; most are duds.
  - **Expert knowledge as tangled Christmas lights**, not a Lego set: connections only show up as you untangle.
  - **The reverse outline**: (1) write the draft as a timed brain dump; (2) go section by section and label what each part actually DOES — if it cannot be labelled it is fluff or a "Frankenstein's monster of ideas"; (3) lay the labels out in order and look only at them; where the flow feels off, fix. Labels should correspond to the reader's questions and expectations; the result is a map from the reader's current understanding to the new one.
  - **Restaurant-directions analogy**: you cannot give a friend your own route; start from where they are.
  - **Interference**: the writer's thinking patterns (rambling exploration, heavy background, step-by-step working through confusion) collide with the reader's reading patterns. "Unclear / disorganised / I couldn't get it" describe this friction, not content.
  - **Two steps of expert writing = two different documents**: writing for the writer, then writing for the reader. Revision is rebuilding the path from scratch.
  - **Code words / community-specific code words**: vocabulary that tells a reading community "this is your world" — "a secret handshake". Universal value words also exist: but, however, although (instability, "catnip for the curious brain").
  - **"Value is in the eye of the reader"**; half of expertise is knowing the subject, half is knowing the reader.
- **Process / habits**
  1. Every morning open a new fleeting note and brain dump. If you have a problem, write it as a question at the top, set a timer, "let yourself dream while writing."
  2. Do not wait to feel ready; the writing makes you ready. "Write badly enough to find the gold."
  3. Reverse outline (five minutes after finishing a draft).
  4. Write the reader document from scratch.
  5. **Code-word hunt** before publishing: find the artefacts the community shares (a journal, a widely shared article), scan like an anthropologist for recurring in-group terms, and build your value statements from them.
- **Examples**: Alexi solved his own read-vs-write problem while typing the question. School outlines that students secretly wrote after the essay — "this bad habit is the perfect strategy" at expert level. Joan Didion: writes "to find out what I'm thinking." W. Brian Arthur's 1996 HBR article "Increasing Returns and the New World of Business", revised over four days with Cormac McCarthy: instead of "early market entry produces compounding benefits", "It pays to hit the market first" — "it pays" hits HBR readers' values; other code words: strategy, competitive advantage, market lock-in.
- **Stance notes**: Against outlines-first, templates, better prompts, waiting for clarity. "If you know exactly what you're going to say before you write, you are not writing valuably" — that is what AI can replace. You cannot line-edit a thinking draft into a reader draft. Writing for "everybody" never works.

## The 5 Writing Skills A.I. Will Never Replace (2026-04-01, https://youtu.be/JGSA6Wbbaxo)

- **Core thesis**: Staying irreplaceable is not about style tips (avoid "delve", avoid em dashes). It rests on five human capacities that come from being alive, having a body and having something at stake — "that's your moat."
- **Named concepts (the five skills)**
  1. **Voice as an emergent phenomenon**: voice is a complex adaptive system (ant colony, stock market — simple agents, no central controller, macro pattern). It cannot be reduced to grammar and word choice; it emerges from those elements interacting with the writer's **lived experience** ("vibes"). Voice = blend of experience, expertise and perspective. AI is the average of all voices: "smooth, homogeneous, hygienic… slop."
  2. **Embodied metaphor**: AI metaphors are formally correct but "a few degrees off" because AI is disembodied. Lakoff & Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By*: metaphors are how we conceive the world (argument as war, time running out). Ocean Vuong: metaphor as a portal linking unfamiliar to familiar, where the familiar must modify or amplify understanding of the unfamiliar.
  3. **Deliberate rule-breaking**: learn the rules first (chef/techniques, musician/scales), then break them for a specific effect. AI is "teacher's pet", a parrot, and will smooth over the strangeness.
  4. **Stakes**: real writing happens when people you respect will read it and your reputation is on the line. AI has no consequences, no embarrassment, no growth ("an incompetent intern").
  5. **Whimsy / creative unpredictability**: an approach, not a trait — follow a thread into the unknown. Writing as a **space of play**; Martha Nussbaum's typewriter as a **transitional object** (security enables play). "Stop performing and start discovering."
- **Practices**: Trust the metaphors that occur to you spontaneously (they are grounded in the body, like your readers'). Publish under your own name work you would not be embarrassed by. Revise multiple times before anyone sees it. Treat drafting as "let's see where this goes", not "I must finish this". AI is acceptable as a surprise-generating co-pilot for divergent thinking, never as the writer.
- **Examples**: "As close as an orange to its navel" (mother/daughter; he thinks Mahfouz); NYC sanitation workers' "disco rice" for maggots. Final line of McCarthy's *Suttree* — "Fly them": an ungrammatical verb choice that gives the line its power. Shakespeare wrestling with the sonnet's constraints. His undergraduate critical-theory seminar (Derrida, Lacan, Lyotard): each week one student wrote a two-page précis summarising and arguing against the reading, in front of respected peers. Bezos's memo culture (he says five pages; silent reading at the start of meetings). David Foster Wallace requiring two or three revisions before submission. GPT-2-era absurd continuations as the good kind of AI surprise; "AI writing peaked" then. David Deutsch on creativity.
- **Stance notes**: Against custom instructions designed to make AI text pass as yours — "why would you want to trick your readers?" Writing without AI is not a handicap; it is a different approach. Admits he cannot lose himself in play as fully as he would like.

## Expert Writing Patterns that Keep Readers Hooked (2026-04-05, https://youtu.be/n1CrHXxcfbA)

- **Core thesis**: The patterns that organise words matter more than the words. Two principles fix most tangled prose: characters in subjects with actions in verbs; familiar, consistent topics at the start with new, heavy information at the end.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Fixed vs. variable elements**. Fixed (syntax level): adverbial/opening – subject – verb – object – adverbial. Fixed (organisation level): **topic** (from the start of the sentence, including opening adverbials, to the end of the subject) and **comment** (the verb and everything after). Variable: **characters and actions** (can sit anywhere: "Bees make honey" / "Honey is made by bees" / "The making of honey is carried out by bees") and **old vs. new information**.
  - **Principle 1**: concrete characters in the subject; important actions in the verb.
  - **Principle 2**: consistent, familiar, backward-linking context in the topic; new, complex, emphasis-worthy information in the comment.
  - **Index position**: the end (comment) of the opening sentence, which sets up what the rest of the passage will be about; later topics should "index" / link back up to it.
  - **Topic strings**: the chain of topics across sentences; when they keep pointing to the same one or two things (here Truman and the domestic political issues) the passage coheres.
  - **Sentence as runway**: start with simple, familiar material so readers can take off into complexity.
  - **Overloaded subject / lopsided front-loaded sentence**: a long subject before an empty verb is a red flag; move the detail to the end.
  - **Coordination vs. subordination**: "so"/"and" (FANBOYS) give clauses equal weight. If one clause interprets, defines or gives the effect of the other, use a non-restrictive relative clause (", which…") and optionally reduce it to a non-finite -ing ("…, making the Cold War an issue").
  - **Prefer transitive verbs**: only nature is truly intransitive (sun rises, leaves fall); the Cold War did not "become" an issue — something made it one.
  - Old-vs-new can override characters-first: he moves "Truman's foreign policy" back to the subject because it is old information.
- **Process**
  1. Mark characters (green), actions (red), grammar words (grey).
  2. Draw reference lines between sentences; crossing, tangled lines indicate the problem.
  3. Sentence 1: recurring character in the subject; the controlling new information at the end (index position).
  4. Rework each sentence so the topic links back; pick a new verb when the subject changes ("support was shrinking" -> "was losing support from…").
  5. Number parallel items (first, second, third) so each ties to the index position — signposts are justified when information is heavy.
  6. Demote a minor cause with "since".
  7. Turn nominalised conclusions into verbs ("Truman's conclusion… was an inevitable one" -> "Truman inevitably concluded that…").
  8. Move attributions into an opening adverbial ("According to some historians, Truman should have risked…").
  9. Close by echoing sentence one. Compare before/after topic strings.
- **Examples**: Full rewrite of a passage on Truman and the Oppenheimer committee's hydrogen-bomb recommendation. Also makes "China and Russia" parallel with "Sino-Soviet".
- **Stance notes**: Dislikes signposts "all over the place" but uses them for complex material. Invites alternative rewrites — several solutions exist. Vocabulary and grammar are not the problem; organisation is.

## Why You Should Write an Essay (Even If No One Reads It) (2026-04-09, https://youtu.be/4nIo92ALrd0)

- **Core thesis**: Replace "write well" with "write valuably" — answering questions and solving problems, which requires generating new knowledge through writing. The durable product of writing is "an articulate mind", and articulacy is power. But the "write for yourself, ignore readers" meme is wrong for non-fiction: both phases are required.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Write valuably**: readers trade attention for value, not for grammar or word choice.
  - **Writing in reverse** (school habit): start with the thesis and then hunt for evidence, which turns evidence into "decoration".
  - **Topic vs. question**: a topic (a noun) gives no way of selecting; "a question holds the conclusion in suspension." **"The question is your filter"** for evidence.
  - **The inquiry method** (a counterpart to the scientific method for the world of values): (1) **Question**; (2) **Evidence** — what anyone can see by looking, raw data with no values baked in; (3) **Reasoning** — values/explanations in your own thinking; (4) **Claim** — your original idea. Formula: **evidence + reasoning = claim**. A critic must dispute either your description of the evidence or your reasoning.
  - **Style as a by-product / style as integrity**: style is not reducible to mechanics; it emerges from the writer's struggle with their inquiry. It is an integration between what you think and what you say. It cannot be planned.
  - **The text as a third party** that goes out to do work: persuade customers, overturn an assumption, earn status with people you respect.
  - **Power / authority** = the ability to effect change and raise the likelihood that others comply with your requests; "no meaningful difference between being authoritative and being articulate."
  - **Two phases, two failure modes**: skip phase 2 -> "writing for Substack" (everyone writes, nobody reads). Skip phase 1 -> **audience capture**: nothing new, just giving readers the opinions they want. Thinking about readers too early causes writer's block and corrupts your thinking ("social monkeys"; readers have "crept into the writer's room").
  - **Writing shapes character**: arguments you write become part of your cognitive structure -> perception -> behaviour -> character -> destiny.
- **Process (as he summarises it)**
  1. Start with a question.
  2. Say aloud: "Hmm, I don't know. What evidence would I need to answer this?"
  3. Read widely about the question.
  4. As relevant evidence appears, explain it to yourself in writing.
  5. Synthesise into a framework that makes sense to you.
  6. Only then refine style at sentence, paragraph and word level, and (phase 2) position the idea as the solution to a problem readers care about.
- **Exercises**: Classroom trap — asks students to pick sides on online vs. offline education; those who move are "busted", because you cannot have an answer before writing about it. Students write the essay's question and say the "I don't know" line out loud.
- **Examples**: Hemingway, McCarthy, David Foster Wallace as writers whose style cannot be explained by mechanics. Writing a university paper to flatter the professor as a form of audience capture.
- **Stance notes**: Admits the title is clickbait aimed at the "write even if nobody reads it" meme, which he calls a bad export from fiction. Claims gurus who say "ignore readers" are themselves audience-captured. Notes fast/divergent thinkers feel reined in by the method. Changing your mind later is fine; the value was never the specific claim.

## The writing advice that's keeping smart people small (2026-04-23, https://youtu.be/ED_LXx3Hzak)

- **Core thesis**: Experts do not need to dumb ideas down. Good science/idea writing uses four storytelling principles, shown through Siddhartha Mukherjee's New Yorker essay on epigenetics (his twin mother and aunt).
- **Named concepts (four moves)**
  1. **Drive the piece with a single question, not a topic**. "A topic gives you a pile of things to say, but a question gives you a reason to say them." A question licenses material that would otherwise be digression (Waddington's wartime politics, the Dutch hunger winter, ant experiments), because each helps answer it. Consequence: a question forces **judgment calls** — the author must step forward. Stories have a "moral universe"; "a story without judgment is not a story" — it is Wikipedia.
  2. **Foreground and background** (the fix for the exposition problem). Foreground = what you personally saw: who you spoke to, the room, props, faces, details revealing character — always relevant to the question. Background = research, papers, statistics, history, definitions. **Three sources of material: research, interviews, reporting.** In the AI age research is "essentially free" and is the least valuable part; "you in the room" is the value.
  3. **Structure in time**. The writer has a **god's-eye view** (looking down at a map); the reader walks the path one sentence at a time. Time is the dimension in which you can **set up payoffs**. Mark every time shift with a plain signal ("By the time I was born in 1970", "One afternoon in April", "In the fall of 2003") — dull phrases "doing enormous work".
  4. **Assume the reader does not care about what you care about**. Do not preach to the converted with words treated as self-evidently good (sustainability, diversity, progress). Find what these readers already care about and **build a bridge/door** from there; do not debate them. Carry vocabulary from the world they care about into the dry material ("hyperlinking"). Turn labels into characters.
- **Rules of thumb / exercises**
  - Convert a topic to a question: "climate policy" -> "why did this town vote against solar panels it had already paid for?"
  - If you lack credentials for a judgment, interview someone credible — but do not skip the judgment.
  - Never stop the narrative to define a term; deliver it through a scene and an image.
  - Plant an image early and reuse it late.
  - Interview people as humans with motives: "How did you end up spending your life on this?" "What was your most important experience?" People in the story include patients, homeowners, students, neighbours, not only experts.
  - "If your reader can see people, they'll follow you anywhere."
- **Examples**: The scientist (Allis) crosses his office to a model; the histone described as a python coiled around a skewer of marshmallows. Time jumps: Delhi 1942, the 1970s, the present-day lab, the 1940s (Waddington), 1944, 2003. Payoffs: freckles planted twice in the opening (telling the twins apart) return as "freckles" on the genome; "the first derivative of identity" lands only after the whole journey. The ant colony described with the word "caste", importing the Indian family story. Allis's fraternal twin sister; he retook a biochemistry class after an A-minus because he felt he had not mastered it. Exposition crammed into dialogue in biopics (The Social Network, Bohemian Rhapsody).
- **Stance notes**: Against "dumb it down", against explain/define/teach in the foreground — says he learned this the hard way on YouTube and is embarrassed by early videos. Narrative non-fiction is not objective reporting.

## I learned a system for writing effortlessly (2026-04-26, https://youtu.be/_ribgj7VIGc)

- **Core thesis**: A Zettelkasten-based Obsidian vault should be a "windmill", not a "filing cabinet": it turns inputs from culture and life into concentrated ideas you can write about on demand. AI is used only as a read-only navigation layer over a knowledge graph you built yourself.
- **Named concepts / components**
  - **Purpose of a system is what it does**; outputs must be worth more than inputs.
  - **Two zones**: outside the vault (life and culture flowing through you) and inside the vault.
  - **Two input streams**: **Culture** (books, essays, podcasts, articles) -> **Sources folder**, saved raw and unanalysed (Obsidian Web Clipper). **Life** (conversations, dreams, thoughts, experiences) -> **fleeting notes** (e.g. voice memo -> AI-cleaned transcript).
  - **Dream log**: recommended; the unconscious is the source of creativity and of what gets called unique style.
  - **Daily note** (template): auto-index of notes created/edited that day, plus a to-do of two or three high-leverage activities with links. Drawn from the **weekly note/plan**. Produces a calendar history of your thinking.
  - **Three note types, each a refinement of the last**: **Fleeting note** = scrap of paper; frictionless, ugly, auto-titled with a timestamp ID so you never have to think of a title. **Literature note** = marginalia; a record of your dialogue with a text (quote + your response), not highlights, not a summary or review. **Permanent note** = one atomic, self-contained idea in your own words, with a link to its source and a link to a note already in the system. May be an idea you doubt or a question.
  - **Alphanumeric IDs** (e.g. 3.1a2): not a hierarchy, just a guarantee that **no note is orphaned**; the structure is a **rhizome**; knowledge as a semantic tree (Musk).
  - **Active-reading question**: "Where are things the same and where are they different?"
  - **Human layer vs. AI layer**: agents can read but never rewrite or delete human notes. Principle: AI works ON my thinking rather than doing it — no summarising sources, extracting main ideas, or proposing links in the human notes.
  - **Three weekly AI agents**: (1) **Keyword index** — like a book's back index for the permanent notes; surfaces themes and higher-order links; a source of serendipity. (2) **Wiki agent** — **hub notes** (maps of notes on a topic under headings) and **structure notes** (Wikipedia-style narrative synthesis built only from permanent notes); both flag gaps, unanswered questions, unfollowed links. (3) **Weekly review** — Sunday morning, reads the whole vault including manuscripts; reports tasks done, dream patterns, fleeting-note themes, key ideas, progress against quarterly goals, time left in the quarter, suggested focus.
  - **Q&A function**: ask the vault "have I thought about this before?"; it surfaces forgotten notes and tells you which sources/fleeting notes to process next. Outputs live in a separate folder and are excluded from the wiki agents so they do not "muddy the waters".
- **Workflow**: capture -> daily note -> fleeting note on whatever puzzles you -> recall or find a relevant source -> literature note alongside it -> promote the live ideas to permanent notes (own words, source link, link to an existing note) -> patterns accumulate -> weekly AI review -> weekly plan -> daily to-dos -> manuscripts.
- **Examples**: A Dan Koe tweet ("if you don't write, you can't think") struck him as going too far -> fleeting note -> recalled McCarthy's essay "The Kekulé Problem" (the unconscious solves problems without language; McCarthy's joke inviting readers to write and explain how they use language to think) -> literature note -> permanent note "we don't think with language", linked to the question note "is language used to think?". A Lacanian analyst's "truth of desire" matched a David Deutsch tweet on following present desire and using long-term goals as criticism. A conversation on South Korea's fertility rate led to an ant-colony/emergence idea, which Q&A connected to old notes.
- **Stance notes**: Earlier Zettelkasten attempts "always felt like a filing cabinet"; adding AI changed that — a shift from his reluctance. Rejects Readwise-style highlight imports ("quotes on their own are not valuable"). Still holds that AI should not write or think for you. Notably he is now questioning his own "writing is thinking" slogan.

## Cross-video patterns in this batch

- **The spine: two functions / two phases of writing.** It appears in six of ten videos. Phase 1 serves the writer: a question, messy drafting, inquiry, play, fleeting notes. Phase 2 serves the reader: a new document that starts from the reader's beliefs and problems, places value up top, and uses their code words. The two are "in direct conflict", so they must be separated in time. The 04-09 video gives each skipped phase its own failure mode (unread Substack vs. audience capture) and adds that considering readers too early corrupts thought.
- **Interference** is the linking concept (01-29, 02-22): "unclear" is feedback about friction between thinking patterns and reading patterns. This is why he says you cannot line-edit a thinking draft, and why the **reverse outline** is the practical bridge between the phases.
- **Value is defined by the reader.** Attention is exchanged for value; value comes from a problem carrying a cost for a specific community; it is signalled by the language of instability (but, however, although) and by community code words. "New" does not equal "valuable" (gap model; knowledge as model rather than puzzle). The anthropologist stance recurs: study the texts your readers share.
- **Question over topic/thesis** (02-22, 04-09, 04-23, 04-26): the question heads the morning brain dump, filters evidence in the inquiry method, licenses digressions in narrative non-fiction, and exists as a permanent note in the vault. It also directs reading ("read when the writing gets hungry").
- **One sentence-level doctrine in three versions** (02-01, 02-08, 04-05). Readers treat what comes first as what the sentence is about. So: characters as subjects, actions as verbs; old/familiar/simple first, new/complex last; consistent topic strings; end a sentence on what the next one picks up. Passive voice, longer sentences, "since", ", which" clauses, -ing reductions and noun-phrase "suitcases" are tools for achieving this. 02-08 adds the value angle: choose the topic by what this reader cares about (reader-question test). 04-05 adds terminology (topic/comment, index position, runway) and shows old/new overriding characters-first. All of this is explicitly revision-stage work.
- **Tools, not rules**, stated almost word for word in 01-29 and 02-08 and applied in 02-15 (em dash) and 04-01 (break rules deliberately after learning them). He regularly declines grammar-correctness arguments; the test is the effect on a specific reader. Signposts, "however" and em dashes share one economy: use them rarely so they keep their force.
- **Position on AI develops.** January–February: AI produces slop and floods readers with information, and rules can be "automated" by it. 04-01: the human moat is voice, body, rule-breaking, stakes and whimsy; AI is tolerable as a surprise-generating co-pilot. 04-23: research is now "free", so first-hand reporting is the value. 04-26: he adopts AI after prior reluctance, but only as a read-only navigation layer with outputs quarantined. The constant: AI must not do the writing or the thinking.
- **Voice/style as emergent** (04-01, 04-09, 04-26): irreducible to mechanics; it emerges from lived experience, from struggle with an inquiry ("integrity"), and from the unconscious (dream log). It cannot be planned — consistent with treating sentence craft as functional rather than aesthetic.
- **Tensions and evolution**
  - "Writing is thinking" is asserted strongly in 02-22 and 04-09, then questioned in 04-26 via McCarthy's claim that problem-solving is not done in language. This is left as an open question in his own notes.
  - He attacks templates and outlines while offering principle lists and a fixed move sequence (belief -> problem -> cost -> solution). His reconciliation: these describe functions and reader expectations, not forms.
  - "Obsess over readers" vs. audience capture is resolved by sequencing the phases.
  - "Don't explain, define or teach" (04-23) sits beside his own teaching-heavy videos; he admits this. Likewise he notices himself opening with background in 01-29.
  - Signposts are "overused" yet used liberally in both rewrites; his condition is that they expose macro-structure or tie back to the index position.
  - 04-23 extends the system from argument-driven expert prose to narrative non-fiction (foreground/background, time structure, setups and payoffs). The reader-first premise stays the same: start from what the reader already cares about and build a bridge.
- **Recurring references**: Cormac McCarthy (archive grant, the Arthur HBR edit, *Suttree*, "The Kekulé Problem"), David Deutsch, ant colonies/emergence (voice in 04-01, fertility idea in 04-26, Mukherjee's ants in 04-23), David Foster Wallace, Hemingway, Didion; the bees/honey sentence pair (02-08, 04-05).
