## This type of essay turns normal experience into valuable writing (2025-08-26, https://youtu.be/DkigGUpCR1I)

- **Core thesis**: You do not need an interesting life to write creative non-fiction. Ordinary experience is ideal raw material provided you aim "beyond the facts" toward something universally true that gives readers value; otherwise you produce "glorified journal entries" / navel-gazing that nobody reads.
- **Named concepts / frameworks**
  - **The creative/non-creative x fiction/non-fiction axis (four quadrants)**:
    - Creative fiction (literary, avant-garde, sci-fi): goal is innovation; made-up stories that touch something universal.
    - Non-creative fiction (genre romance, detective, YA, 99-cent Kindle): reproduces familiar patterns.
    - Non-creative non-fiction (journalism, academic reports, memos, manuals): plain information transfer, factual and functional.
    - **Creative non-fiction** ("the sweet spot"): anchored in reality but, like creative fiction, aims beyond the facts at something deeper/true/universal that gives readers real value.
  - **Pointing the camera**: non-fiction is reporting facts, but the creativity lies in *which* features the writer selects and the concepts used to illustrate them. Selection of detail reveals the writer's preoccupation and carries the theme.
  - **Opening as "opening number"**: like the first number of a musical, the opening paragraphs introduce all characters and all main themes. Everything later is an expansion of the germ in the opening; "we don't get anything new" afterward.
  - **Opening image / opening metaphor**: one concrete scene from which the whole piece unfolds.
  - **Web of signification**: keep word choice inside one image-environment (e.g. "lured" by a paycheck, "on the hook" in an ocean essay). Build contrasting word families for opposing themes (stability words: relaxed, graceful, symphonic, glide vs. instability words: disorient, shaky, unsettled, fall apart).
  - **Artistic/poetic licence with purpose**: CNF is not journalism; you may contrive a phrase or detail (the credit officer's "weak stability") but only to serve a purpose (here, contrast with the theme of stability).
  - **Outside research couched in narrative**: quotes, definitions, research are brought in only a "few select times", each serving a function, and always inside the unfolding story (he watches birds -> goes home and reads -> "my books confirm what I noticed" -> back to the walk). Never "orphaned" facts; never break from the narrative to lecture like Wikipedia. Writer poses as learner, not expert.
  - **Section-function labelling**: label each section by its function (his labels: opening; weak stability; joy; [pelican neighbours]; taste of balance; evolutionary perspective/ritual; small achievements; habituation; novelty fading into intimacy; culminating day). "Every paragraph should have a single message" and these messages should cohere into a macro pattern.
  - **Two time scales**: the local (up/down, birth/death, stability/instability) and the zoomed-out (evolutionary "great and epic continuation"). Moving between particulars and the expansive view is what yields the transcendent point.
  - **Mirror device**: a secondary subject (pelicans) serves as a mirror against which the author fashions himself; although readers learn much about pelicans, the narrative is about the author's adaptation.
- **Process / workflow**
  1. Take a real, straightforward experience.
  2. Probe it more closely; ask what you keep noticing and why.
  3. Build an opening image containing all characters and themes.
  4. Develop in labelled functional sections; plant concepts early (functionslust, ritual, habituation, "lustrous") and call them back later.
  5. Bring in outside material sparingly, in-story.
  6. Arrive at a thesis stated late (here: we are animals; pretending to be better makes us worse) and a closing that reframes (beginnings as continuation).
  7. Classroom practice: each session the class reads one student's piece and everyone writes a response paper; they rehearse this on the model essay first.
- **Rules of thumb / diagnostics**
  - Ask of every quotation/research insert: why is it here, how does it develop the piece?
  - Zoom in (line level) then zoom out (whole units) when analysing.
  - Callbacks: return to the opening image (the relaxed girl on the board) near the end.
  - A change of setting can mark the climax (first time the story leaves the coast is the final section).
  - Use your writing not just to express yourself but to help readers understand the world differently and solve problems.
- **Examples**: David Gessner, "Learning to Surf" (new father, new job, moves to coastal North Carolina, learns to surf while watching pelicans). Concepts: functionslust (pleasure in what one does best); Thoreau on joy as the symptom of right conduct; early ornithologists calling pelicans grotesque (author takes it personally); Bible and King Lear's "pelican daughters"; Buddha-like calm; Dawkins' Blind Watchmaker alluded to for the puzzle-piece/evolution image. Crossover technique: surfers described as animals, pelicans as athletes.
- **Stance notes**: Against Substack-style experience writing that stays at the facts. Against the belief "I'm not a real writer / my life is boring".

## The novelist helping experts write well (2025-09-02, https://youtu.be/qPb2659z8jQ)

- **Core thesis**: Expertise is useless unless packaged into clear writing. W. Brian Arthur's HBR article "Increasing Returns and the New World of Business" (1996), reworked over four days with Cormac McCarthy, models how novelist's principles adapt to non-fiction: minimalism, problem construction, consistent subjects, concreteness, a single theme.
- **Named concepts / frameworks**
  - **Punctuation X-ray / thumbprint**: strip all words, view only punctuation. Little punctuation between periods = simply built sentences. McCarthy's tip: **use minimalism to achieve clarity**; for every mark, word, sentence, paragraph, section ask "do you need that?"
  - **Good writing is a function of the reader's experience**: the reader decides what good writing is; easier processing = better writing.
  - **Problem construction — five pieces**: (1) **status quo** (what we think), (2) **concession** ("roughly valid" for its day), (3) **destabilizing condition** (signalled by "but"), (4) **consequence**, (5) **solution / new idea**. Your idea must be positioned as the solution to a problem.
  - **Conceptual problem vs practical problem**: a conceptual problem tells readers their understanding is wrong or incomplete; conceptual problems are "upstream" of practical ones (new model -> new business practices).
  - **Value words**: words that signal value/instability to readers. Universal: "but", "mistake", "assumption", "still", "passed down", "handful", "more than a century ago". **Community-specific value words**: in-group code words ("strategy", "it pays" for HBR readers).
  - **Topic string**: the same entity (or synonym) held in subject position across sentences (our understanding -> it is an understanding -> the theory -> it still thrives). Camera metaphor: paragraph = scene, sentence = shot, writer = director choosing what goes in subject position. Second paragraph: every subject is "increasing returns".
  - **Concrete language — "something the reader could stub their toe on"**: every abstraction grounded in something material (bulk processing -> "smokestack economy"; a school of thought -> Alfred Marshall as a character; two paradigms -> two "worlds"/"regions"). Rationale: the visual brain is older than the language brain. Provides a "set design".
  - **Thread and needlework (McCarthy)**: decide the theme and two or three points every reader should remember; these are the thread; words/sentences/paragraphs are the needlework. Focus on the pattern.
  - **Paragraph = unit of one idea / one message** ("each paragraph has one job"). You should be able to label each paragraph's function and the list of labels should itself read coherently. One-sentence paragraphs are legitimate, typically as transitions.
  - **Concepts as chess pieces / chunking**: pose a question -> tell a familiar story (Apple vs DOS/IBM) -> sum up key observations one sentence each -> package each into a named concept (market instability, multiple potential outcomes, unpredictability, lock-in, possible predominance of an inferior product) -> redeploy those pieces through the rest of the piece. Recurrence gives coherence.
  - **Intuition pump**: better than an example; puts the reader in the detective position to think the point through themselves (cockpit vs cabin crew for hierarchy; factory vs casino gambling where you also choose which game to play).
  - **Inviting tone / same side of the table**: question-led paragraphs, "let us look", "notice several things", personal asides ("they surprised me"). Positions reader beside writer looking at the world; avoids putting readers on the back foot in what is really persuasion.
  - **Introduction mirrors structure**: the clauses of the opening paragraph's final sentence open up into the article's sections.
- **Process**: (implicit) choose theme + 2-3 points; build problem opening; hold subjects constant; ground abstractions; tell story -> analyse -> package concepts -> reuse; cut ruthlessly.
- **Rules of thumb / exercises**
  - **Anthropologist exercise**: pick 4-5 pieces popular among your readers and mine them for value words / code words.
  - Choose examples your specific readers already know.
  - Pre-empt "this isn't new" objections (Hicks warned of this, but had it wrong) — this is value language.
  - Do not come "off the top ropes" calling the old idea trash; concede first.
- **Examples**: Arthur/McCarthy article; "passed down" evokes hand-me-down clothes; T. S. Eliot's Prufrock opening as analogue to "let us".
- **Stance notes**: Against putting every sentence on a new line (Substack/newsletter habit): be parsimonious, since each break signals a new idea. "Never say I/you" lore has reasons but here personal pronouns serve the purpose. Writing should be fun and take the reader on a journey. Teaser: the writing process is "two processes, not one".

## give me 43 min, I'll DELETE your writer's block (2025-09-08, https://youtu.be/KSVv5SW2YU4)

- **Core thesis**: Writer's block/resistance is really **fear**, which arises only when we attempt writing that matters. It never disappears; the fix is a small, consistent, kind habit system and using fear as a signal rather than fighting it.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Invent the person who can do the writing** (from Sheila Heti): design the self/alter ego, not the book.
  - **Become a goal-meeting person** / **Aim low** (Jordan Peterson; James Clear's gym-goer who only showed up): the early goal is identity, not output.
  - **Virtuous procrastination**: productive-feeling avoidance (cleaning, desk organising, admin, Instagram strategy, website redesign, excessive research). Flip side: endless research/revision because unfinished work cannot be judged ("perfect potential"). "It's not your magnum opus, just your next work."
  - **Fear as metal detector / ally**: fear beeps where something valuable lies (Pema Chödrön: fear signals nearness to truth). Fearless writing = complacent writing.
  - **Artist and architect**: artist channels ideas uncensored; architect later steps back and sees how it hangs together, cutting generously.
  - **Writing is recursive; revision is writing**: pause after each section, reread, hunt for strongest ideas, build structure around them; the emerging structure shows where to go next (feedback loop). Research is also part of writing.
  - **Writing anorexia**: premature concision as self-punishment disguised as virtue. Money analogy: you can only afford to cut when rich in material.
  - **Wile E. Coyote rule**: when you hit a perfect-ending idea mid-draft, write it in where it occurs; forces you to find something better for the ending.
- **Process / workflow (eight tips, in order)**
  1. Schedule: plan two months of ruthless discipline to form the habit (protect the "seedling"). Write in the morning (willpower depletes; rest of day is a victory lap). 30 min/day is fine, an hour is good; he has done three 90-minute sessions, Pomodoros, and a chess-clock game (4 h work vs 4.5 h break clocks). Consistency over exact timing. Same place, for boundaries.
  2. Tell people the habit ("I write 90 minutes a day"), not the outcome ("I'm writing a book"); get an accountability partner.
  3. Quantity: about two pages/1,000 words a day = book draft in 4-5 months; decompose to 4 x 250 words, 10 words a minute. If word counts fail, use time. Seinfeld rule: you need not write, but may do nothing else. Revision counts. Benefit: knowing when you may stop.
  4. Missed goals: forgive immediately; look forward; **never add today's gap to tomorrow's task**; celebrate hits (day off, or an untracked "bonus day"); drop the drill-sergeant voice; it must be fun.
  5. Getting stuck: (a) in-session: open a new document and type non-stop for 5-10 minutes ("I'm stuck on this piece because..."), same as his EFL fluency drill; (b) lost faith in project: reread everything slowly as a reader, then **outline what you actually have** (reverse outline), moving from sentence level to structure level; (c) talk it through one-to-one; groups above 3-4 get polite and vague; share work when it is going badly.
  6. Catch virtuous procrastination sooner; say your excuses aloud to another writer.
  7. Make fear an ally: ask "what am I most afraid of in this paragraph?" and write about it; keep enough uncertainty that the writing stays a discovery.
  8. Do not prune early; write without knowing the outcome; integrate writing, research, revision.
- **Diagnostics**: productive research moves the current project forward; avoidance research merely eases blank-page feelings.
- **Examples**: Faulkner inspired at nine every morning; the smoker who realised life is never stress-free; Victor Hugo locked in until pages passed under the door; Stephen King (not the model).
- **Stance notes**: Against "write first, revise later" as separate stages ("completely backwards"). He used to obsess over word counts and used to separate research days from writing days; abandoned both. Concision advice is right for style, harmful when applied early. Against outsourcing to AI (dilutes value). He insists this is his actual professional practice, not training wheels.

## I'm an Editor, these are the 7 First-Draft Mistakes I Fix All the Time (2025-09-24, https://youtu.be/bBg7GyIfn0A)

- **Core thesis**: Writers reread their drafts by association, not communication. To edit objectively you must know consciously what readers expect intuitively; a seven-step, top-down diagnostic does this, with mechanics last.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Coffee stain problem**: your words trigger your memory of the idea, like a coffee stain that means something only to you.
  - **Writing patterns vs reading patterns / interference**: writers use text to solve a problem (question -> evidence -> resolution at the end); readers need the point first. Mismatch causes interference. **Directions to the restaurant**: describing your route is useless to someone starting elsewhere.
  - **Three kinds of readers**: **uninformed** (give background so they see problem and stakes); **indifferent** (establish consequences relevant to them); **resistant** (speak their language, acknowledge their position, present it so it seems less stable, so your idea stabilises it).
  - **Text as play/musical**: limit characters; one main character per scene; secondary concepts develop it by comparison/contrast. **Key terms** build coherence.
  - **Subject = spotlight**: clarity is "one simple sentence trick".
  - **Core / core clause**: clear character as subject + clear action as verb; **logical bridges** connect cores.
  - **Grice's maxims** (captioned "Grace's"): readers assume juxtaposed items are logically related, so unspecified relations get guessed.
- **The seven-step diagnostic checklist (in order)**
  1. **Know your reader, not just your audience**. Four questions: what motivated them to click/read? what problem are they dealing with? what answer are they looking for? what decision are they trying to make? He will not look at a client's text until these are answered.
  2. **Lead with the main point**. Find the one sentence readers must accept, act on, or use (usually at the end of the draft or buried mid-paragraph) and move it to the **end of the introduction**.
  3. **Start with a problem readers recognise**, not background. Identify the specific belief blocking your solution and dismantle it.
  4. **Build coherence through key terms**: pick a section, identify the key terms readers must accept, track them start to finish, use the same words or close synonyms.
  5. **Characters in subject position**: put the people, companies, or concepts readers care about in the subject, consistently.
  6. **Logical bridges + actions as verbs**: because, therefore, however, although; express actions as verbs not nouns.
  7. **Edit for mechanics, polish for professionalism** last: grammar, punctuation, spelling, house style, unwritten genre conventions of the platform, formatting.
- **Examples**: "You can always get another pet" (a sentence cannot be judged without its reader). Recommendation rewrite: "After analysing... I recommend" -> "We should launch. Here's why: ...". Squirrel op-ed: v1 ignores resistance; v2 adds irrelevant fact (viruses); v3 targets the blocking belief ("adorable squirrels are harmless" -> 70% carry viruses communicable to humans). Dog chased the cat / cat was chased by the dog: neither is better; depends on which the reader cares about. Jerry Stiller Dramamine joke for implied relations.
- **Stance notes**: Against "avoid repetition, use variation" (grade-school advice; confuses readers). Against blanket active-voice preference. Against grammar-first editing. Feedback words such as dull, passive, abstract only describe reader feelings, not the fault. "Most professional writing fails because it serves the writer instead of the reader."

## I wish I knew this before I started writing online (2025-10-19, https://youtu.be/iCaX5cZ6roo)

- **Core thesis**: "Write about whatever interests you" is a writer's definition. The **reader's definition** (post-it sized): *An essay helps readers understand something better that they want to understand.*
- **Named concepts — the definition unpacked in five parts**
  1. **Helps (specific) readers**: **arguments of fact** (whodunit; belong in reports/briefings) vs **arguments of judgment** (values: good/bad, right/wrong, better/worse; the essay's territory). Judgment-knowledge exists only by consensus in a community, so the reader is "not icing on the cake, they're the batter": structure, conventions, word choice, references all flow downstream from the reading community. Ask "valuable to whom?" Writing for several audiences = several simultaneous chess games, each with its own particulars.
  2. **Understand, not persuade**: every genre has a function. White papers persuade to act; essays improve understanding. Action comes second; chasing it risks failing the primary function.
  3. **Something they want to understand**: **self-centredness** is the biggest block. **Puzzle-piece vs invention model of knowledge**: for facts, a new piece is automatically valuable; for judgment, an idea is an invention taken to market, adopted only if understood and useful. "Your idea only becomes knowledge when readers accept it." Original is not the same as valuable. Coaster-camera-lens gag.
  4. **Better is decided by readers**: essay as directions; know the starting position. Questions: what do they already understand? what do they want to understand? where are the gaps?
  5. **Ability, not intention**: (from a Buddhist monk's definition of love as the ability to help someone suffer less). The ability is **argument**: **claim** (falsifiable; someone could disagree: "water is wet" is not a claim, "water beats Gatorade for hydration" is), **evidence** (whatever this reader finds credible: authorities, primary sources, or an apt anecdote), **warrant** (logical bridge from evidence to claim; evidence does not speak for itself).
- **Process**: before drafting, fix the reading community; identify a judgment question they already care about; locate their current understanding and gaps; build claim-evidence-warrant.
- **Rules**: "Something is not anything and readers are not you." You cannot be agnostic about your reader and write well.
- **Stance notes**: Opens with a clip where an interviewee calls "know your reader" the worst advice and recommends writing for yourself; Fitzpatrick rejects this. Not audience capture: great writers can shift what a community cares about, but it is rare, hard, risky. Against the "if you build it they will come" romantic model. Rejects the manuals' claim that the essay's goal is persuasion (note tension with 2025-09-02, where he calls the Arthur piece's ultimate function persuasion). Prefers reader psychology over lists of rules.

## How to write so well that readers stop scrolling (2025-10-26, https://youtu.be/IqPLeyTahLs)

- **Core thesis**: "Easy writing makes for hard reading." Reading is linear decoding under a working-memory limit of three to four items; readers cope by **chunking** into a **mental briefcase**. Control the four things in the briefcase and prose flows.
- **Named concepts**
  - **Decode/encode; left-to-right construction**: the image builds word by word, so word order is cinematography (particulars-then-context = tilt-down/zoom-out; context-then-focus = wide to close). Neither is better; both are available effects.
  - **Mental briefcase — four contents**:
    1. **The last few words of the sentence** (stress position): lingers like a hand's impression on water. Begin the next sentence on that note: **relay race / baton / sentences shaking hands**.
    2. **The topic**: who or what the sentence is a story about; readers take whatever comes first as the star, and expect it to continue. Reader's mind = camera following a main character.
    3. **Sensory impressions**: concrete language is stored as a compressed full experience and the brain fills in context for free. Abstract nouns-for-actions give nothing to hold.
    4. **Expectations**: micro-level promises that pull readers forward (he prefers this term to "cliffhangers"); set in one sentence, paid off strategically. "The reading brain is in pursuit."
- **The four writing hacks**: (1) put emphasis-worthy information at the end, in the **stress position**; (2) put main characters at the start, in the **topic position**; (3) ground abstract concepts in concrete images; (4) manage reader expectations carefully (track where set, where paid).
- **Examples**: breadcrumbs/sparrows sentence in both orders; "Sarah sat across from her mother. The two women..."; Fred interrupting talk of Jessica's business with the weather; "David pushed through the office door" followed by "The printer was out of paper" (jarring) vs "He headed for the coffee machine"; grandmother's kitchen; "Delivery of the package... occurred" vs "The package was delivered"; "Sarah discovered something shocking in the attic."
- **Rules**: consistency of topic does not mean the same word: pronouns and synonyms (David/he/the writer) are fine. Do not vary sentence openings for variety's sake.
- **Stance notes**: Against "vary your sentences / avoid repetition": monotony and repetition help readers track. Avoids boring signposts; expectations do the work.

## 9 Techniques for Clearer Writing (I Wish I Knew Sooner) (2026-01-04, https://youtu.be/iTZt1_eocUg)

- **Core thesis**: Clarity is storytelling at the sentence level (characters and actions), extended through cohesion/coherence at paragraph level and problem framing plus an index/discussion fractal at idea level. Framed against AI clean-up that erases voice and trust.
- **Techniques (three groups)**
  - *Sentence level*
    1. **Main characters in the subject position** (centre stage / middle of frame).
    2. **Actions in verbs**: how you **construe** the action matters (Rembrandt vs Picasso on the same fruit bowl). Actions in nouns force "vague, flabby verbs" (undertaken, took place, resulted).
    3. **Nominalizations ("zombie nouns") — four good uses**: (a) as subject linking back to the previous sentence ("their discovery"); (b) replacing "the fact that / the idea that"; (c) turning a clause into a concrete **object** ("I approved his proposal") — they work as objects, not as subjects; (d) concepts so familiar to your readers they act as characters ("greenhouse effect", "marketing"). Skill: distinguish nominalizations hiding important actions from familiar ones. Revision drill: "There is a need for an examination of the marketing strategy" -> find hidden verbs, ask who? -> "We need to examine the marketing strategy."
  - *Paragraph level*
    4. **Busan taxi driver effect** (choppy start-stop prose). Fix: end sentences with information the next can pick up (handoff). Passive voice is a legitimate tool for this.
    5. **Cohesion vs coherence** (jigsaw: pieces fitting vs the picture on the box). Incoherence has three causes: unrelated subjects, no common **theme** (a web/ecosystem of related words), no thesis. **Three-step diagnostic**: underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence (stop at the verb); ask whether they form a small set of related topics *your readers* would see as related and name the key characters; if not, rewrite so subjects name topics and sit near the start.
    6. **Stress position / runway**: a plane needs a clear runway before turbulence. Sentence template: short introductory phrase -> short concrete subject -> specific-action verb -> then length and complexity. Put long/complex phrases and new information, especially unfamiliar technical terms, at the end. Order: simple to complex, old/familiar to new.
  - *Idea level*
    7. **Problem framing**: reader as market shopper; "new" and "I worked hard" are not value. "If readers cannot perceive a problem, they cannot perceive value." Readers vary by awareness (know problem; know solution, seeking provider; unaware). Framework: **status quo** (the opposite of thesis X) -> **concession** -> **destabilizing condition** (power word but/however) -> **cost** (or benefit), tuned to the specific reader -> **solution**.
    8. **Four signals of coherence** (coherence is an experience readers create, not something on the page; the **curse of knowledge** hides its absence): clear section boundaries with headings built from key concepts that recur in the section; a short introductory segment; a **point sentence** at the end of that introduction; **repeated key concepts**, introduced toward the end of the point sentence. Give the conceptual map before the terrain.
    9. **Index and discussion (fractal)**: every unit has a short, easily grasped opening stating the point and key concepts, then a longer, more complex development. Sentence: short subject + longer predicate. Complex sentence: short main clause + longer supporting clause. Paragraph: short opener + development. Section: opening paragraph + discussion. Document: introduction + body.
- **Examples**: Jack and the Beanstalk in nominalised vs verbal form; Watson and Crick; network-effects fill-in-the-blank; Portland coffee passage (cohesive, incoherent); web-navigation rewrite with "visitors" as subject; 22-word subject on school funding vs "In this research, we examine..."; Seattle testing zone; coral reef/ocean acidification; algebra training study paragraph.
- **Stance notes**: Against macro story formulas (hero's journey, story circle, but/therefore) as the locus of storytelling. Against "avoid the passive". Against "write for yourself" ("one of the worst pieces of advice"). Headings must not be riddles or inside jokes. "Writing is not paint by numbers." Material clearly tracks Joseph Williams's *Style* tradition (not named).

## I Analysed 100 Opening Paragraphs. Here's What Makes Readers Obsessed. (2026-01-11, https://youtu.be/ePQoPNHZ7UY)

- **Core thesis**: Openings fail because they present hooks or a topic plus the author's enthusiasm rather than value. Value is a function of problems and solutions; the introduction must answer the reader's number-one question, "So what?"
- **Framework: the three-part introduction**
  1. **Status quo / common ground**: get the reader nodding. Steps: (i) **invert your idea** and open with its opposite; (ii) find what readers already believe and will not dispute — three ways in: historical background, recent event, common belief; (iii) state it plainly without over-explaining; (iv) treat it as the setup for a "but", so it must deserve to be challenged.
  2. **Problem = condition + consequence**. Condition: what is wrong/unstable, signalled by but/however (reserve these words for this job in the opening). Consequence: cost the reader does not want to pay or benefit they want. Apply the **"so what?" test** by imagining a specific reader saying it; your answer is the consequence. Keep condition and consequence close together; "a condition with no cost is not a problem."
  3. **Solution preview**: state immediately what you offer: the promise and direction, not details. Two failure modes: lecturing on background ("asked the time, explains how to build a watch") and vagueness ("we need to think differently"). **Practical problem** -> solution says what to do; **conceptual problem** -> solution changes what they think.
  - Optional **prelude** (statistic, anecdote, quote, meme); unnecessary if the problem is strong.
- **Examples**: George Mack's "High Agency" essay (chosen because the idea is good and it succeeded, so critique is "punching up"); its intro asserts importance and personal effort but gives no reason to care. His rewrite: common ground (many people wait for orders; the principal-agent problem) -> but low-agency thinking is spreading -> costs to individuals and organisations -> high agency can be developed through patterns and small compounding behaviours, which the essay will show. Two-vendor market parable; party guest who says everything you believe is wrong.
- **Rules**: tune the consequence to the specific reader (individuals vs CEOs). Focus on the *function* of each move, not polish.
- **Stance notes**: Against cliché hooks. Here the five-piece problem of 2025-09-02/2026-01-04 is compressed to three parts; the concession is folded into common ground. Claims the pattern holds across newsletters, white papers, research reports, sales pages, book proposals.

## How to Articulate Your Thoughts More Clearly than 99% of Writers (2026-01-18, https://youtu.be/nsa44tXal7A)

- **Core thesis**: Writing as you speak imports verbal tics. Readers want prose "like computer code: simple, elegant, efficient". The goal is **compression** (less cognitive load), not short sentences. Five techniques: three prune, two add back.
- **The five techniques**
  1. **Cut filler words** ("throat clearing", "verbal static"): actually, basically, certain, various, particularly, really, virtually, individual, generally, given, practically.
  2. **Cut doublets**: each and every, first and foremost, any and all, full and complete, true and accurate, basic and fundamental. Origin: pairing French/Latin with Anglo-Saxon words to display learning after the Norman Conquest. Keep the stronger word.
  3. **Cut redundancy — three types**: **redundant modifiers** (anticipate *upcoming*, *totally* transform, *previous* experience, *ultimate* result, *suddenly* astonish; future plans, free gift, consensus of opinion); **redundant categories** (large *in size*, round *in shape*, intuitive *in nature*; sometimes convert to an adverb: "in a logical manner" -> "logically"); **general implications** (spread across phrases: "attempting to learn the conventions for writing the art of poetry" -> "learning the conventions of poetry").
  4. **Replace phrases with the exact word** (le mot juste): change the vehicle, not the route. Swaps: the reason for -> why; despite the fact that -> although; in the event that -> if; in a situation where -> when; concerning the matter of -> about. Diagnostic: "look through the words to the ideas"; many words for few ideas signals replacements.
  5. **Write in the affirmative**: negatives make readers process and then reverse. Swaps: not different -> similar; not allow -> prevent; not notice -> overlook; not many -> few; not often -> rarely; not include -> omit. Watch **implicit negatives** (preclude, prevent, lack, fail, doubt, reject, avoid, deny, refuse, exclude, contradict, prohibit, bar, without, against, unless, except) compounding with not/no, and worst of all with passives and nominalizations. Repair order: get actions out of nouns into verbs, then flip to the positive. Exception: keep the negative when the warning is the point.
- **Examples**: "In my personal opinion, it is necessary that we should not ignore the opportunity to think over each and every suggestion offered" -> "We should consider every suggestion." Long revising sentence -> "As you revise your writing, first replace abstractions with examples." Benefits sentence with four reversals -> "To receive benefits, submit your documents." Payments sentence -> "If you are paying more than $100, notify this office."
- **Stance notes**: "Write the way you speak" is among the worst advice. Sentence length does not matter if shape is right. A wide vocabulary is for compression, not showing off. Normally avoids nominalizations but uses one as the object of a transitive verb ("revise your writing"), consistent with 2026-01-04.

## Write 10X Clearer: Do These 3 Writing Exercises to Connect with Readers (2026-01-25, https://youtu.be/UqiEjaCiWmQ)

- **Core thesis**: Professional writing fails from self-enclosure, not incorrectness. Clear writing anchors whatever complex situation you are in to the **classic scene of joint attention** (two people looking at one thing). What conversation offers is "not the words, it's the scene".
- **Named concepts**
  - **"Autistic writing"** (his term, from Greek *autos*, self): careful, formal, distant prose that turns in on itself and makes readers fill gaps.
  - **Scene of conversation / joint attention / classic scene of shared attention**: shared mental context is the anchor of clear writing.
  - **Rhetorical planning wheel**: centre = **genre and actions**; rim = the **situational network**, of which he treats four components: **purpose** (and the actions/steps that accomplish it); **writer's role** (relation to topic: expert or reliant on secondary sources; relation to reader: appropriate social attitude, e.g. confidence without arrogance; ownership of and confidence in the information); **audience** (what they know, want to know, use the text for, where they expect to find things); **context** (the text as an agent in the world: what event caused it, what is expected to happen after).
  - **"The text must match the context."** Generic advice is useless for professional purposes.
  - **Belief decomposition question**: "For the reader to believe X about me, what must they also believe?" Break the trait into qualities; find a real story for each.
  - **Blending**: structure the network with the classic scene: distant audience -> one companion beside you; abstract topic -> something visible; past event -> present tense; persuasion/analysis -> showing (show the problem, then show the solution) rather than arguing.
- **The three exercises**
  1. **Joint-attention drill** (about 10 minutes, with a partner): alternate presenting verifiable, present details "like a gift" (the oak's middle branch curves like a fish hook; a bird's three notes, a warble, three more). No imagination or inference. Solo: record yourself, or describe a picture to an imagined companion.
  2. **Map the situational network** with the rhetorical planning wheel before writing a word.
  3. **Blend**: write one paragraph on the mapped network anchored in the classic scene; read it aloud; repeat until automatic.
- **Examples**: the Stanford postdoc applicant whose YouTube-watching anecdote signalled passive consumption; the panel screens for "intellectual vitality", so they decomposed the trait and mapped stories to it. Jane Austen to Cassandra: she says she has been "talking" to her throughout the letter despite distance and delay. Apple tree described in present tense.
- **Stance notes**: Here he repairs rather than rejects "write the way you speak": the usual reading (swap utilize for use) misses the point. Doing this unconsciously yields a style you cannot control. Draws on the classic-style tradition (joint attention, blending), not named. Closing: clarity is half of expert writing; the other half is having something valuable to say.

## Cross-video patterns in this batch

- **One governing axiom**: good writing is a function of the reader's experience; the reader decides what is good, valuable, and "better". It recurs as the coffee stain problem, writing vs reading patterns, the curse of knowledge, the reader's definition of the essay, and "autistic writing". His recurring villain is self-centred writing; his recurring "worst advice" list: write for yourself, vary your words/openings, avoid the passive, write the way you speak, write first and revise later, cut early, lead with hooks.
- **Two halves of expert writing**: value (problem/solution, reading community, argument) and clarity (sentence, paragraph, document mechanics). He says value comes first logically ("cart before the horse") and mechanics last in editing.
- **The problem framework is the backbone of value**, in three versions: five pieces (status quo, concession, destabilizing condition, consequence, solution; 2025-09-02 and 2026-01-04); reader-type version (uninformed/indifferent/resistant; 2025-09-24; awareness levels in 2026-01-04); three parts (status quo, problem = condition + consequence, solution; 2026-01-11). Constant elements: invert your thesis for the opening, concede before challenging, signal with but/however, attach a reader-specific cost or benefit, distinguish conceptual from practical problems, put the point at the end of the introduction.
- **The theatre/camera metaphor is the backbone of clarity**: subject = spotlight/centre stage/topic position; characters as subjects, actions as verbs (core clauses); topic strings; key terms as a limited cast; concepts as chess pieces that are introduced, named, and redeployed. The same idea appears in the CNF video (characters and themes all introduced in the opening number, then recalled).
- **Sentence architecture is consistent**: old/simple/short at the front, new/complex/emphatic at the end (stress position); end-to-start handoffs for cohesion; consistent topics for coherence. This scales up as the index/discussion fractal and the four signals of coherence; "one paragraph, one message/job" and section-function labelling appear in both model-essay analyses and in the reverse-outline unblocking technique.
- **Concreteness** runs throughout: stub-your-toe language, smokestack economy, intuition pumps, sensory impressions in the briefcase, presenting abstractions as visible things via blending.
- **Teaching method**: annotate model texts (Gessner, Arthur/McCarthy, George Mack, Austen) by function; before/after rewrites; diagnostics that defeat self-familiarity (underline first 7-8 words, punctuation X-ray, so-what test, four reader questions, reverse outline, value-word mining).
- **Process philosophy** (mainly 2025-09-08): drafting is discovery, generous and uncensored (artist), followed and interleaved with structural judgement (architect); revision and research are writing; compress only when rich. This reconciles the apparently opposed advice to not cut early (Sept 2025) and to be a ruthless compressor (Jan 2026; McCarthy's minimalism): they apply to different phases.
- **Tensions / evolution**
  - Persuasion: the Arthur piece's "ultimate function" is persuasion (Sept 2025), yet the essay's goal is understanding, not persuasion (Oct 2025), and the Jan 2026 blending advice says show rather than argue. Reconcilable: he favours an inviting, same-side-of-the-table, showing stance even when the aim is to change minds.
  - "Write the way you speak": flatly rejected for diction (2026-01-18), then rehabilitated a week later as being about the scene, not the words.
  - Nominalizations: blanket "actions as verbs" (Sept-Oct 2025) matures into four legitimate uses (Jan 2026). Likewise passive voice is consistently defended as a cohesion/focus tool.
  - Problem framework shrinks from five pieces to three for teaching introductions; the concession becomes "common ground".
  - Channel framing shifts from creative non-fiction and online essays (autumn 2025) toward professional/academic clients and anti-AI positioning (Jan 2026), with Williams-style and classic-style material more prominent.
