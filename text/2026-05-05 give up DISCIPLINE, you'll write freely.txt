The point here is not about the ideas themselves,&nbsp;&nbsp; but about the conditions that gave rise&nbsp; to them. So what do these two situations&nbsp;&nbsp; have in common? And how can you set&nbsp; those same conditions in your life? To do that, I need to give you a piece&nbsp; of psychoanalytic theory that completely&nbsp;&nbsp; changed the way I understand creativity and&nbsp; the unconscious mind — because ultimately,&nbsp;&nbsp; that's where your creativity&nbsp; comes from. The unconscious. The basic idea in psychoanalysis&nbsp; is that we are not a whole,&nbsp;&nbsp; coherent self — just one self — but&nbsp; actually, well, Freud would have said&nbsp;&nbsp; that we're more like two: the conscious mind&nbsp; and the unconscious mind. Like an iceberg,&nbsp;&nbsp; you know — we only know the tip, but there's&nbsp; a deeper layer called the unconscious. But there's another thinker named Lacan who takes&nbsp; this a little bit further, and I think this gives&nbsp;&nbsp; us a really good model for understanding how&nbsp; creativity works and where it comes from. Lacan&nbsp;&nbsp; says that the self — human subjectivity — is&nbsp; actually made up of three different layers,&nbsp;&nbsp; or registers. He calls them the Real, the&nbsp; Symbolic, and the Imaginary. The Real goes&nbsp;&nbsp; to the Imaginary, the Imaginary to the&nbsp; Symbolic, and the Symbolic back to the&nbsp;&nbsp; Real. And in the middle there is this force called&nbsp; *jouissance*, which bubbles up through the Real. All right, so what does any of that mean? The Imaginary layer of yourself is who you&nbsp; imagine yourself to be. It's the self you&nbsp;&nbsp; see when you look in the mirror in your mind.&nbsp; It's the story you tell yourself about who you&nbsp;&nbsp; are and who you're supposed to be. The&nbsp; Symbolic is your social self — the set&nbsp;&nbsp; of masks and personas that you put on&nbsp; to navigate the world and satisfy the&nbsp;&nbsp; demands that other people put on you. It's your&nbsp; job, your relationships, and your reputation. But the Real — to put it simply — is&nbsp; who you really are behind closed doors.&nbsp;&nbsp; It's what makes you uniquely you. You could&nbsp; call this your soul, or your true self,&nbsp;&nbsp; or however you want to put it. It's the&nbsp; thing that makes you *you* like nobody else. But here's the thing: we have these layers&nbsp; on top of it. We have an Imaginary self,&nbsp;&nbsp; like an ego — a story we tell ourselves about&nbsp; who we are that has a kind of consistency.&nbsp;&nbsp; If somebody asks you who you are, you tell&nbsp; a story about it, and it has a beginning,&nbsp;&nbsp; middle, and end and a coherent whole.&nbsp; The Real isn't like that at all. It's&nbsp;&nbsp; incoherent. It can't be contained.&nbsp; It exceeds all bounds and forms. So you have the Imaginary — the self you see&nbsp; when you look in the mirror — and then you&nbsp;&nbsp; have all these masks you use to navigate the&nbsp; world, your job and your relationships. And&nbsp;&nbsp; all of these are piled on top of the Real,&nbsp; holding it down and containing its excesses&nbsp;&nbsp; so that you don't screw up socially and so&nbsp; that you don't lose the sense of self you've&nbsp;&nbsp; cultivated. These things are blocking&nbsp; you from the source of your creativity. And I think that's what was happening in my&nbsp; story. Whenever I was packing up my typewriter,&nbsp;&nbsp; I was not just clearing room off of my desk.&nbsp; In a sense, I was killing the Imaginary part&nbsp;&nbsp; of myself. I'd been carrying this vision of&nbsp; myself as a novelist for years and years — I&nbsp;&nbsp; don't even know where I got it — but I was at&nbsp; a point in my life when that ego was no longer&nbsp;&nbsp; in service. I know that because in living out&nbsp; that fantasy, I was not happy. I was miserable.&nbsp;&nbsp; I didn't feel free. I didn't have a real outlet&nbsp; to express who I am. And it was driving me crazy. It was only when I packed it up that I was&nbsp; able to unleash all this pent-up energy.&nbsp;&nbsp; It came through. And then I built up a new&nbsp; image of myself — one who's here teaching on&nbsp;&nbsp; YouTube — interested in everything that&nbsp; comes with this completely new genre,&nbsp;&nbsp; this completely new medium. It only came into&nbsp; the world when I killed that part of myself. The same goes for the Symbolic, the social&nbsp; persona. It was only when I was on the&nbsp;&nbsp; meditation retreat and separated from all my&nbsp; relationships that I was able to access this&nbsp;&nbsp; new wave of creativity. Of course, these things&nbsp; are necessary — you're not going to just dispense&nbsp;&nbsp; with them. But sometimes they can calcify.&nbsp; If we don't allow the energy from the Real&nbsp;&nbsp; to come up and invigorate these conceptions&nbsp; of self and these symbolic relationships,&nbsp;&nbsp; then we can become stuck. And I&nbsp; think that is what writer's block is. It's when the energy from *jouissance* is&nbsp; not able to come up and inform the kind&nbsp;&nbsp; of persona and the fantasies that you use to&nbsp; shape yourself in the world. In both cases,&nbsp;&nbsp; the thing that unleashed this creativity was not&nbsp; a new discipline routine. It was the ability,&nbsp;&nbsp; in one way or another, to get out of the way&nbsp; — to loosen my grip on these constructs and&nbsp;&nbsp; allow them to be rejuvenated through this&nbsp; energy that's already pent up inside of me. Although this might seem pretty inspirational&nbsp; and easy to do at a theoretical level,&nbsp;&nbsp; it's not easy in practice. We're actually&nbsp; very attached to these things. In my case,&nbsp;&nbsp; I would not have easily dispensed with the idea&nbsp; of myself as a novelist. And similarly with the&nbsp;&nbsp; masks we wear in public — to lose that façade,&nbsp; we have that expression (at least in Asia):&nbsp;&nbsp; to lose face. It's very painful and&nbsp; embarrassing to lose face, because that&nbsp;&nbsp; means you are letting down and disappointing&nbsp; the expectations that others put on you. But it's also painful to release the energy&nbsp; through the Real into these layers — because,&nbsp;&nbsp; as I said, when I was lying there in bed, these&nbsp; were exciting ideas I was getting flooded with,&nbsp;&nbsp; but it was almost too much to bear.&nbsp; It's very overwhelming. In fact,&nbsp;&nbsp; that's what these layers are for. They're&nbsp; protective. These are the floodgates that&nbsp;&nbsp; hold back the tidal waters. But the cost&nbsp; of that protection is your creative work. Which brings me to the point that runs&nbsp; underneath this entire video: **you have&nbsp;&nbsp; to let yourself die in order for your work to&nbsp; live.** Real creativity means letting go of&nbsp;&nbsp; control — especially control over who you imagine&nbsp; yourself to be and who others expect you to be. So if discipline is not the answer to your&nbsp; writer's block, then what is? And how do you&nbsp;&nbsp; develop a relationship to the Real and&nbsp; to your unconscious without forcing it? If you've been developing a writing&nbsp; practice for any amount of time,&nbsp;&nbsp; you've probably heard of all kinds of productivity&nbsp; routines — from people like Jerry Seinfeld,&nbsp;&nbsp; who locks himself in a room for whatever it&nbsp; is, one hour a day, with a rule where during&nbsp;&nbsp; that time he doesn't need to write, but he's not&nbsp; allowed to do anything else. No phone, no books,&nbsp;&nbsp; no music. You just sit there until something&nbsp; comes. This story gets passed around as advice,&nbsp;&nbsp; but has anybody ever actually tried&nbsp; it? Well, I've tried it, folks,&nbsp;&nbsp; and let me tell you — it doesn't work. I think&nbsp; it's more myth than actual practical advice. It probably works for a certain kind of low-value&nbsp; writing. For emails or reports, I guess it would&nbsp;&nbsp; work to have a forcing function that makes you do&nbsp; it. But for creative work that's uniquely yours,&nbsp;&nbsp; this does not work. Because look at what&nbsp; you're doing: you're sitting in a room and&nbsp;&nbsp; trying to force this to come up. And the&nbsp; unconscious just does not work that way. All this language around discipline,&nbsp;&nbsp; when you think about it through this&nbsp; frame of the unconscious — you realize&nbsp;&nbsp; that it's just a substitute for forcing the&nbsp; unconscious. And you simply cannot do that.&nbsp;&nbsp; It doesn't work. It's your unconscious&nbsp; calling the shots around here, not you. The best way to understand this is with the&nbsp; metaphor of the elephant. You've got an elephant,&nbsp;&nbsp; and then you've got a rider on top. The rider is&nbsp; sitting on top of the elephant holding the reins,&nbsp;&nbsp; and they think they're in control. The&nbsp; elephant might have good humor — it might&nbsp;&nbsp; take some suggestions if you guide it&nbsp; this way and that. But if the elephant&nbsp;&nbsp; decides that it wants to go in a certain&nbsp; direction, what are you going to do? I think this comes up a lot whenever we&nbsp; pick long-term projects. You decide five&nbsp;&nbsp; years ago that you're going to write some&nbsp; fantasy novel or some collection of essays,&nbsp;&nbsp; and suddenly you feel blocked. Well, I think&nbsp; that's because you're up there trying to&nbsp;&nbsp; push the elephant in one direction, but&nbsp; the elephant is done with that. Every&nbsp;&nbsp; blocked rider is doing the same thing: they&nbsp; picked some destination long ago — some book&nbsp;&nbsp; project or idea of self — and they're trying to&nbsp; force the elephant to go in that direction. But&nbsp;&nbsp; whenever you say you have writer's block, I&nbsp; think it's because you've picked a project&nbsp;&nbsp; that the elephant no longer wants to comply with.&nbsp; So here you are sitting on top of the elephant,&nbsp;&nbsp; yanking at the reins, trying to control&nbsp; it, and wondering why nothing's happening. But what if the elephant actually knew better&nbsp; than the rider? What if the elephant had the&nbsp;&nbsp; rider's best intentions in mind and&nbsp; could see a little bit farther? Would&nbsp;&nbsp; you loosen your grip a little bit, trust it,&nbsp; and let it take you where it's trying to go?&nbsp;&nbsp; And what if the place where it was trying to&nbsp; go was better than the one you had in mind? The unconscious is working&nbsp; on your behalf all the time,&nbsp;&nbsp; even when you sleep. Scientists and&nbsp; mathematicians call it the night shift,&nbsp;&nbsp; because there have been so many cases when&nbsp; a researcher will be working on a problem,&nbsp;&nbsp; then go to sleep, and the solution&nbsp; comes to them in the riddle of a dream. So one&nbsp;&nbsp; day Kekulé goes to sleep and has a dream&nbsp; of the mythical snake eating its own tail,&nbsp;&nbsp; the Ouroboros. He wakes up and suddenly&nbsp; realizes: *aha, it's a ring.* Kekulé&nbsp;&nbsp; solved this very important scientific&nbsp; problem not at his desk, but in his sleep. But this doesn't just work for scientists and&nbsp; researchers. Anybody who's played an instrument&nbsp;&nbsp; knows how this works. If you play guitar or&nbsp; piano — chime in in the comments if this has&nbsp;&nbsp; ever happened to you — you start learning a&nbsp; new song on day one at zero percent. You pick&nbsp;&nbsp; a section and you practice and you practice,&nbsp; and after about an hour you get exhausted and&nbsp;&nbsp; hit your limit. You're not making any more&nbsp; marginal progress. So you set it down and go&nbsp;&nbsp; to sleep. Let's say on day one you went from 0%&nbsp; to 15% — 15% of the song you can now play. Well,&nbsp;&nbsp; the following day when you practice&nbsp; that song again, do you start at 15%?&nbsp;&nbsp; No. You start at like 25%, 30%. You make&nbsp; progress in the song *in your sleep.* I don't mean that you're learning notes&nbsp; you haven't studied before — I'm talking&nbsp;&nbsp; about the dexterity to perform the song. It&nbsp; becomes a lot easier to play and a lot more&nbsp;&nbsp; natural. That's because the muscle memory gets&nbsp; consolidated and streamlined in your sleep.&nbsp;&nbsp; You were working on a problem during the day with&nbsp; your conscious mind, and then your conscious mind&nbsp;&nbsp; handed it over to your subconscious, which did&nbsp; its own procedures on it. That's the night shift. Why do you suppose it is that the unconscious&nbsp; speaks to us in dreams with mythological snake&nbsp;&nbsp; shapes instead of just telling us the&nbsp; answer in plain English? Simply enough,&nbsp;&nbsp; it's because the unconscious is older than&nbsp; language. Language came around only about&nbsp;&nbsp; 10,000 years ago, but the unconscious has been&nbsp; around for millions of years — as long as Homo&nbsp;&nbsp; sapiens have been around. The unconscious&nbsp; is the operating system for an animal. If&nbsp;&nbsp; an animal did not have an unconscious, it would&nbsp; be a plant. The unconscious existed long before&nbsp;&nbsp; we had words to think with, and it still&nbsp; runs underneath every thought that you have. So whenever you can't reach an idea with this&nbsp; newest part of your brain — the neocortex,&nbsp;&nbsp; the language part — the unconscious is still&nbsp; there working in the background. It just&nbsp;&nbsp; speaks in a different register: in images,&nbsp; sensations, dreams, hunches, and itches. So if you're feeling blocked, this is what&nbsp; your elephant has been trying to tell you.&nbsp;&nbsp; The unconscious is not your adversary. It's not&nbsp; some chaotic force that you have to manage. It's&nbsp;&nbsp; constantly working on your behalf, solving&nbsp; problems that the neocortex, this new kid&nbsp;&nbsp; on the block, couldn't solve itself. It's going&nbsp; to be composing the sentence you couldn't finish&nbsp;&nbsp; yesterday, and connecting the two ideas&nbsp; you'd never thought to connect together. This, I think, is how I've learned to trust&nbsp; the elephant — my unconscious. I've learned&nbsp;&nbsp; through experience that it gives you what&nbsp; you need precisely when you need it. And&nbsp;&nbsp; the times in my life when I've just followed&nbsp; it — call it intuition or grace or whatever&nbsp;&nbsp; it might be — it has led me somewhere&nbsp; better than I, in my previous state,&nbsp;&nbsp; could have ever imagined. We use the&nbsp; best of our knowledge to make our plans,&nbsp;&nbsp; but sometimes those plans are lousy because&nbsp; you don't know what you don't know. The&nbsp;&nbsp; unconscious is looking out for you and it's&nbsp; trying to tell you something important. So if you're blocked on this project&nbsp; and it's just not letting you through,&nbsp;&nbsp; maybe just consider: I'm all for goals. I don't&nbsp; think there's anything wrong with trying to be&nbsp;&nbsp; more disciplined in your life. But the problem&nbsp; is taking a discipline approach to what is&nbsp;&nbsp; essentially a creative endeavor. If you want&nbsp; to be an artist, you need to learn to listen&nbsp;&nbsp; to your unconscious and follow it wherever it's&nbsp; trying to lead you. You cannot summon it. You&nbsp;&nbsp; can't give it a deadline, and you can't bully&nbsp; it. You are not going to David Goggins your&nbsp;&nbsp; way through this book. But the good news is&nbsp; you don't have to. It's already on your side. So if the unconscious is working on your behalf,&nbsp;&nbsp; the only question left is: how do&nbsp; you give it the room to deliver? My answer is freewriting. Here's a way to&nbsp; think about it. If you imagine yourself as&nbsp;&nbsp; being made up of those three different layers, it&nbsp; actually looks kind of like a volcano. Below you,&nbsp;&nbsp; inside, is magma — pressurized and full&nbsp; of energy, with no shape and no language.&nbsp;&nbsp; This is the source of your creativity. Freewriting&nbsp; is the act of pulling a little bit of that magma&nbsp;&nbsp; to the surface every day. You sit down and just&nbsp; write whatever comes to mind. No filter, no plan. You've probably heard something like this before,&nbsp; but let me clarify a few things. When you do this&nbsp;&nbsp; freewriting, it's really important not to put&nbsp; any pressure on yourself to produce something&nbsp;&nbsp; meaningful. You're not sitting down and doing&nbsp; freewriting and then trying to adapt it into a&nbsp;&nbsp; newsletter. You're not freewriting as the first&nbsp; phase of your essay. I'm not even talking about&nbsp;&nbsp; first drafts here. What I'm talking about&nbsp; is dredging up the Real — pulling up this&nbsp;&nbsp; creative magma within you and just getting&nbsp; it into language, into a shape. A lot of&nbsp;&nbsp; which is going to be totally trash. It's not&nbsp; even "trash" in the usual sense, because you&nbsp;&nbsp; can't apply qualitative judgments to it&nbsp; — something is only good or bad against&nbsp;&nbsp; something you were trying to achieve. If&nbsp; you're using language like good, bad, better,&nbsp;&nbsp; worse, you've already missed the point. The&nbsp; product of this freewriting is not meaningful. If there's a goal to it, the goal is just to&nbsp; open up a vent. Because when you're blocked,&nbsp;&nbsp; it's because these things are&nbsp; blocking this. What you want is&nbsp;&nbsp; to open a vent and let a little&nbsp; bit of this juice out every day,&nbsp;&nbsp; so it can energize and rejuvenate the images&nbsp; and symbols we use to fashion ourselves and&nbsp;&nbsp; our understanding and our model of the&nbsp; world. Almost like you're dropping the&nbsp;&nbsp; magma into a bucket. When you drop magma&nbsp; into a bucket, you don't decide what the&nbsp;&nbsp; shape is. It's just going to take whatever&nbsp; shape it takes based on the interaction of&nbsp;&nbsp; the original source material and the new&nbsp; conditions in the environment. That's it. Now, you as the excavator of this magma can&nbsp; look at that final shape — and of course&nbsp;&nbsp; you're not going to try to give this&nbsp; to somebody else; it's not meaningful,&nbsp;&nbsp; it's not useful, it's just magma. But it&nbsp; might solidify into a shape that you can&nbsp;&nbsp; then refine into something that another&nbsp; person might find useful or beautiful. When you do this freewriting, it's going to take&nbsp; whatever form it takes. There's nothing you can do&nbsp;&nbsp; about that. You can't will it to be prettier. You&nbsp; can't will it to be more useful. You just drop it&nbsp;&nbsp; into the bucket, drop it into language, and let&nbsp; it cool down. Whatever you get is what you get.&nbsp;&nbsp; But that's fine, because freewriting is not meant&nbsp; to be meaningful. It's not for anybody — and that&nbsp;&nbsp; includes you. What comes up is going to look&nbsp; like gobbledygook, and that's perfectly fine. If you were a painter, this would be the&nbsp; equivalent of just moving paint around on&nbsp;&nbsp; the canvas. This isn't an early sketch of&nbsp; a painting you're going to do. It's not a&nbsp;&nbsp; study. Sometimes painters will do that&nbsp; — little sketches, experiments with the&nbsp;&nbsp; paint. That's not what we're doing. We are&nbsp; *playing* with paint at this stage. That's it.&nbsp;&nbsp; Magma has no coherent structure. It's just full of&nbsp; energy. The whole point of this practice is just&nbsp;&nbsp; to get that energy out of the underground and&nbsp; put it into a form cool enough for you to hold. Once the magma's cooled — which might be moments&nbsp; later, or hours later, or days later — then the&nbsp;&nbsp; analytical mind can come online and you can&nbsp; read what you wrote and start to sort it out.&nbsp;&nbsp; You're going to find that a lot of it is dross,&nbsp; and that's fine. But you're also going to find&nbsp;&nbsp; certain bits that have a bit of a shine to them,&nbsp; or what some people say has *energy* — there's&nbsp;&nbsp; something to it, something you see, something&nbsp; meaningful, something true, something that you&nbsp;&nbsp; can work with. As you work with it, you're going&nbsp; to start to shape it into something you can share&nbsp;&nbsp; with others — something that's either useful&nbsp; like a tool or beautiful like a sculpture. This kind of analysis is where your craft lives.&nbsp; That's when the manuscript, the newsletter,&nbsp;&nbsp; the essay, the script — that's when it gets&nbsp; made. But the sequence here is really important,&nbsp;&nbsp; because you can't sculpt magma. You've&nbsp; got to let it cool down. You've got to&nbsp;&nbsp; let these ideas first percolate inside&nbsp; of you. You can't force this to come up. Whenever you do these freewriting sessions,&nbsp; you just get whatever you're given. The&nbsp;&nbsp; Greeks wrote about this as being given&nbsp; gifts from the gods — it's as though&nbsp;&nbsp; it's coming *through* you. You can't choose&nbsp; what comes up. You've just got to allow it&nbsp;&nbsp; to come up and then review it. It's great&nbsp; if you do this in note-taking software,&nbsp;&nbsp; because you can use a random note function&nbsp; to resurface old freewriting scraps. You can&nbsp;&nbsp; really discover things months or years later&nbsp; that you didn't see the day you wrote them. Even if you've never actually done this writing&nbsp; practice, you already know the difference I'm&nbsp;&nbsp; talking about. Think about the difference&nbsp; between two different kinds of conversation. Sometimes you have a conversation with somebody&nbsp; and you're just in your head. You're a little&nbsp;&nbsp; self-conscious, a little dry-mouthed — your&nbsp; ideas aren't really coming and they're pretty&nbsp;&nbsp; clunky when they do. And if you do hit any kind&nbsp; of coherent patch, what you're doing is actually&nbsp;&nbsp; rehearsing something you've said before. The&nbsp; other person, whenever this happens, usually&nbsp;&nbsp; gets a sense like they're being talked *at* rather&nbsp; than talked *with*. That's a bad conversation. It's not an old idea&nbsp;&nbsp; you've rehearsed. It's not a familiar argument.&nbsp; As teachers, we have these kinds of teaching&nbsp;&nbsp; routines: you say this, then you say that, then&nbsp; you insert a joke here. That's not what we're&nbsp;&nbsp; talking about. Sometimes in a conversation&nbsp; you just hit your stride and the ideas start&nbsp;&nbsp; pouring out one by one, and they have a kind of&nbsp; energy. It's really exciting when this happens. And every time this happens, it's not because you&nbsp; planned it to be this way — it's because you got&nbsp;&nbsp; out of the way. You weren't generating&nbsp; and judging. You were just letting the&nbsp;&nbsp; magma rise. Because your unconscious is&nbsp; always working for you in the background,&nbsp;&nbsp; it will give you sometimes these ideas that&nbsp; are entirely coherent, thought through,&nbsp;&nbsp; easy to articulate, well-argued, substantiated&nbsp; by evidence and experience — the whole package.&nbsp;&nbsp; That happens in conversation. Of course, you can never force it. But&nbsp; by regularly practicing freewriting,&nbsp;&nbsp; you give it an opportunity to happen. Most&nbsp; of what comes up is trash — probably 90%&nbsp;&nbsp; of it. This is magma we're talking about. But&nbsp; the 10% that isn't is going to be better than&nbsp;&nbsp; anything you could have planned. That's how&nbsp; the unconscious works. It knows who you are.&nbsp;&nbsp; It knows what you're working on and it's&nbsp; always working for you in the background.&nbsp;&nbsp; You've just got to get out of the way&nbsp; and give it a chance to step forward. The way to do this is to write the raw material&nbsp; first and then bring your intellect online to&nbsp;&nbsp; do the shaping and refining.
