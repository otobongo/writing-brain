# Writer Science: Master Technique Catalog

A deduplicated catalog of every technique William Fitzpatrick teaches across the 50 *Writer Science* videos (V1 to V50, Feb 2024 to Sep 2026), merged from ten deep-read reports and spot-checked against the transcripts. It is built to be lifted into a Claude writing skill that helps a person write and revise pointed nonfiction.

## How to read this catalog

- **Grouping.** Techniques are grouped by the stage of writing where they are used. Stage 0 holds the governing principles that justify everything else. Stages 1 to 8 follow the order a piece moves through: thinking and drafting, planning for readers, structure, paragraphs, sentences, compression and tone, feedback, and genre modules.
- **Merging.** Where he taught the same thing more than once, the versions are merged into the fullest one and every video is listed under Sources, earliest first. Where later videos refine or reverse earlier ones, the entry says so.
- **Fields.** Each entry has: what it is, why it works (his reasoning), steps, symptoms in a draft, when not to use it, an example, and sources.
- **Terms.** Names in **bold** marked *(his term)* are labels he uses himself. Other entry names are descriptive labels added for this catalog.
- **Inference.** "(inferred)" marks a limit, step or symptom worked out from his reasoning where he did not state it.
- **Examples.** Every example is original, written in the spirit of his teaching. None is copied from his videos or his model passages.
- **Quotes.** Kept under 15 words and rare. Almost everything is paraphrase.
- **Citations.** "V27 18:19" means video 27 at 18 minutes 19 seconds. A range such as "V19 21:46–25:11" covers a stretch of the video. V-numbers follow the Source index in `writer-science-system.md` (V1 = 2024-02-29, V50 = 2026-09-13).
- **Scope.** The system is for **pointed** writing: text that answers a question or solves a problem for a reader (V50 0:48). Fiction, poetry and memoir craft appear only where he teaches it (V13, V21, V43) and sit in Stage 8.

# Stage 0. Governing principles

These are the tests every other technique answers to. A skill should apply them before recommending any specific fix.

### 0.1 The reader decides
- **What:** Good writing is not a property of the text. Meaning, clarity and coherence arise in what a specific reader does with the text. A sentence can't be judged good or bad without knowing who reads it and what they need.
- **Why it works:** Language is social, even though writing is done alone (V24 1:40–3:01). Words said to a grieving friend can be accurate and still wrong for the moment. Coherence emerges from the relation of writer, reader and text; his own master's thesis, clear to him when he wrote it, is partly opaque to him ten years later because the reader changed (V49 7:34–10:14). Ideas exist only once they reach a reader's mind (V42 22:14–23:06). The reader decides what good writing is (V22 3:19). Meaning is what readers do with a text: use, apply, reject (V33 8:53).
- **Steps:**
  1. Before judging any passage, name its reader and what they need from it.
  2. When two readers would judge a choice differently, write for the one the piece must reach.
  3. Distrust any fix offered "in general". Ask what it does for this reader.
- **Symptoms:** A draft revised without any reader in view. Advice or tool edits accepted because they are "correct". The writer answers "who is this for?" with "everyone".
- **When not to use:** Private thinking drafts, where the reader is deliberately absent (see 1.1).
- **Example:** An internal onboarding page that the 2022 team found perfectly clear baffles a 2026 hire. Nothing in the text changed; its reader did.
- **Sources:** V2 0:21–1:11; V22 3:19; V24 1:40–3:22; V25 2:24; V33 8:53; V42 22:14–23:06; V49 7:34–10:14.

### 0.2 Tools, not rules
- **What:** Every writing element (the passive, jargon, repetition, long sentences, "I", "you", nominalizations, em dashes, fragments) is a tool, chosen for its effect on a particular reader. There are no universal rules because there are no universal readers.
- **Why it works:** Language changes, so rulebooks can't settle quality, but reader psychology can (V2 0:21–1:11). Many school rules exist to make grading defensible, not to help readers (V31 10:11–14:34). Rules give a feeling of certainty and are easy to automate, which is why grammar checkers and AI enforce them (V33 8:08). The deeper danger is that rules train writers to stop thinking about specific readers at all (V33 12:31–14:11). Style authorities often break their own rules in their best work (V14 10:59–12:35).
- **Steps:**
  1. When a rule is invoked, ask where it came from and what it protects the reader from (V2 19:54).
  2. Check whether good writers, including the rule's own advocates, break it. If they do routinely, treat it as a conditional tool (V14 10:59–12:35).
  3. Before dropping a device to avoid criticism (for example, em dashes because they "look like AI"), compare the visible risk with the invisible cost to the reader. He likens avoidance to switching off your headlights to dodge a ticket for a faulty one (V34 1:42–2:31).
  4. Keep learning the grammar. Rejecting rules as laws is not permission to ignore how the tools work (V31 14:34; V36 12:09).
  5. Judge by the one master test: does this choice move this reader from where they are to where you want them? (V42 23:06)
- **Symptoms:** Edits justified only by "that's the rule". Contorted sentences that dodge a starting "But" or a split infinitive. Style-checker suggestions accepted without asking what the sentence is about. A writer asking "will this look AI-written?" instead of "does this help the reader?"
- **When not to use:** If rules are already producing successful writing for someone, he declines to meddle (V33 8:08). House style you are required to follow still applies (V2 15:33).
- **Example:** A reviewer flags "Late fees are waived for members" as passive. In a members' newsletter the members' benefit is the topic, so the passive stays.
- **Sources:** V2 0:21–1:11, 19:54–20:42; V6 11:07–12:04; V8 11:53; V9 6:01; V10 4:40–6:56; V12 0:41, 7:30; V14 7:04, 10:59–12:35; V15 1:51, 15:15; V25 11:00; V27 4:54, 24:06; V31 10:11–14:34; V33 8:08–14:11; V34 1:42–2:31, 5:35; V42 0:00, 23:06; V46 0:17; V48 0:00.

### 0.3 Value before clarity
- **What:** Readers read only when they perceive value, and value means solving a problem they already care about: helping them decide, act, or understand something they want to understand. Work on clarity, organization or polish only once the value is settled.
- **Why it works:** Clear, organized, persuasive and useless is still useless (V12 35:29–36:13; V19 9:25; V42 21:23–21:48). Readers owe the writer nothing; school hides this because teachers are paid to read (V18 7:47–8:24; V31 1:24). Value that readers can't perceive is, in practice, no value (V18 9:14). Novelty, effort, "true" and "interesting to me" are not value (V27 26:10–26:33; V28 1:59–4:20; V19 1:06:47). Motivated readers even judge prose clearer (V27 27:52). Clarity is only half of expert writing; the other half is having something valuable to say (V30 15:41).
- **Steps:**
  1. Before revising sentences, state what the reader will be able to do or understand differently afterward (V19 9:44–10:39).
  2. If you can't, stop polishing and go back to the problem (2.9) or to thinking (Stage 1).
  3. Judge every later element by whether it serves that value (3.29).
- **Symptoms:** Polishing a piece whose reader and payoff can't be named. A client worried about being "clear and organized" but unable to say why anyone would read it (V19 8:34). Writing sold on effort or novelty.
- **When not to use:** Entertainment fiction sits outside this exchange (V31 3:35). Private freewriting has no value test at all (1.9).
- **Example:** A beautifully clear history of time-blocking, written for readers whose actual problem is that meetings eat every afternoon. Clear, and no use to them.
- **Sources:** V11 15:32–15:50; V12 35:03–37:20; V18 7:47–9:52; V19 8:34–10:39; V27 25:12–27:52; V28 1:15–4:20; V30 15:41; V31 3:35–4:34, 22:21; V38 0:58–1:36; V42 21:23–21:48.

### 0.4 Revise top-down, one tool at a time
- **What:** Fix problems in order of scale: reader, point, problem, structure and key terms, subjects and flow, connections, and only then mechanics. Work on one tool at a time rather than applying everything at once.
- **Why it works:** Purpose and structure problems can't be fixed at the sentence level (V23 23:19–24:09; V47 17:30). Most professional writing fails because it serves the writer, not because of grammar (V24 21:58–22:56). He warns that trying many tools at once causes overwhelm (V12 51:02) and that applying all 27 of his steps at once will freeze you (V19 1:14:43). Pick one practice for a week, then add another (V23 40:48).
- **Steps:**
  1. Establish the reader and value (Stages 0 and 2).
  2. Fix the point, introduction and section order (Stage 3).
  3. Fix paragraphs, then sentences (Stages 4 and 5).
  4. Compress and calibrate (Stage 6), then mechanics (6.20).
  5. In each pass, run one diagnostic, not all of them.
- **Symptoms:** Line edits on a draft whose argument is unsettled (V38 9:31). A writer overwhelmed by a long list of fixes.
- **When not to use:** Private drafts, which get no revision pass until the writer switches to the reader phase.
- **Example:** A grant draft gets three passes: first, move the aims to the end of the first paragraph; second, make each section open with its point; only third, cut filler.
- **Sources:** V12 51:02; V19 1:14:43; V23 23:19–24:09, 40:48; V24 22:56; V38 9:31–10:24; V47 17:30; V50 35:11.

### 0.5 The AI line
- **What:** His position on AI hardened over the corpus. Final form: no AI in communicative writing at any stage, including "cleaning up" your own notes or voice memo. Acceptable uses are navigating your own notes (read-only index, maps, "have I thought about this before?"), private logistics, transcription, and finding a precise word.
- **Why it works:** AI produces language, not thought (V1 2:25, 10:40). Writers who draft with it start to sound the same (V19 0:23). Research through AI tends to confirm whatever you asked for (V19 40:04). Style is not a coat of paint over substance: AI sees only the words you used to point at a thought, never the thought, so its version is fluent but subtly distorted (V45 7:52–14:53). Coherence is relational, so no master prompt can produce it (V49 7:34, 9:16). If you spend the effort to give AI full context about your reader, you are already communicating, with the wrong party (V45 14:53–16:23).
- **Steps (what this means for a writing skill, inferred):**
  1. Diagnose, question and locate problems; don't generate the writer's ideas.
  2. When showing a rewrite, keep it as an illustration of a move, built from the writer's own material, and hand the rewrite back to the writer.
  3. Help the writer navigate their own notes and drafts: retrieve, map, flag gaps.
  4. Never polish a thinking draft into "finished" prose on the writer's behalf.
- **Symptoms:** Smooth prose the writer couldn't elaborate on if a reader asked "why?" (V45 9:06–11:41). Drafts that began from "give me three points on X". Metaphors with the right form that feel slightly off (V36 6:22–6:48).
- **When not to use:** His earlier, softer allowances (AI clean-up of a transcript in V20 7:40; turning a voice note into bullets for private use in V44 22:48) are superseded for reader-facing prose by V45. AI for matching people and drafting logistics appears in V44 11:19–14:31.
- **Example:** A writer asks for help with a stalled essay. The skill asks what they have seen to be true, points them to the paragraph where the claim actually appears, and shows one reordered sentence as a model. It doesn't write the section.
- **Sources:** V1 1:07–2:25, 10:40; V14 7:44, 9:31; V15 0:00; V16 13:10; V19 0:23, 40:04; V20 7:40, 24:58; V23 42:48; V27 0:00; V33 13:04; V34 12:17; V35 8:11; V36 5:02, 19:52–21:12; V40 15:54–23:23; V44 11:19–14:31, 22:48; V45 0:21–18:09; V49 7:34, 9:16; V50 0:00, 34:15.

# Stage 1. Thinking and drafting

The writer's draft is for the writer. Its job is to find out what you think. None of the reader-facing checks in Stages 3 to 6 apply here, and he is explicit that bringing them in too early causes block (V20 5:22; V33 14:11; V47 21:09).

## 1A. Generating the writer's draft

### 1.1 Separate the writer's draft from the reader's draft
*(his terms: door closed / door open; writer's draft and reader's draft; the artist and the architect; two functions of writing)*
- **What:** Writing has two jobs that pull against each other: thinking (for you) and communicating (for them). Do them in separate phases. Draft with the door closed, as the only audience, following your thinking wherever it goes. Then, with the door open, build a reader's version organized around what you found.
- **Why it works:**
  - Editing while producing is like having an editor behind you criticizing each word before the sentence is finished (V11 2:03–2:24). From his language teaching: learners improve through confident use, not constant correction (V11 1:05–1:36).
  - Trying to think and write for readers at once is torture; you do both badly (V19 21:46, 24:00).
  - Reader awareness arriving too early is what freezes experts (V20 1:05–5:22).
  - Bringing readers into the room too early bends your opinions toward whoever you imagine reading (V38 18:28).
  - The patterns you use to generate text interfere with the patterns readers use to read it (V47 0:00–3:55), so one document can't serve both.
- **Steps:**
  1. Before each session, decide which mode it is: producing or perfecting (V11 5:13).
  2. In the artist phase, fix nothing, write for no one, follow hunches (V42 16:31–18:15).
  3. Stop when it makes sense to you and you feel you have the pieces (V20 7:40–9:12; V47 12:03).
  4. Switch explicitly to the architect phase: reverse outline (2.1), find the through lines (2.2), then build the reader draft (2.4).
  5. Diagnose which phase is missing from a published piece: nothing new in it means phase 1 was skipped (audience capture); nobody reads it means phase 2 was skipped (V38 19:34).
- **Symptoms:** Progress one sentence at a time; rereading the opening again and again; deleting almost as much as you write (V11 2:24). Inner commentary ("that's not good") or reaching for backspace, which means other people's judgment has crept in (V42 17:29). Or the reverse: a published piece that is still a thinking record.
- **When not to use:** He qualifies the separation in one direction: rereading and restructuring *during* drafting is legitimate (1.13); what he rejects is revision as a separate clean-up of a finished text (V23 36:18). Short routine writing (emails, reports) may need no thinking draft (V23 1:47; V41 13:46).
- **Example:** An analyst spends ninety minutes polishing the first two paragraphs of a board memo and never reaches the recommendation. Next time: twenty-five minutes drafting the whole memo badly, then a separate session, next morning, building the memo for the board.
- **Sources:** V11 1:05–5:13; V12 44:07–45:38; V19 21:25–29:54; V20 1:05–9:33; V23 35:22–36:18; V31 7:55–10:11; V33 14:11; V35 0:50–6:40; V38 16:38–21:34; V42 14:20–20:01; V47 0:00–3:55, 20:14–21:09.

### 1.2 Write to think
*(his phrases: writing is thinking; write first, understand later; the page as a laboratory)*
- **What:** Use drafting as the method of working out what you think, not as the record of a finished thought. Expect to start believing X and finish somewhere better, Y.
- **Why it works:**
  - Anything worth writing involves thoughts you have never put into words, so they come out half-formed at first (V11 6:14–7:51).
  - Expert knowledge is too tangled to sort in your head. His images: tangled Christmas lights you untangle only by handling them, and a cognitive map like a plate of spaghetti (V19 22:35–24:00; V35 6:15; V47 0:59).
  - Writing is how you become an expert, not a report of having become one (V17 2:43–4:07).
  - If you already know exactly what you will say, you are reshuffling known material, which AI can replace (V35 8:11).
  - Waiting until you feel ready is a trap, because writing is what makes you ready (V35 3:56).
- **Steps:**
  1. Start writing about the problem directly, messily, before you have an outline or a thesis.
  2. When doubt appears, keep writing, now about the doubt (V11 6:14–6:54).
  3. When a new idea clicks, follow it.
  4. Accept ending on a different claim from the one you began with.
  5. Postpone all concern for style until ideas exist (V35 4:54).
- **Symptoms:** Procrastinating until "I know what I want to say"; an outline that can't be filled; a draft that stays loyal to its opening thesis; bland work that restates known ideas; asking for style feedback on material with no idea yet (V35 3:56).
- **When not to use:** He later softens the slogan. By 2026 he entertains the view that some thinking happens off the page (in the shower, asleep) and that writing is where it becomes visible (V40 5:53–12:17; V42 15:45–16:02). The practice doesn't change; treat writing as the best tool for thinking, not the only place it happens (see 1.18).
- **Example:** Start: "Code reviews slow teams down." Midway: "Actually, the slow reviews were all on huge pull requests." End: "Review speed is set by the size of the change, not by the review process."
- **Sources:** V3 11:39–12:11; V7 28:11 (clear writing follows clear thinking); V11 5:30–8:33; V17 2:43–4:07; V19 21:46–25:11; V20 4:04–6:12; V35 0:50–8:11; V38 9:31–10:24, 21:34; V40 5:53–12:17; V42 15:45–16:02; V47 0:59, 16:21.

### 1.3 Start from a question, not a topic
*(his phrases: question, not topic; the question is your filter; writing in reverse)*
- **What:** Begin with a question you can't yet answer, not with a topic (a noun) or a thesis. Starting from a conclusion and hunting for support is **writing in reverse** *(his term)*.
- **Why it works:** A topic gives you a pile of things to say; a question gives you a reason to say them (V39 2:47). The question holds the conclusion in suspension, so you can discover rather than decorate (V38 3:23). It filters what is relevant from a flood of material (V38 5:06–5:42). Evidence recruited for a thesis you already hold becomes ornament, a square peg in a round hole (V17 7:38; V38 2:33).
- **Steps:**
  1. Write the question at the top of the page.
  2. Say, or write, "I don't know. What evidence would I need to answer this?" (V19 3:19–4:05; V38 3:23–5:42)
  3. List the evidence you would need.
  4. Notice whether you have already picked a side. In class he asks students to walk to opposite sides of the room on a debate question, then points out they chose before thinking (V38 4:17–4:39). If you have, put it back in suspension.
  5. For each quote, story or statistic, ask: was this found while answering the question, or recruited to prop up a view?
- **Symptoms:** A thesis written before any exploration; evidence that only ever agrees with you; a working title that is a noun; quotes that feel like decoration; material kept because it is interesting, not because it answers anything.
- **When not to use:** Fast, opinionated thinkers resist this; he treats that as a reason to use it, not an exception (V38 5:42–6:40).
- **Example:** Topic: "remote work". Writing in reverse: "Remote work boosts productivity", then a search for supporting studies. Question: "Why did our most productive team ask to come back to the office?"
- **Sources:** V17 6:42–7:38; V19 3:19–4:05; V20 6:12–7:40; V35 6:40; V38 2:14–5:42; V39 0:32–3:20; V42 17:29.

### 1.4 Ask "why?" until you reach a real question
- **What:** To find a question worth a whole piece, ask "why?" about a frustrating experience, repeatedly, until you reach a systemic question.
- **Why it works:** Pressing questions that everyone half-asks and nobody answers are a sign you are onto something valuable (V17 3:34). You need no credentials to start, only questions (V17 4:46).
- **Steps:** 1. Take a bad day or recurring frustration. 2. Ask why it happened. 3. Ask why of the answer, about five times. 4. Stop when the answer is a question about how a whole system works.
- **Symptoms:** Waiting for authority before starting; a project that begins from a thesis; no question you actually care about.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** "The client meeting went badly." Why? "I didn't have the numbers." Why? "The dashboard was stale." Why? "Nobody owns it." Why? "Why does every team assume data is somebody else's job?"
- **Sources:** V17 3:34–6:04.

### 1.5 The inquiry method: evidence plus reasoning equals claim
*(his term: the inquiry method)*
- **What:** A way to reach principled conclusions on questions of value, which experiments can't settle. Question, then evidence the question calls for (described neutrally, since evidence has no values built in), then your own reasoning about what it means. The claim is what follows from evidence plus reasoning. Let it emerge rather than starting from it.
- **Why it works:** People are biased; this does for judgment questions what the scientific method does for empirical ones (V38 6:40). It makes the idea open to challenge: a critic must say whether they dispute the evidence or the reasoning, and both are on the page (V38 8:29–9:12). Starting from the story or evidence avoids manufactured examples (V17 6:42–7:38).
- **Steps:**
  1. State the question.
  2. Read with the question in mind and gather what it calls for.
  3. Explain each piece of evidence to yourself in writing.
  4. Synthesize into a framework that makes sense to you.
  5. Write the claim as what follows from 3 and 4.
  6. Test it: can you point to the exact evidence or reasoning step a critic would have to reject?
  7. Only after this, refine style (V38 9:31–10:24).
- **Symptoms:** Claims with no visible reasoning; evidence described in loaded language; a story that feels manufactured to fit a point; disagreements you can't pin down.
- **When not to use:** Purely empirical questions call for empirical methods (inferred from V38 7:24).
- **Example:** Evidence: after going remote, one team's ticket closures fell a fifth while other teams held steady. Reasoning: that team's work runs on quick, unplanned conversations. Claim: remote work hurts teams whose work runs on interruptions, not teams in general.
- **Sources:** V17 6:42–7:38; V38 6:40–10:43. (Presentation of the same anatomy for readers: 3.24.)

### 1.6 The timed, question-tethered session
*(his terms: non-editing; brain dump; the tether; rip a solo)*
- **What:** A short, timed burst of continuous writing with no deleting, usually with the question you're trying to answer written at the top of the page.
- **Why it works:** A small target makes starting easy: 250 words in 15 minutes is about a sentence a minute (V11 3:01). The timer marks the mode, so the inner editor has no license to speak. The question keeps your "centre of gravity" on a problem readers will value, where morning pages would let you drift into a dream log (V20 7:00–7:40). Improvising within a time box keeps writing alive, like a guitarist who knows the instrument and doesn't pre-plan the solo (V12 44:07–45:38).
- **Steps:**
  1. Write the question at the top of the page (V20 6:12; V35 6:40; V42 17:29).
  2. Set a timer: 10 to 20 minutes.
  3. Type without deleting. Stuck on a word? Type the first one that comes plus a bracketed note like [better word] and move on (V11 3:32).
  4. Refuse the voice that says you need more research, tea, or a clean kitchen first (V11 3:32–3:49).
  5. Remove distractions: a plain-text editor outside the browser, a site blocker (V11 3:49–4:27).
  6. When you drift, glance back at the question.
  7. Repeat daily.
- **Symptoms:** Reaching for backspace; switching tabs mid-sentence to check a fact; long pauses hunting for the perfect word.
- **When not to use:** What it produces is a mess you could never share as it is (V11 12:01). It feeds the writer's draft, not the reader's.
- **Example:** At the top: "Why do clients stop replying after the second proposal?" Then twenty minutes: "Maybe the second proposal is too long... [check: page count?] ... or they've already chosen someone and don't want to say..."
- **Sources:** V11 3:01–4:27; V12 44:07–45:38; V20 6:12–7:40; V35 6:40–7:25; V42 16:31–17:29.

### 1.7 Keep-moving moves inside a session
- **What:** Four small moves for when drafting stalls.
  - **Address yourself as "you"** ("You think the pricing page is the problem, but you've only watched three users") to observe your thinking without being attached to it. He picked this up from Krishnamurti's diaries (V11 8:33).
  - **When a sentence feels wrong, write about why** on the next line, instead of rewording it again and again (V11 8:33–9:14).
  - **Start with a placeholder opener:** "the thing is", "what I'm trying to say is" (V11 9:14).
  - **Imagine an audience and a deadline:** a specific room of listeners and 30 minutes to get the point across (V11 9:14–10:00).
- **Why it works:** Anything that gets you started is good enough; openings are revisable. Writing about the discomfort breaks the barrier between your real ideas and your "good" ideas (V11 9:14). An imagined audience brings the clarity he gets walking into a seminar room after a night of worrying over a lesson plan (V11 9:14–10:00).
- **Steps:** Use whichever move fits the stall; keep the timer running.
- **Symptoms:** Freezing on one sentence; several abandoned first lines; defensive drafting.
- **When not to use:** Delete these crutches in the reader's draft (inferred). The "you" address is a private thinking device, not a published style (inferred; contrast 6.14).
- **Example:** "Pricing should be simple. That feels wrong because simple for whom? The buyer wants one number. Finance wants every line item. So maybe the real point is..."
- **Sources:** V11 8:33–10:00.

### 1.8 Alternate working with words and working with ideas
*(his terms: working with words; working with ideas; fermentation)*
- **What:** A two-mode loop. Free generation of words, then zooming out to name the ideas that surfaced, then generating again from one of them. Ideas brought together on the page interact and grow into something richer, like a ferment rather than a salad.
- **Why it works:** Your best ideas come from interactions between ideas, not from lightning strikes (V11 13:47). Ideas that seemed too simple become metaphors for others; ideas that seemed clichéd become lenses (V11 13:30–13:47). It breaks the cycle of either writing passionately or passively waiting for inspiration (V11 12:01).
- **Steps:**
  1. Words: a 10-minute timer, write about anything.
  2. Zoom out: read it and write a one-sentence summary of each idea that stands out.
  3. Ideas: pick one summary, set a 10 to 15 minute timer, and write with that idea in view.
  4. Repeat, harvesting summaries each time. Over time the body of work "takes on a life of its own" (V11 15:32).
- **Symptoms:** A folder of unrelated fragments that never connect; not knowing what to write about.
- **When not to use:** It generates material. Readers still need it shaped into a solution to their problem (V11 15:32–15:50).
- **Example:** Two summaries from separate sessions, "recipe cards leave out quantities" and "our runbooks fail when on-call engineers face something new", ferment into "Good runbooks, like good recipe cards, leave room for judgment."
- **Sources:** V11 11:16–15:50. (Related note-linking practice: 1.24.)

### 1.9 Daily goal-free freewriting (the magma vent)
*(his terms: open a vent; magma; shine; energy)*
- **What:** Every day, write whatever comes, with no filter and no plan. It is not newsletter material, not the first phase of an essay, not even a first draft. Its only purpose is to let pressure out.
- **Why it works:** He pictures the unconscious as shapeless, pressurized magma. A small daily vent lets that energy refresh the images and ideas you think with (V41 22:53–24:59). You can't sculpt magma while it flows: generating and judging at once blocks the flow (V41 27:53). After it cools, the analytical mind can find the bits worth shaping.
- **Steps:**
  1. Show up daily and write without filtering.
  2. Expect nothing meaningful; it is not for anybody, including you (V41 25:56).
  3. Don't use good, bad, better or worse; those only make sense against a goal (V41 24:03).
  4. Let it cool for hours or days (V41 27:01).
  5. Reread with the analytical mind. Expect roughly 90% dross (V41 31:10). Look for bits with **shine** or energy.
  6. Shape those bits into something useful or beautiful. This is where craft lives.
  7. Keep scraps where they can resurface later, for example with a random-note feature (V41 27:53).
- **Symptoms of doing it wrong:** Judging the freewrite's quality; trying to turn today's pages into a post; starting from a planned point.
- **When not to use:** It doesn't replace a purposeful draft (V41 22:53), and effort can't make what comes up better (V41 25:56). It raises the odds of the good mode, never guarantees it.
- **Example:** A week later, one line in a rambling page about hospital parking has shine: "resilience" as the name for a cost someone else avoided paying. The rest stays in the drawer.
- **Sources:** V41 22:17–31:10. (Distinct from the question-tethered session in 1.6, which aims at a piece.)

### 1.10 Talk it out
- **What:** When typing stalls, speak the idea aloud, as if to a listener, into voice-to-text software. Work from the transcript as raw material.
- **Why it works:** Speech keeps the stream going; ideas often come out when you explain them to a friend (V20 5:22). He cites a Tim Ferriss podcast guest who dictated all his output (V11 10:00–10:41).
- **Steps:** 1. Set a timer. 2. Record yourself explaining the idea to an imagined friend. 3. Transcribe. 4. Treat the transcript as writer's-draft material.
- **Symptoms:** You can explain the idea to a colleague easily but can't type it.
- **When not to use:** Don't hand the transcript to AI to "clean up" into prose for readers; by V45 he rejects that workflow (0.5). Transcription itself is fine (V40 2:45).
- **Example:** A surgeon records twelve minutes explaining a new consent process "to a resident" while walking between buildings, then drafts from the transcript.
- **Sources:** V11 10:00–10:41; V20 5:22, 7:40; V40 2:45–3:21; V45 7:52–8:47.

### 1.11 Treat the draft as a lab and a play space
*(his terms: excavation site; science lab; whimsy)*
- **What:** Approach early drafts as a place to dig, combine ideas and see what reacts, and to follow a loose thread with no fixed destination.
- **Why it works:** Most combinations are duds, but odd combinations followed with curiosity are where inspiration comes from (V35 7:25). Play only happens where you feel safe; if the page feels like a place where you are judged, you perform instead of discovering (V36 22:01–22:59). Painters and potters arrive at the studio without a fixed plan and let the image emerge (V20 9:12–9:33; V11 11:16).
- **Steps:**
  1. Start from something you are curious about, not a destination.
  2. Bring two ideas or sources into contact and write about how they relate.
  3. Frame the session as "let's see where this goes".
  4. Keep what reacts.
- **Symptoms:** Anxiety before starting; every paragraph marching toward a conclusion fixed in advance; nothing in the finished piece the writer didn't know at the start.
- **When not to use:** A thinking-phase stance only; the reader draft needs deliberate structure (inferred from V36 25:12). He admits he rarely manages to lose himself this way (V36 22:59).
- **Example:** Instead of "today I write the section on why onboarding fails", open with "Odd: our best hires got the worst onboarding. Why?" and follow it.
- **Sources:** V7 0:00–0:49, 39:21 (connections from disorder); V11 11:16–12:01; V20 9:12–9:33; V35 6:40–8:11; V36 19:29–25:12.

### 1.12 Spend your best idea where it appears
- **What:** If a great ending or insight turns up mid-draft, write it down there instead of saving it for the finish. Don't draft toward a conclusion you already know.
- **Why it works:** Writing when you already know the conclusion involves no discovery; the idea is dead on arrival. Spending the good idea forces you to find something better (V23 32:41–34:19).
- **Steps:** Write the idea when it arrives; keep going past it.
- **Symptoms:** A drafted ending that everything is steering toward; ideas hoarded "for later".
- **When not to use:** He calls it perhaps a personal preference (V23 32:41). It governs drafting only; in the reader draft, the point goes where readers expect it (3.3).
- **Example:** Halfway through a draft on meetings you realize "meetings are where blame goes to be shared". Write it now, then ask what that implies.
- **Sources:** V23 32:41–34:19.

### 1.13 Reread as you go: revision is writing
- **What:** Pause after each section or paragraph, reread it, notice what you have actually done, and let the emerging shape tell you where to go next. Revision creates the text as much as first sentences do.
- **Why it works:** School teaches revision as fixing marked errors on a finished text. Real revision is where you discover what you are trying to say (V23 36:18–38:09). His uncensored drafts hold several ideas, some connected and some orphaned; building around the strongest ones shows where the next section must go.
- **Steps:**
  1. After each section, reread.
  2. Map the shape of the whole so far.
  3. Hunt for the strongest thought.
  4. Build the structure around it; cut or save weaker ideas.
- **Symptoms:** Treating revision as grammar clean-up; refusing to reread until a full draft exists.
- **When not to use:** This rereading is structural and exploratory, not line editing or pruning (see 6.1).
- **Example:** After three paragraphs on why meetings fail, you notice the orphan line "people bring status, not questions". It is the real idea; the next sections reorganize around it.
- **Sources:** V23 36:18–38:09. (Tension with 1.1 resolved in Appendix C.)

### 1.14 Reading is food, not fuel
*(formulation from his subscriber Alexi, which he endorses)*
- **What:** Don't read everything before writing, and don't write with no reading. Read when the writing gets hungry: when you hit a specific gap. Let the question guide the reading. Reading done this way counts as writing time.
- **Why it works:** Reading everything first is like cramming: satisfying, unproductive (V35 1:43–2:28). Writing without reading leaves you starving mid-draft. Reading aimed at a named gap solves it (V35 3:15). Browsing and notes can be moments of discovery (V23 38:09–39:56), but reading is also a classic form of avoidance.
- **Steps:**
  1. Start writing with what you know.
  2. When stuck, name the specific gap.
  3. Read to fill that gap, guided by your question.
  4. Return to writing.
  5. Test: am I reading to understand something the piece needs, or to avoid the blank page? (V23 38:59)
- **Symptoms:** A reading list that grows while the draft doesn't; a draft that runs out of material halfway.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Midway through a piece on readmissions you realize you can't say whether rates differ by discharge day. You find two studies on exactly that and go back to writing.
- **Sources:** V23 38:09–39:56; V35 0:50–3:38.

## 1B. When the writing won't come

His account of block moves outward over time: from fear (2025), to hardened self-image (May 2026), to having no one you respect to write to (July 2026). He never withdraws the earlier accounts; they sit together, and all of them reject willpower as the fix.

### 1.15 Diagnose the block before treating it
*(his terms: the expert's curse; resistance renamed as fear; block as a signal)*
- **What:** Block is information, not a character flaw. Identify which kind it is before choosing a remedy.
  - **Fear:** it appears only around writing that matters. Nobody fears emails (V23 0:00–2:36).
  - **Reader too early (the expert's curse):** the more you know, the more every sentence seems inadequate, because you are imagining a demanding reader you respect. That pressure is a good sign; you are just in the reader phase too soon (V20 1:05–5:22).
  - **An outgrown project:** the project was chosen by a past version of you, and the unconscious has moved on. He describes the self in Lacan's terms: a coherent story you tell yourself and a social mask, both of which can harden and seal off creative energy (V41 0:00–12:09). His image: the conscious rider can suggest, but the elephant goes where it wants (V41 14:41–16:41).
  - **No one to talk to:** you never have block when talking to someone you respect and whose respect you want (V44 30:39–32:40).
  - **Persistent resistance:** if every method fails, stop and listen to the part of you that resists; it may know something (V11 10:41–11:16).
- **Why it works:** Each cause has a different fix. Forcing (word counts, stricter rules) makes the outgrown-project kind worse and breeds resentment (V41 1:13).
- **Steps:**
  1. Is the piece one that matters? If so, expect fear and use 1.16.
  2. Are you imagining judges while drafting? Switch back to the writer's draft (1.1).
  3. Are you logging hours with nothing to show and resenting the sessions? Ask who chose this project and when. Consider putting it physically away and watching what arrives (V41 1:13–3:07).
  4. Do you know who you are writing to? If not, find early readers (7.4).
  5. Has the resistance survived every method? Walk, journal, ask what it is telling you.
- **Symptoms (in the writer):** Starting and deleting; ideas that flow in conversation but not on the page (V20 1:05); ever-stricter rules; ideas for a different project that keep interrupting.
- **When not to use:** Refuse a momentary urge to escape during a timed session (1.6); investigate only resistance that persists (V11 3:32 vs 10:41). Forcing functions are fine for routine writing like reports and emails (V41 13:46).
- **Example:** A consultant has promised herself "the definitive leadership book" for three years and writes nothing, while her notes fill with observations about how juniors learn by overhearing partners' calls. The unconscious has already chosen the second project.
- **Sources:** V11 10:41–11:16; V20 1:05–5:22; V23 0:00–2:36; V41 0:00–16:41; V44 0:00, 30:39–32:40.

### 1.16 Fear as a metal detector
- **What:** When stuck, ask: What am I most afraid of here? What scares me about this paragraph? Why am I stuck? Then write about that fear and follow it.
- **Why it works:** Following Pema Chödrön, he treats fear as a sign you are getting closer to the truth: the beep of a beachcomber's detector (V23 30:00–32:41). Writing that produces new knowledge is always frightening; fearless writing is complacent (V23 30:53–31:54).
- **Steps:** 1. In a freewrite, answer the three questions honestly. 2. Follow the thread. 3. Expect the avoided paragraph to hold the most interesting problem in the piece.
- **Symptoms:** A section you keep skipping; rewriting an opening six times.
- **When not to use:** Don't try to eliminate the fear; the aim is to write with it (V23 30:00).
- **Example:** "I'm afraid this paragraph admits the method I've taught for five years failed with my best client." That admission is probably the heart of the essay.
- **Sources:** V23 30:00–32:41.

### 1.17 Stuck mid-session: the new-document freewrite
- **What:** When you want to get up and escape, stay five to ten more minutes, open a new document, and type without stopping, starting from "I'm stuck because I can't work out how to..."
- **Why it works:** The rambling usually drifts into focused writing and leads back into the project. He reports it works about nine times in ten, and uses the same drill to build fluency in language learners (V23 19:52–22:22).
- **Steps:** 1. New document. 2. Real sentences, no keyboard-mashing; repeat yourself if you must. 3. Don't stop for five to ten minutes. 4. Carry any usable line back.
- **Symptoms:** Your legs are lifting you out of the chair; you reach for a snack.
- **When not to use:** If you have lost faith in the whole project rather than one passage, reread and reverse outline instead (2.1).
- **Example:** "I'm stuck because I don't know whether section 3 comes before the case study... actually the case study *is* the argument, so section 3 is only its setup..."
- **Sources:** V23 19:52–22:22.

### 1.18 The night shift
*(he says scientists and mathematicians use the term)*
- **What:** Work a problem consciously until progress stops, then hand it over: sleep, walk, rest. Treat odd images, hunches and nagging itches that come back as reports from the unconscious, and write them down.
- **Why it works:** The unconscious keeps working while you rest and reports in images rather than sentences, because it is far older than language (V41 16:41–20:09). A musician who gets 15% into a new piece on day one often starts day two further on. He also keeps a dream log as a source of original images (V40 3:00–4:00).
- **Steps:** 1. Grind until you stall, then stop. 2. Rest. 3. Capture recurring images and hunches. 4. Expect something that needs translating, not a finished sentence.
- **Symptoms:** Grinding at the desk long after progress has stopped; dismissing odd hunches as noise.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** A policy analyst stops at 4 p.m., unable to frame a report on building permits. Next morning a toll booth keeps nagging her. The frame: every permit charges a toll in time.
- **Sources:** V40 3:00–4:00; V41 16:41–20:09; V42 16:02.

### 1.19 Catch virtuous procrastination
*(his term)*
- **What:** Notice avoidance that feels like diligence: editing during drafting time, extra research, tidying, formatting references, admin, marketing, SEO.
- **Why it works:** These tasks have deadlines and clear outcomes, unlike the uncertain toil of writing, so they feel important; if writing frightens you, cleaning feels like a holiday (V11 5:13–5:30; V23 25:45–27:37).
- **Steps:** 1. During writing time, notice when you have drifted into fixing or looking things up. 2. Name it. 3. Leave a placeholder for what you wanted to check and return to producing. 4. Test any task: is it moving this piece forward or making me feel busy?
- **Symptoms:** A session that ends with a better-formatted document or longer reading list and no new paragraphs.
- **When not to use:** In scheduled editing time, editing is the job. Do a decent job on the business side too; just remember you want to be known for your ideas (V23 26:48).
- **Example:** A researcher "prepares" to write a discussion section by reformatting every reference. The hour is gone before paragraph one.
- **Sources:** V11 3:32–3:49, 5:13–5:30; V23 25:45–27:37, 38:59; V35 3:15.

### 1.20 Break the perfectionism loop
*(his phrase: not your magnum opus, your next work)*
- **What:** The opposite failure to procrastination: endless research and revision so the piece stays in "perfect potential" and is never judged. Set a release date and treat publication as the next lesson.
- **Why it works:** Being evaluated teaches more than another month of polishing (V23 27:37–29:19). Writers fear both that no one will read them and that everyone will judge them; both can't be true, and a quality-control routine answers the second fear (V19 1:05:09–1:05:58).
- **Steps:** 1. Set a release date. 2. Say your excuses aloud to a colleague ("I can't publish until I redesign my site"); aloud they sound absurd (V23 28:26–30:00). 3. Run the quality checks (Stages 3 to 6), then publish.
- **Symptoms:** A finished draft with no date; another extension; another source to read.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** An essay that has been "nearly done" for six weeks gets a Friday date and one last pass on the introduction only.
- **Sources:** V19 1:05:09–1:05:58; V23 27:37–30:00.

### 1.21 A small, forgiving habit (and its limits)
- **What:** In 2025 he recommends a structure that keeps you writing despite fear: design the person who could finish the work, protect a small daily habit for about two months, aim low, and forgive misses. In 2026 he rejects discipline as the frame for creative work and puts goal-free freewriting and early readers in its place.
- **Why it works:** Conditional rules ("when nothing more important comes up") always fail, because something always comes up (V23 3:26–5:13). Meeting small goals builds a loop of motivation; unmet big goals don't build habits (V23 13:37–16:20). But forcing the unconscious makes creative block worse (V41 0:00–1:13, 13:10–13:46).
- **Steps (the 2025 container):**
  1. Write down the habits of the person who could finish this piece (after Sheila Heti) (V23 5:13–6:00).
  2. Protect the pattern for about eight weeks, like a seedling (V23 6:00–6:52).
  3. Pick a consistent slot; he prefers mornings, when willpower is fullest (V23 6:52–7:38). Thirty minutes is plenty if busy (V23 7:38).
  4. Aim for about two pages, or simply for completing the session. During reserved time you don't have to write, but you can't do anything else; revising counts (V23 10:07–11:49).
  5. Break the target down: a thousand words is four 25-minute sessions of about one sentence a minute (V23 11:49–12:41).
  6. Tell people the habit, not the project (V23 9:17–10:07).
  7. Know when you are done for the day, and write in one place (V23 12:41–13:37).
  8. After a miss: forgive yourself, look forward, never add today's gap to tomorrow, celebrate hits, drop the drill sergeant (V23 16:20–19:52).
- **Symptoms:** Planning 2,000 words a day, missing, and quitting; carried-over debts that spiral; a self-punishing inner voice.
- **When not to use:** He later calls the locked-room rule more myth than practice and warns against brute discipline for a long creative project you chose yourself (V41 13:10–13:46, 21:17). Keep forcing functions for routine writing, and never use self-destructive regimes (V23 13:37, 18:04). Finishers who need a push do better with a deadline and a partner (7.10).
- **Example:** Week one: one paragraph before opening email. Week two: two paragraphs. Missed Wednesday? Thursday is still one paragraph.
- **Sources:** V11 4:27; V23 3:26–19:52, 40:48; V41 0:00–1:13, 13:10–13:46, 21:17.

## 1C. Capturing raw material

### 1.22 Capture raw material continuously
*(his terms: collecting scraps; fleeting notes)*
- **What:** Keep quick, rough notes of whatever moves or provokes you: conversations, reactions to reading, teachable moments from work, dreams, striking names. Capture without titling or judging; process later.
- **Why it works:** Scraps pile up; some stay orphans, others cluster, and the through-lines between them become your own ideas (V12 29:53–31:16). A timestamp ID instead of a title removes the friction that stops capture (V40 6:36–7:02). A weekly log of real stories gives you evidence before claims (V17 6:04–6:42). A striking name can invite a story (V12 40:37–41:39).
- **Steps:**
  1. Keep one inbox for raw sources and one place for your own fleeting notes (V40 1:39–3:21).
  2. After a provoking conversation or line, record a quick voice memo or note with an automatic timestamp.
  3. Keep a weekly log of "teachable moments" from your work (V17 6:04).
  4. Use a linkable store so any note can connect to any other (V12 30:46).
  5. Forget them; retrieve and link them when something new comes up.
  6. Optionally run everything through a daily note (two or three tasks pulled from a weekly review, plus a list of notes touched that day), which over years becomes a record of how your thinking developed (V40 4:00–5:53).
- **Symptoms:** Starting every piece from zero; good conversations lost; thousands of saved highlights and no drafts (V40 0:00).
- **When not to use:** Don't analyze in the sources folder; it is for collecting only (V40 2:06).
- **Example:** "202605031412: The speaker said data beats intuition and I bristled. Why? Our best call last year was a hunch the data confirmed later."
- **Sources:** V12 29:53–31:16, 40:37–41:39; V17 6:04–6:42; V40 0:00–7:26 (daily note 4:00–5:53).

### 1.23 Literature notes as dialogue
- **What:** A note on a source is a record of your conversation with it: the line that set you thinking, and your reply. Not highlights, not a summary. The question running underneath: where is this the same as what I think, and where is it different?
- **Why it works:** Quotes alone have no value; they matter only as far as they make you think (V40 9:00–9:19). The same/different question draws out your own ideas (V40 10:14–10:37; the rule is also his reading rule in 7.7).
- **Steps:** 1. Copy only lines that provoke you. 2. Under each, write your response: how it resembles or differs from what you already think. 3. Don't summarize the whole work.
- **Symptoms:** A highlights file you never reread; summaries with no reaction.
- **When not to use:** Most entries will never be used, and that is fine (V40 10:37).
- **Example:** Quote: "Culture eats strategy." Note: "Same as my view that incentives beat memos. Different: the author treats culture as fixed; I've seen one manager change a team's culture in a quarter."
- **Sources:** V40 7:26–10:37.

### 1.24 Permanent notes that link
- **What:** One self-contained idea per note, in your own words, linked to its source and to at least one existing note, so no idea is orphaned. A note can also be an open question or an idea you disagree with. When a new input echoes an old note, link them and write how they agree and differ.
- **Why it works:** Linking makes ideas talk to each other, and patterns surface on their own (V40 10:37–15:54). The resulting web is messy and associative, which is the point.
- **Steps:** 1. Pick one idea from a fleeting or literature note. 2. State it in your words. 3. Link the source. 4. Link an existing note it speaks to. 5. When two notes echo, write a short comparison note.
- **Symptoms:** Notes that are quotes, not ideas; notes covering several ideas; notes linked to nothing.
- **When not to use:** Not stated. For AI's role here, see 0.5: navigation only.
- **Example:** "Teams adopt tools they helped choose and resist better tools handed to them. Source: interview with ops lead. Links to: ownership predicts adoption."
- **Sources:** V11 13:30–13:47; V12 30:46–31:16; V40 10:37–15:54.

## 1D. Building skill between pieces

### 1.25 Copy from memory (steal style)
*(his thesis adviser's phrase: steal style)*
- **What:** Take a passage by a writer whose effect you admire, read it two or three times, close the book, and write it out from memory. Start with sentences and work up to paragraphs and sections. Then pour your own ideas into its structures.
- **Why it works:** Style is open to everyone; ideas belong to their authors (V12 29:15). There are no secrets in style manuals; you learn from examples (V14 12:35–13:16). Working from memory has more effect than copying with the book open (V14 13:16–13:50). He once typed out a whole McCarthy novel to find out what made it tick (V7 7:00). Translation is a related apprenticeship that adds your own interpretation (V7 13:45).
- **Steps:** 1. Choose a passage. 2. Read it two or three times. 3. Close it and write it from memory. 4. Compare with the original and note what you dropped (inferred). 5. Reuse the structure with your own content.
- **Symptoms it fixes:** You admire a style but can't say what produces it; you know the terminology but lack the skill.
- **When not to use:** It is for style, not ideas (V12 29:15). Prefer models that have lasted (V7 14:36–15:56).
- **Example:** A grant writer memorizes a funded one-paragraph abstract, writes it from memory each morning for a week, and notices she keeps dropping the one-sentence statement of stakes.
- **Sources:** V7 7:00–7:46, 13:45–15:56; V12 27:40–29:53; V14 12:35–13:50.

### 1.26 Sentence bird-watching
- **What:** Keep a running list of sentences (not words) whose shape surprises you, and get curious about how they are built.
- **Why it works:** Meaning lives in the relationships between words; a scrambled sentence is word salad (V10 1:57). Writers become writers by loving their raw material, and that material is the sentence (V10 1:26–2:27).
- **Steps:** 1. Keep a sentence notebook while reading. 2. Copy in any sentence with a striking pattern. 3. Note how it is built and where its weight falls (inferred). 4. Use entries as models for 1.27.
- **Symptoms it fixes:** Vocabulary notebooks with no sense of structure; a narrow range of sentence shapes.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Entry: "The town had two bars, one church, and no secrets." Pattern: concrete counts that turn on an abstract noun at the end.
- **Sources:** V10 1:26–2:27.

### 1.27 Great Sentences Mad Libs
*(his name)*
- **What:** Keep a model sentence's structure and swap words slot by slot, as in the party game. It practises form, not content.
- **Why it works:** Writers do have scales to practise. A grammatical but meaningless sentence is a vase without flowers; practising the vase builds skill (V10 6:56–8:33). You don't need to know the grammar; you feel which swaps fit a slot (V10 9:17–10:08). Unlike scales, it produces new sentences.
- **Steps:** 1. Pick a model sentence. 2. Mark the slots. 3. List replacements that fit each slot, and notice ones that don't. 4. Write several complete new sentences in the same shape. 5. Repeat regularly.
- **Symptoms it fixes:** Waiting for inspiration; a limited stock of sentence shapes.
- **When not to use:** Practice is not performance; once the skill is in place, write meaningful content (V10 10:46).
- **Example:** Model: "The river kept its own calendar, flooding whenever the town forgot it." New: "The market kept its own clock, crashing whenever the analysts ignored it."
- **Sources:** V10 6:56–10:46.

### 1.28 Annotate a model text by function
- **What:** Read an admired piece and label each section (or paragraph) in one to three words for what it does in the arc, then read the labels alone as a storyboard. Also read it closely for how specific effects are produced.
- **Why it works:** Close reading shows sentences; labels show movement: tension, relief, first success, retreat, culmination (V21 15:32, 29:54–30:30). Every paragraph should carry one message, and the messages should add up (V22 19:00–20:27).
- **Steps:** 1. Label each section. 2. List the labels. 3. Do the same to your own draft and compare. 4. Merge sections with the same label; rework ones you can't label.
- **Symptoms it fixes:** Admiring a piece without being able to learn from it.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Labels for an essay on learning to swim at forty: fear at the edge; humiliation in the kiddie lane; first float; plateau; swimming with my daughter.
- **Sources:** V21 3:09, 15:32, 29:54–30:30; V22 19:00–20:27; V44 22:00–24:38 (the analytic second reading, 7.6).

### 1.29 The précis: summarize, then argue against
- **What:** To master hard material, write a short paper that states a reading's argument in your own words and then argues against it, and present it to peers.
- **Why it works:** Accountability to readers you respect produces clarity and depth; there are no summaries to hide behind (V36 13:48–16:10, 18:08).
- **Steps:** 1. Summarize the argument in your words. 2. Argue against it. 3. Put it in front of people whose judgment you care about.
- **Symptoms it fixes:** Reading that never becomes understanding.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** A product manager writes one page restating a competitor's pricing thesis, then one page on where it fails for small teams, and presents both to her team.
- **Sources:** V36 13:48–16:10.

### 1.30 One paragraph per page
*(Don DeLillo's method, as reported in The Paris Review)*
- **What:** While drafting or revising, give each paragraph its own page or screen, with whitespace around it.
- **Why it works:** You see each set of sentences more clearly and work at the level of the sentence (V10 0:00–0:44, 10:08–10:46).
- **Steps:** 1. Isolate each paragraph. 2. Read and adjust its sentences alone. 3. Reassemble for the final layout (inferred).
- **Symptoms it fixes:** Dense pages where individual sentences blur; revision that skims.
- **When not to use:** It is a working view, not the final format (inferred).
- **Example:** Isolated on their own screens, three paragraphs in a row turn out to open with "However".
- **Sources:** V10 0:00–0:44, 10:08–10:46.

### 1.31 The joint-attention drill
*(his terms: joint attention; the classic scene)*
- **What:** A spoken warm-up. With a partner, take turns offering one precise, observable detail of your surroundings at a time, for about ten minutes. Only what is present and checkable; no inference. Alone: record yourself describing a picture to someone imagined beside you.
- **Why it works:** It trains the felt experience of two people looking at the same thing, each aware of the other, which is what clear prose has to rebuild on the page (V30 2:44–4:46). Offering a detail "like a gift" makes you point precisely.
- **Steps:** As above; then use 6.16 to carry the scene into writing.
- **Symptoms it fixes:** Prose that talks at the reader; details only the writer can see.
- **When not to use:** He calls it a bit of a conceit and a training exercise only (V30 4:46).
- **Example:** Not "look at that building" but "the office block across the road has four rows of windows, and only the top row has its blinds half down, all at the same angle."
- **Sources:** V30 2:44–5:36.

# Stage 2. Planning for readers

The bridge between the writer's draft and the reader's draft. The reader arrives here. He won't look at a client's text until the reader questions are answered (V24 3:22).

## 2A. From the writer's draft to the reader's draft

### 2.1 Reverse outline
*(his term)*
- **What:** After the thinking draft exists, go through it section by section and label what each part actually does, not what you meant it to do. Lay out the labels alone and read them as a sequence.
- **Why it works:** A pre-planned outline suits neither thinking (you don't know the idea yet) nor readers (V35 9:41; V47 20:14). Labels reveal the real logic of the draft (V35 9:41). When you have lost faith in a whole project, you can't fix macro problems with sentence edits; you need to see the structure (V23 23:19–24:09). Rereading slowly as if meeting the text for the first time often shows it is better than you feared, or shows exactly where it went sideways (V23 22:22–23:19).
- **Steps:**
  1. Reread the draft slowly, following the thought of the person who wrote it (V23 22:22).
  2. Mark section boundaries.
  3. Label each part by its function. If you can't label it, it is fluff or still a tangle (V35 8:57; V47 21:09).
  4. List the labels alone. Look for parts with the same label, the key claim arriving late, and sections in order of discovery.
  5. Ideally, the labels should line up with the reader's questions, so the sequence becomes a map from the reader's current understanding to the new one (V35 9:24–9:41).
- **Symptoms:** A sense that the whole thing isn't working, combined with fiddling with sentences (V23 23:19).
- **When not to use:** Don't outline *before* drafting expert work (V42 15:25–16:02; V47 20:14). Simple list-shaped pieces may be fine with a prior outline (inferred from V47 20:14).
- **Example:** Labels: company history; define churn; customer story; CLAIM: churn is an onboarding problem; more history; usage data. The problems are visible at once: the claim sits fourth, history is split in two, and the data comes after the claim it should support.
- **Sources:** V21 15:32, 29:54; V23 22:22–24:09; V35 8:11–9:41; V47 18:15–22:07.

### 2.2 Find the through lines
*(his terms: the architect; through lines)*
- **What:** Step back from the generated material and find the structure native to it: the major and minor through lines that keep recurring. Strengthen those in the reader's draft instead of forcing the material into a template.
- **Why it works:** Structure can emerge without a blueprint, as ant colonies build efficient forms with no planner (V42 16:31). The subconscious already knows the shape of ideas it has worked on; your job is to dig it out (V42 18:15). Readers still need hierarchy: they navigate text like physical space (V42 19:27).
- **Steps:** 1. Reread the generated pages. 2. Circle ideas and words that recur. 3. Name two or three through lines. 4. Build the outline, if you need one, from those (V42 20:01). 5. Give each chapter or piece a short "table of ideas" in order (V18 23:07).
- **Symptoms:** A pre-set "History / Problems / Solutions" outline that the material doesn't fit; a draft that reads like filled-in blanks.
- **When not to use:** Not a license to skip organization; he is explicit he is not against it (V42 19:27).
- **Example:** Three messy sessions on why senior engineers stop writing design docs keep returning to "status" and "an audience of one". Those become the two through lines.
- **Sources:** V18 16:48–17:41, 23:07; V23 35:22–38:09; V42 16:31–20:01.

### 2.3 Diagnose interference
*(his term: interference; also writer patterns versus reading patterns)*
- **What:** Find the patterns in your draft that came from how you thought, which clash with how readers read. The main ones:
  - the answer arrives at the end, because that is where you found it;
  - background or context first;
  - chronological order, the order you discovered things;
  - narrating your thought process ("first I thought X, then I read Y...");
  - loops, side trails, and your own compressed shorthand.
- **Why it works:** Writers use the text to think about the world; readers use it to learn about the world (V31 20:45; V47 1:52). Readers want certainty up front and a move from a stable, familiar world to the discovery that something is wrong with it (V47 3:55, 7:16–8:19). Handed the thinking draft, they relive your whole search. They won't say "your patterns interfere with mine"; they say "unclear" or "disorganized" (V19 26:31). School trained these patterns because the teacher is paid to read and wants to see that you did the work (V19 2:10–5:41; V20 9:33–15:41; V31 19:11–21:15; V46 5:13).
- **Steps:**
  1. Accept that the thinking draft is a writer's document; these patterns are normal, not mistakes (V47 2:51).
  2. Scan for each pattern above.
  3. For each, write down the reader's pattern that should replace it: point first, familiar world first, problem order.
  4. Rebuild (2.4).
- **Symptoms:** Main claim in the last third; "it turns out that" late in the piece; paragraphs opening "After reviewing..." or "When I first started looking into this..."; "unclear" feedback on a piece whose ideas are strong.
- **When not to use:** Never run this lens while generating (V47 21:09).
- **Example:** Before: "I began by asking why volunteer numbers fell. Weather showed nothing. The calendar was unchanged. Eventually I saw sign-ups dropped the week we added a second verification step." After: "Volunteer sign-ups fell by a third the week we added a second verification step. Here is how we know it wasn't the weather or the calendar."
- **Sources:** V19 2:10–6:26, 25:11–27:22, 1:08:47–1:11:07; V20 9:33–15:41; V24 4:18–7:28; V31 18:37–21:37; V35 9:41–13:02; V46 4:50–5:41; V47 0:00–3:55, 9:18, 12:59.

### 2.4 Build the reader draft as a new route
*(his image: directions to the restaurant)*
- **What:** Build the reader's version as a new document that starts from where the reader is and walks them to your point, taking material from the thinking draft only as it serves each turn. Don't line-edit the thinking draft into the final.
- **Why it works:** Giving a friend directions, you start from where they are, not from your apartment (V24 5:17; V35 9:41–10:31; V47 10:18–11:07). The thinking draft's problems are structural, so no amount of comma-moving fixes them (V31 8:47; V35 12:09–13:02; V47 17:30). Copying and pasting imports writer patterns (V19 28:15). Once the thinking is done, the reader draft is easier than it feels: you have the pieces and only need to map a trail (V47 12:03).
- **Steps:**
  1. State where the reader starts: what they know, believe, want (2.6).
  2. State where they must end up.
  3. Lay out the major turns between the two as section points (3.16).
  4. Pull the points out of the thinking draft (often from its conclusion; see 3.4) and relocate them to where readers look (V50 0:00, 15:06).
  5. Rebuild the support around each point in the order the reader needs it.
- **Symptoms:** A "revision" that only changed words and moved paragraphs (V19 28:15); a route that follows your chronology.
- **When not to use:** His strictest version (blank document, no copy and paste, V19 27:22–29:54) softens by V50 into extracting and relocating points while keeping the writer's language and voice. Use the V50 version in practice. Routine summaries built from a known outline may not need two documents (inferred from V47 16:21).
- **Example:** Your route: data audit, interviews, a failed training pilot, then the discovery that the handover form's layout causes errors. The ward manager's route starts from "we need more training", shows why the pilot didn't help, then reveals the form.
- **Sources:** V19 27:22–29:54; V20 7:40–9:33; V24 4:18–7:28; V31 7:55–10:11, 21:37; V35 9:41–13:27; V47 9:18–12:03, 17:30–18:15; V50 0:00, 15:06.

## 2B. The reader brief

### 2.5 The purpose chain
- **What:** Before touching ideas or prose, write a chain: your goal (definition of success) → who must read this for the goal to come true → that reader's main pain → the piece or table of contents as a bridge from their situation to the solution → a filter for every element. Also name what you want the text to *do* in the world, and to whom.
- **Why it works:** When a piece's content doesn't match what readers came for, trust breaks (V18 9:52). The chain becomes the razor for what goes in (V18 13:56), and it keeps editing from being about taste: an editor helps you name your goals and works backward from the reader (V18 14:32–15:26; V19 13:47–14:33). A text is a third party sent into the world to cause change, so being articulate and being authoritative amount to the same thing (V38 13:22–14:48).
- **Steps:**
  1. Step away from the draft. Write your goal (clients, authority, a decision, understanding, legacy) (V18 10:47–11:35).
  2. Who must read it for that to happen? What do you know about their time, format, context? (V18 11:35–12:30)
  3. Name their main problem or pain (V18 12:30).
  4. Design the sequence as a bridge: actions for a practical problem, belief changes for a conceptual one (V18 12:30–13:23).
  5. Filter: does each story, idea and example help this reader with this problem? (see 3.29)
- **Symptoms:** "What does this have to do with X?"; readers calling the piece unfocused; side roads that interested the author (V18 9:52, 14:32).
- **When not to use:** He suggests fiction can run on personal taste (V18 14:32).
- **Example:** Goal: bookings for a nutrition practice. Reader: parents of a child just diagnosed with food allergies. Pain: fear at every meal. Chapters: "Clear your kitchen in one weekend", "Read any label in ten seconds". Cut: the chapter on your marathon training.
- **Sources:** V17 3.1 framing (goal selects type, 0:00); V18 9:52–15:26; V19 10:39–15:13; V38 13:22–14:48.

### 2.6 The reader brief
- **What:** Answer a fixed set of questions about one specific reader before writing the reader draft.
  - What motivated them to click or pick this up? What problem are they dealing with? What answer are they looking for? What decision are they trying to make? (V24 3:01–3:22)
  - What do they currently believe? Where does their knowledge stop? What must they think differently afterward? (V42 23:44; V25 7:59–8:27)
  - Do they already agree with the premise, or need warming up? Will they resist? Is this idea dear to them? Are they looking for a critique? (V20 24:03–24:25)
- **Why it works:** You can't judge a text without knowing what it responds to (V24 3:22). "Business people" or "a general audience" is a target you can't see (V19 10:39); generic readers don't exist (V19 11:31). Readers decide what counts as better understanding, so you must know their starting point (V25 7:59). "Old information" itself depends on who is reading (V15 5:16).
- **Steps:** 1. Name one reader (a person or a narrow group). 2. Answer every question above in writing at the top of the draft. 3. Judge each later choice against the answers. 4. If you write for several audiences, treat it as several simultaneous chess games (V25 2:24).
- **Symptoms:** An audience described as a demographic ("marketers"); explaining basics readers know or skipping steps they need; an editor's comments that feel arbitrary.
- **When not to use:** Not during the writer's draft (1.1).
- **Example:** Not "HR managers" but: an HR manager at a 50-person firm whose key hire just quit, trying to replace her without overpaying, deciding this week whether to use a recruiter.
- **Sources:** V15 5:16; V19 10:39–15:13; V20 21:38–24:25; V24 3:01–3:22; V25 2:24, 7:59–8:27; V42 23:44–24:08.

### 2.7 Diagnose the reader's stance
- **What:** Classify the reader and adapt the problem accordingly.
  - **Uninformed:** doesn't know the problem exists; give enough background to see it and its stakes.
  - **Indifferent:** knows but doesn't care; establish consequences that touch them.
  - **Resistant:** knows the problem and dislikes your solution; acknowledge their position in their language, then show it is less stable than they think, so your idea looks like what secures it (V24 10:50–12:20).
  Related awareness levels: some readers know their problem and want a solution; some know the solution and want someone to deliver it; some don't know there is a problem (V27 27:00).
- **Why it works:** Information that doesn't touch the reader's resistance produces "why are you telling me this?" (V24 8:15). For resistant readers, the "however" has to hit the specific belief that stands between them and your idea, not a side fact (V24 8:15–10:27).
- **Steps:** 1. Name the reader's current belief. 2. Decide which type they are. 3. For resistant readers, find the blocking belief and aim the turn at it.
- **Symptoms:** A "however" followed by a fact that doesn't contradict anything the reader believes; background given to experts; consequences given to people who don't yet see the problem.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Moving a team's decisions from chat to email, for readers who love chat because it's fast. Weak: "Everyone loves chat. However, we send 4,000 messages a week." Better: "We use chat because it makes decisions fast. But three decisions last quarter were made twice because nobody could find the first thread."
- **Sources:** V24 8:15–12:20; V27 27:00.

### 2.8 Map the situation: the rhetorical planning wheel
*(his terms: rhetorical planning wheel; situational network)*
- **What:** A planning map for professional texts. At the center: the genre and its usual functions. Around the rim: purpose and actions; the writer's role (relation to the topic, the reader, and the information: how sure are you?); the audience (knowledge, wants, uses, where they'll look); and the context (what event triggered the text, what should happen after it). Add the question: to get the reader to believe X, what else must they believe?
- **Why it works:** The context is too large to hold in your head, so the wheel breaks it into parts (V30 7:08). Generic advice fails in professional settings because the text must fit its context. His case: a graduate applicant's error-free, guide-compliant statement opened with a love of tech videos, which signaled passive consumption to a panel screening for intellectual vitality (V30 5:36–7:08).
- **Steps:**
  1. Name the genre and its functions.
  2. Write the purpose as a belief or action you want.
  3. Break it down: what else must they believe? Find real evidence for each part (V30 7:08).
  4. Sketch your role and set the tone to match (confident without arrogance).
  5. Profile the audience.
  6. Write the trigger and the outcome.
  7. Do this before writing a word (V30 10:12).
- **Symptoms:** Advice-compliant but off-target content; anecdotes that show the wrong trait; information placed where readers won't look.
- **When not to use:** Casual, low-stakes texts need little mapping (inferred).
- **Example:** Grant proposal. Purpose: panel believes the project is fundable. What else must they believe? The question matters, the method works, this team delivers on time. Evidence: a pilot result, a validated method, a past project finished on schedule.
- **Sources:** V30 4:46–11:13.

## 2C. Framing the value

### 2.9 The problem statement
- **What:** A private sentence at the top of the page, not for publication: *I am writing about [topic] because I want to find out [question] so I can help my reader [gain a benefit / avoid a cost].* Delete anything that doesn't serve it.
- **Why it works:** Topics give readers no reason to read; an opening like "In this piece I will explore..." has already lost them (V12 36:13–36:40). Before drafting, write down the question or problem; if there isn't one, you aren't yet writing valuably (V38 0:58–1:36).
- **Steps:** 1. Fill in the template. 2. Keep it visible. 3. Test every section against it. 4. Vision check: also state in one sentence what you have actually seen to be true. With that in mind and the reader in front of you, the words come without planning; if you can't state it, go back to thinking, not polishing (V45 5:09–7:30).
- **Symptoms:** Sections that are true and interesting but serve no reader problem; a draft that began from "give me three points on X" (V45 6:52).
- **When not to use:** It is a planning tool; it doesn't need to appear in the piece (V12 37:20).
- **Example:** "I am writing about discharge instructions because I want to find out why patients misread them, so I can help nurse managers cut avoidable readmissions."
- **Sources:** V12 35:03–37:20; V19 9:44–10:39; V38 0:58–1:36; V45 5:09–7:30.

### 2.10 Benefit gained or cost avoided
- **What:** For each idea, ask what benefit this reader gets from it or what cost it helps them avoid, and write that down in their terms. Make costs personal: food off this reader's table, not "inconsistencies".
- **Why it works:** It turns knowledge into value by tying it to the reader's gain or loss (V20 15:41). "This creates some inconsistencies" is abstract; "this stops you keeping your best customers" is urgent (V19 55:02–55:31). The same problem carries different stakes for different audiences (V19 55:31–56:18). He declines to give templates, because template writing is where writers go wrong (V20 15:41–16:36).
- **Steps:** 1. Write the idea. 2. Under it: "Benefit:" or "Cost avoided:" for this reader. 3. Lead with that line where it matters.
- **Symptoms:** Problems described only as gaps, tensions or inconsistencies, with nobody affected.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Idea: "Batching small tasks reduces context switching." Benefit: "You get back the ninety-minute block you keep losing to ten-minute jobs."
- **Sources:** V19 54:13–57:08; V20 15:41–16:36.

### 2.11 Practical versus conceptual problems
- **What:** A practical problem is solved by changing what the reader does. A conceptual problem is solved by changing what they understand: their model is wrong or incomplete. Conceptual problems sit upstream of practical ones.
- **Why it works:** It gives every piece, even one about ideas, an outcome to promise: new actions, or an old idea swapped for a better one (V18 4:21–5:10; V19 7:13–7:44). Once the model changes, new practices follow downstream (V22 10:19–11:17).
- **Steps:** 1. Classify the problem. 2. Practical: promise the actions. 3. Conceptual: name the old idea and the idea that replaces it. 4. Preview the solution accordingly (3.9).
- **Symptoms:** A chapter that "explores" a theme without changing either behavior or belief.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Conceptual: "Motivation isn't a feeling you wait for; it follows from starting." Practical: "Start the task for five minutes before deciding whether you feel like it."
- **Sources:** V18 4:21–5:10; V19 7:13–10:39; V20 19:13–20:12; V22 10:19–11:17; V28 14:11–14:52; V31 6:02–7:00.

### 2.12 GPS, not garden hose: the reader-change test
- **What:** Stop thinking of writing as a hose that carries ideas from your head to the reader's. Think of it as directions that meet the reader at point A and take them to point B. The test for any choice: does it move this reader from where they are to where you want them?
- **Why it works:** The hose model is self-centered; it assumes readers want your thoughts (V19 6:26–7:13). Nonfiction exists to change the reader's ideas, not to express yours (V42 21:48–22:14). Most writers revise by asking "have I expressed myself clearly?", which is the wrong question (V42 24:08).
- **Steps:** 1. Replace "how do I explain my ideas?" with "how do I move readers from A to B?" 2. Treat the text as a guided route, not a store of knowledge.
- **Symptoms:** A draft organized around your ideas ("my framework has five parts") instead of the reader's route.
- **When not to use:** The thinking draft, where developing your own ideas is the job (V19 8:34).
- **Example:** Hose: "My theory of pricing rests on three principles." GPS: "If you price by adding a margin to cost, you're undercharging your best customers. Here's what to change on Monday."
- **Sources:** V19 6:26–8:34; V42 20:28–24:08.

### 2.13 Replace the gap model with the model-error frame
*(his term: the GAP model)*
- **What:** Don't justify a piece by a gap ("no one has studied X"). Instead, name the model or assumption your readers currently use, show where it fails (an anomaly it can't explain, a prediction it gets wrong), state what that costs them, and offer your idea as the repair.
- **Why it works:** If knowledge were a finite puzzle, any new piece would be valuable. But readers use knowledge as a working model to explain and predict, so new information is valuable only when it improves the model; otherwise it is just information (V31 15:55–18:10). Readers are drowning in information, especially since AI (V31 18:10). An idea is an invention taken to market; it becomes knowledge only when readers adopt it (V25 5:42–7:01). His case: a grant pitch for a novelist's archive justified only by "nobody has read these yet" (V31 15:00).
- **Steps:** 1. Name the readers' current model. 2. Find where it fails. 3. State the cost. 4. Present your work as the repair and name the benefit.
- **Symptoms:** "No one has", "first to", "understudied", "gap in the literature"; the pitch would be equally true of any unstudied topic; the readers' beliefs never appear.
- **When not to use:** A tiny audience of enthusiasts may read anything on a topic (V31 15:00), but that isn't broad value. Shifting what readers care about is possible but rare and risky; treat it as the exception, not audience capture in reverse (V25 4:50, 7:01).
- **Example:** Before: "No study has examined night-shift nurses' use of vending machines." After: "Hospitals plan staff meals around cafeteria hours, assuming night staff eat early in the shift. Our logs show most night-shift eating happens after 2 a.m., when only vending machines are open."
- **Sources:** V25 3:18–7:01; V27 26:10–27:24; V31 14:34–18:37; V46 6:03, 7:47.

## 2D. Language for this reader

### 2.14 The code-word hunt
*(his phrases: read like an anthropologist; code words; value words)*
- **What:** Every reading community has in-group words that signal "this matters" and "I'm one of you". Collect them from texts your readers share and admire, and phrase your problem and payoff in them. Harvest value words too, in three columns: opportunity (*enables, unlocks*), cost (*prevents, erodes*), urgency (*critical, this quarter*), plus universal turn words (*but, however*).
- **Why it works:** Language carries social information as well as content (V31 10:11–13:10; V42 12:12). "I have a prior" and "I have an assumption" say the same thing, but one signals membership in a statistical community (V12 3:46–5:04). Outsider language distracts readers even when grammar is perfect: his Korean students picturing a North Korean speaker (V31 10:11–12:28; V42 11:29–12:12). The same point needs different words for a founder and a Fortune 500 executive (V35 16:49). Writing at a "fifth-grade level" tells readers how you see them (V12 5:04).
- **Steps:**
  1. Name the community (V35 16:49).
  2. Pick four or five prominent, widely shared pieces from it (V19 17:16; V22 31:32).
  3. Read like an anthropologist; list recurring value and status words (V19 16:59–18:46).
  4. Sort value words into opportunity, cost, urgency (V19 50:16–51:23).
  5. Rewrite key claims in those terms (V35 17:57).
  6. Choose register deliberately: a precise rare word or a blunt everyday one, whichever lands with these readers (V12 5:57–7:54).
- **Symptoms:** Value claims phrased in your own discipline's vocabulary; readers who agree but don't care; a piece "for everybody" (V35 17:57); a monotone middle register.
- **When not to use:** Not a license for empty obscurity: if you can't explain it plainly, you may not understand it (V42 13:46). Don't import code you don't understand (inferred). Showy words chosen to sound smart backfire (V12 5:57; V29 13:07). For outsiders, unpack instead (2.15).
- **Example:** The same finding. Academic: "Response latency is inversely associated with retention." For SaaS founders: "Answer support tickets within the hour and customers stay a year longer."
- **Sources:** V12 3:46–7:54, 14:37–18:09; V17 7:38–8:34; V19 16:59–18:46, 49:20–51:23; V22 30:25–32:22; V31 9:42–14:02; V35 13:27–18:54; V42 9:28–14:20.

### 2.15 Unpack expert compression
- **What:** Experts compress what they learned into shorthand: terms, named methods, "everyone knows" moves. Peers share it, so it goes unnoticed; outsiders (funders, investors, the lay public) find it dense. Find the shorthand and unpack what this reader doesn't share.
- **Why it works:** The issue isn't only forgetting what it was like not to know; you don't notice how much compression and field-specific thinking you rely on (V47 12:59–16:21). He rejects "curse of knowledge" as a label that has itself become a slogan leading to "write simply" advice (V47 12:59–13:39).
- **Steps (inferred procedure; he states the principle):** 1. Name the reader community. 2. Mark every term, acronym, named method and assumed step you'd use with peers. 3. For each, ask whether this reader shares it. 4. Unpack it, or replace it with something the reader already has. 5. Make the value stand out for this reader, not for peers.
- **Symptoms:** "Dense" from outsiders but not colleagues; one noun phrase standing in for an argument you made years ago.
- **When not to use:** Writing for peers inside the community, where shared compression is efficient (V47 14:32). The lesson is not "write at a fifth-grade level" (V47 13:39).
- **Example:** For a funding panel, not "we saw the usual novelty decay in both arms" but "both versions of the app got a burst of use in week one that faded by week four; that fade is normal, so we measured success after it."
- **Sources:** V47 12:59–16:21.

### 2.16 Plan the reader's journey as a decision tree
*(his framing: writing is a two-player game)*
- **What:** Plan the reader draft as a game: I say this, they'll think that, they'll ask this so I answer it now, they won't accept this part. Begin with a "greeting" that brings them into your world.
- **Why it works:** In any two-player game you win by watching the other player, not by making the same moves every time (V20 0:00–0:47). The aim is to bring readers to the understanding you reached, on their terms (V20 23:28–24:03).
- **Steps:** 1. After each paragraph of the plan, write the reader's likely response in brackets. 2. Make the next paragraph answer it. 3. Delete the brackets. 4. Use the brief (2.6) to predict responses.
- **Symptoms:** A monologue; a structure following your notes; an argument that never meets resistance because it never imagines any.
- **When not to use:** The thinking phase (V20 8:25).
- **Example:** Reader: a finance director skeptical of a four-day week. Open with a cost she already worries about (burnout turnover). She'll ask about client coverage, so answer that next. She'll doubt the data, so use comparable firms. She'll fear precedent, so propose a reversible trial.
- **Sources:** V20 0:00–0:47, 21:38–24:58. (Objection handling in detail: 3.26.)

# Stage 3. Structure

The "bones" of the reader draft: where the point goes, how the introduction earns attention, how sections are ordered and signaled, how the argument holds, and how it ends. If you fix only one thing, fix the introduction (V46 19:48).

## 3A. Shape and point

### 3.1 Function over form
*(his terms: the template trap; the school writing trap; the Starbucks test is a label for his example)*
- **What:** Choose the shape of a piece from what this reader needs it to do, not from a template. A genre is a predictable sequence of functions that help writer and reader reach a shared goal; the surface wording can vary, the functions can't.
- **Why it works:** At a café the functions are greeting, order, size, here or to go, payment. A barista asking your favorite color is grammatically perfect and useless, because it serves no function (V20 17:08–20:35). Templates like "always start with a thesis" and "every paragraph needs a topic sentence" were built for grading (V20 16:36; V31 2:16). Different reading communities expect different openings: journalism readers expect an incident in the lede (V31 3:15–3:35).
- **Steps:**
  1. State the goal reader and writer share.
  2. List the functions the genre needs to reach it.
  3. Tag each paragraph with its function.
  4. Cut anything that is a "favorite color" question, however well written.
  5. Check what openings and orders this reader's own genre uses (see 3.2).
- **Symptoms:** A structure that would be identical whatever the topic or audience; writers describing a piece by its template ("intro, three body paragraphs, conclusion"); polished paragraphs that make the reader wonder why they are there.
- **When not to use:** He insists his own replacements (index first, problem first) are not new templates but descriptions of where readers look (V19 51:23; V31 9:42).
- **Example:** In a memo recommending a supplier, a paragraph on the founder's childhood is a favorite-color question. The genre needs: the decision, the options, the recommendation, the risks, the next step.
- **Sources:** V19 18:46–21:25; V20 16:36–21:38, 24:25; V25 2:24–3:18; V30 7:46; V31 2:16–3:35, 22:48.

### 3.2 Private, contextual and textual structure
*(his terms)*
- **What:** Three kinds of order.
  - **Private:** makes sense only to the writer; it is what you get when you write the way you think.
  - **Contextual:** readers bring it from background knowledge, for example the expected sections of a scientific report or incident review.
  - **Textual:** a pattern the text itself makes visible, such as a named umbrella or a previewed list (see 4.8).
  Use contextual or textual structure; replace private structure.
- **Why it works:** Structure lives in the reader, so you can rely only on patterns the reader already has or can see on the page (V49 10:14). Faced with a private pattern, readers don't give up; they hunt for a pattern and often invent one you didn't mean, as with a number series that is really someone's phone number (V49 4:34–6:42). Heavy metadiscourse is the symptom of a private order being patched by hand (V49 10:14–12:09).
- **Steps:** 1. For each ordering choice, ask what its principle is and whether the reader can know it. 2. If only you could explain it, it's private. 3. Meet genre expectations where they exist. 4. Otherwise make the pattern visible: name the umbrella, preview the parts, keep key terms stable. 5. Test: if someone asked why the sections come in this order and you had no answer, there is no organizing principle (V50 12:16).
- **Symptoms:** Frequent "as discussed below" and "it is important to note that these are related"; readers inventing causal links you didn't intend; "first, second, third" used as if it were a principle (V50 13:57).
- **When not to use:** He doesn't ban metadiscourse ("you can do whatever you want"); the warning is about using it to prop up a private order (V49 11:14).
- **Example:** Private: "Our CRM launched in March. The vendor demo was during a storm. Q2 sales fell. Training was two people short." Readers assume the CRM caused the drop. Textual: "Three things changed in spring, and only one explains the Q2 drop: the training gap."
- **Sources:** V49 0:00–14:02; V50 12:16–13:57.

### 3.3 Put the point first
*(his terms: point first; point last; the end of the beginning)*
- **What:** Find the one sentence you need readers to accept, act on or use, and put it at the end of the introduction. At smaller scales, open sections and paragraphs with their claims. Holding the point to the end (point last) is an advanced option that needs a preview.
- **Why it works:** Writers think from uncertainty to certainty, so the best idea lands at the end of the draft; readers need the destination first to decide whether to invest (V24 4:18–7:28; V47 2:51–3:55). Even skimmers get the message (V24 7:28). Point last works only if the key concepts are introduced early as a promise, so the ending feels inevitable rather than arbitrary (V50 9:37–11:36).
- **Steps:**
  1. Find the sentence readers must accept; it is usually in your conclusion or buried mid-paragraph (V24 5:17; V31 20:45).
  2. Move it to the end of the introduction (V50 8:23–9:37).
  3. For point last: identify the key concepts in the point, introduce them early, develop each, and bring them together at the end like striking a chord (V50 10:42–11:36).
- **Symptoms:** Paragraphs opening "After reviewing..." or "Having considered..."; the recommendation in the last sentence; a reader who stops after the introduction can't say what you think.
- **When not to use:** Narrative creative nonfiction may let the thesis emerge late (V21 34:19; V50 9:37). Point last also appears at section scale in storytelling (V19 53:46).
- **Example:** Before: "We surveyed 40 customers, reviewed tickets, compared churn by plan, and interviewed sales. Based on this, we think the free tier should be capped." After: "Cap the free tier at three projects. Customers who hit a limit upgrade at twice the rate..."
- **Sources:** V19 4:55–5:41, 53:46–54:13; V24 4:18–7:28; V31 18:37–21:37; V47 2:51–3:55, 7:16–8:19; V50 8:23–11:36.

### 3.4 Frame by comparing the introduction's and conclusion's points
- **What:** Mark the introduction and conclusion, ignore the middle, underline the single sentence in each that best states the point, and compare. Keep the sharper one or synthesize both into one crisp point sentence for the end of the introduction.
- **Why it works:** Because you think as you write, you start with a vague point and sharpen it working through the evidence; the conclusion usually holds the best version (V50 5:58). The two should make the same point so the piece closes where it opened, without repeating the introduction (V50 6:33).
- **Steps:** 1. Underline the introduction (it may run pages) and the conclusion. 2. Underline the best point sentence in each. 3. Same point? 4. Which is sharper, more arguable? 5. Keep or synthesize.
- **Symptoms:** A broad, descriptive point in the introduction and a specific, arguable one at the end; or two different points.
- **When not to use:** Writing that makes no point (V50 0:48).
- **Example:** Intro: "Community gardens bring neighbors together." Conclusion: "Shared plots build trust because they force small negotiations private plots avoid." Synthesis: "Community gardens build trust between neighbors, but only when plots are shared, because sharing forces small negotiations."
- **Sources:** V50 4:24–8:23.

## 3B. The introduction

### 3.5 The problem-first introduction
*(his terms: status quo; concession; destabilizing condition; consequence; solution)*
- **What:** Build the opening as a problem and its solution, not background and a thesis. The full sequence:
  1. **Status quo / common ground:** something the reader already accepts (3.6).
  2. **Concession:** what that view got right (3.7).
  3. **Destabilizing condition:** the turn, signaled with *but* or *however*.
  4. **Consequence:** the cost to this reader of staying put, or the benefit of fixing it (3.8).
  5. **Solution:** your idea, previewed (3.9).
  An optional short prelude (a statistic, anecdote, even a meme) may come first, but only if it earns its place (V28 15:14).
- **Why it works:** Readers must perceive value before they read, and value is a solution to a problem (V22 5:23; V46 8:20). Drama works the same way: someone wants something and can't get it, and your reader is that person (V46 6:41). Background-first openings (the **martini glass** *(his term)*: wide background narrowing to a thesis and widening again) are built on continuity and signal "more of the same" (V31 0:52–4:34). Readers ask "why should I care?" first; recipe blogs that make you scroll through a life story are the familiar failure (V19 31:36–32:26). He later presents the sequence as three parts plus a prelude, folding the concession into sympathetic common ground (V28 14:52).
- **Steps:**
  1. Write your idea X in one line.
  2. State the status quo, often the opposite of X or an accepted state whose cost nobody has counted (V27 27:52; V28 5:18).
  3. Grant what it gets right.
  4. Turn with *but* or *however*.
  5. Say what it costs this reader.
  6. Preview X as the fix.
  7. Remove information-first openings: background then thesis, general to specific, "X studied this, Y studied that, nobody has studied Z" (V46 4:50–6:03).
- **Symptoms:** First paragraph could be pasted into any paper on the topic; the opening announces a topic; the idea is sold on novelty or effort; no *but* before the point; the first paragraph could be deleted with nothing lost (V19 33:26).
- **When not to use:** Adjust how much problem to spell out to the reader's awareness (V27 27:00; see 2.7). Write the moves with grace; his demonstrations are skeletons (V27 29:04; V28 13:36). Narrative creative nonfiction often opens with an image instead (8.5). School forms suit a reader obliged to read (V46 5:13).
- **Example (thesis: small nonprofits should apply for fewer grants):** "Most small nonprofits apply for every grant they qualify for. It's understandable: more applications look like more chances. But each proposal costs a two-person team about forty hours, hours taken from the programs funders want to see. Groups that pick a few well-matched funders raise more with less work."
- **Sources:** V12 35:03–37:20; V19 30:43–33:26; V22 5:23–11:17; V24 8:15–12:20; V27 25:12–31:36; V28 4:20–15:55; V31 0:52–7:55; V46 3:52–8:20, 19:48; V47 7:16–8:19.

### 3.6 Common ground
*(his term: establishing the status quo)*
- **What:** Open with something the reader already accepts, stated plainly, so they nod along before you challenge it.
- **Why it works:** Opening with a hot take makes people defensive; common ground changes how the challenge feels (V28 4:20–5:18). Once readers feel understood, the dominoes are lined up (V28 5:18–6:16). Framing words can quietly mark the old view as dated: "passed down", "based on the assumption", "still thrives in textbooks" (V22 6:18–8:14).
- **Steps:**
  1. Invert your idea: think about its opposite (V28 6:16–6:35).
  2. Find what readers already believe, from one of three sources: historical background, a recent event, a common belief (V28 7:05–7:43).
  3. State it plainly; don't over-explain.
  4. Choose accurate, restrained words that mark it as inherited or unexamined, if true (V22 6:18–8:14).
  5. Check that it really deserves to be challenged (V28 8:06).
- **Symptoms:** The thesis fired at the reader in sentence one; a famous quote used as a hook; nothing a reader would agree with before the turn; or the old view described with open contempt.
- **When not to use:** The three sources are for building common ground, not as clichéd hooks (V28 7:43).
- **Example:** Hot take: "Open offices are a disaster." Common ground: "Most managers assume open offices encourage collaboration, and for quick questions they often do."
- **Sources:** V22 6:18–8:14; V27 27:52–29:04; V28 4:20–8:44; V46 7:04–7:27.

### 3.7 Concede before you turn
*(his phrase: don't come off the top ropes)*
- **What:** Before disagreeing, acknowledge what the existing view got right and credit the work of others. Enter the conversation as someone who wants a seat at the table, not as an adversary.
- **Why it works:** Valuable ideas overturn other ideas, so you must disagree; if you disagree with no one, you are talking to yourself in a corner (V19 35:19). But elbowing in, calling everyone wrong without qualification, ignoring prior work or refusing the community's language gets you thrown out regardless of idea quality (V12 14:37–17:33). A brilliant but combative book probably cost its author with his own academic community (V19 37:13–38:53). Conceding keeps readers from turning hostile (V22 8:14).
- **Steps:**
  1. State the consensus.
  2. Say what it gets right or what it helped its believers do.
  3. Acknowledge prior work and leading figures where relevant (V12 17:33).
  4. Then introduce the destabilizing condition.
  5. Admit where your own work has limits (V12 17:33–18:09).
- **Symptoms:** An opening that dismisses the field; "everyone is wrong" framing; a tone that reads as combative to the very people you need.
- **When not to use:** Not always required (V19 38:53). Deference matters most when you need peers' acceptance; bold positioning books pick fights with systems deliberately (8.24).
- **Example:** "Checklists made surgery measurably safer, and that success is why every team wants one. But a checklist guards only the steps someone thought to write down."
- **Sources:** V12 14:37–18:09; V19 35:19–40:04; V22 8:14; V27 29:22; V28 4:20.

### 3.8 Condition plus consequence, and the "so what?" test
*(his terms: condition; consequence; the "so what?" test)*
- **What:** A problem has two parts: a condition (what is wrong or unstable) and a consequence (what it costs this reader, or the benefit they are missing). Keep them close together. Test with "so what?" in a specific reader's voice; your answer is the consequence.
- **Why it works:** Without a cost the reader shrugs; a problem is only a problem if the reader cares (V28 10:19). You can't run the test for a general audience (V28 11:00–11:53). When the link to the reader isn't spelled out, they do extra work on top of reading (V19 1:08:15).
- **Steps:**
  1. State the condition after *but* or *however*; reserve these words for this job in the opening (V28 11:00).
  2. Ask "so what?" as this reader.
  3. Write the answer as a personal consequence.
  4. Put condition and consequence side by side (V28 11:53).
  5. Scale the consequence to each reader group if there are several (V28 9:34–10:19).
  6. Run "so what?" on every major point in the body too (V19 1:07:30–1:08:47).
- **Symptoms:** A *but* with no cost; a cost pitched at "society"; a point that ends on a fact whose reader response is "okay... and?"
- **When not to use:** No universal consequence exists; it changes with the reader (inferred).
- **Example:** Condition: "But when firms removed office walls, face-to-face conversation fell." Consequence for managers: "Teams take longer to solve anything harder than a quick question."
- **Sources:** V19 54:13–57:08, 1:07:30–1:08:47; V27 30:09–30:36; V28 8:44–12:20; V31 5:35.

### 3.9 The solution preview
- **What:** Right after the problem, say what you're offering: the promise and general direction, not all the details. Let the last sentence or two of the introduction name the parts the body will take, in order.
- **Why it works:** A reader left with only a problem feels told they're wrong and given nothing (V28 12:20). A clear preview lets them decide whether to come along (V28 13:54). An introduction that opens into the article's main parts shows the structure in miniature (V22 32:40–34:30).
- **Steps:** 1. Say there is a solution and roughly what kind. 2. For a practical problem, preview what to do; for a conceptual one, what to think instead (V28 14:11–14:52). 3. Name the parts in section order.
- **Symptoms:** A background lecture before the idea, like answering "what time is it?" with how to build a watch (V28 12:20); a vague solution such as "we need to think differently" (V28 12:20–13:06).
- **When not to use:** Don't give all the details here (V28 13:06).
- **Example:** "Offices work best when they give people a choice between quiet rooms and shared tables. This essay shows how three firms made that switch without new construction."
- **Sources:** V22 32:40–34:30; V28 12:20–14:52; V46 7:27.

### 3.10 The two-vendor test
- **What:** Picture your reader walking through a crowded market with full arms. One vendor talks about how long he spent perfecting his product and how important it is. The other names the shopper's problem and offers a fix. Which vendor does your opening sound like?
- **Why it works:** Readers stop for their own problem, not the writer's story; most writers, even good ones, sound like the first vendor (V28 1:15–1:59). Novelty and effort sell nothing (V27 26:10–26:33).
- **Steps:** 1. Highlight every phrase about you: effort, beliefs, excitement, importance. 2. Highlight every phrase about the reader's problem. 3. If the first color dominates, rewrite around the problem.
- **Symptoms:** "After months of research..."; "I believe this may be the most important..."; reading-time warnings; promises to change how you see reality; a quote that only introduces a topic.
- **When not to use:** No limit given. He notes the essay he critiqued succeeded anyway (V28 2:27).
- **Example:** Before: "I spent two years studying sleep and I'm convinced it's the most underrated productivity tool." After: "If you've ever answered email at 11 p.m. and reread it next morning with dread, you know what tired decisions cost."
- **Sources:** V27 25:31–26:33; V28 1:15–4:20.

### 3.11 Enter through what the reader already cares about
*(his terms: the Bilbo moment; building a bridge)*
- **What:** Before the argument or abstract idea, give a clear, concrete story or example the reader can nod along to, drawn from what they already care about. Order examples from simple to complex, literal to metaphorical.
- **Why it works:** Gandalf wins Bilbo's commitment before the adventure (V12 41:39–42:40). The simple story opens the conceptual field and frames the stakes (V12 42:40–43:38). Even educated readers aren't mainly interested in your field; assume they don't care, find what they do care about, and build a door from there (V39 16:37–18:18). Assuming shared values ("sustainability is good") only preaches to the converted (V39 17:32).
- **Steps:**
  1. Write what you want readers to care about.
  2. Write what they already care about: family, money, health, status, place, fairness.
  3. Open with a concrete story from the second list that leads into the first.
  4. Remove or earn any value word you're treating as self-evidently good.
- **Symptoms:** Opening with definitions or theory; value words carrying the argument ("resilient", "innovative"); a piece that works only for people who already agree.
- **When not to use:** Not stated. In expository pieces it works inside the problem-first frame (3.5), usually as common ground or prelude (inferred).
- **Example:** Not "Sustainable urban drainage is essential for resilient cities" but "The Okafors' basement flooded three times in one summer. The third time, their insurer stopped answering."
- **Sources:** V12 41:39–44:07; V22 23:32–24:30 (a story these readers already know); V39 16:37–19:04.

### 3.12 The continuity-versus-instability check
- **What:** Scan the opening's connectors. Continuity words (*and, furthermore, also, in addition*) signal "more of the same". Instability words (*but, however, by contrast, although*) signal a problem, which is where readers look for value.
- **Why it works:** Readers scanning for value look for signs of disruption (V31 4:34). *But, however, although* work as value signals across communities (V35 16:49). They keep their force only if you reserve them for real turns (V28 11:00; V34 8:51–9:42).
- **Steps:** 1. Highlight every connector in the introduction. 2. If they are nearly all additive, the opening is a background list. 3. Find the real tension and put it behind one *but* or *however*. 4. In the body, check the opposite problem: turn words on every other sentence.
- **Symptoms:** A run of additive connectors with no turn; or *however* scattered everywhere.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** "Remote work grew. Furthermore, tools improved. Also, managers adapted." becomes "Remote work grew and the tools improved. But most managers still judge output by visible hours, which is why their best remote staff quit first."
- **Sources:** V28 11:00; V31 4:34–5:35; V34 8:51–9:42; V35 16:49.

## 3C. Titles and headings

### 3.13 Titles that earn the click
*(his terms: curiosity loop; intrigue triggers; parking lot)*
- **What:** The title is the real first line and must work on cold readers. Build it on one of four curiosity triggers, and consider adding three nouns that are the piece's key terms. Don't treat it as a keyword parking lot or an inside joke.
- **Why it works:** A title that gives everything away opens no curiosity loop (V6 4:12). People who already like you will read anything; reaching strangers takes curiosity (V46 1:04–2:16). The four triggers: pose a question or puzzle; signal that others know something the reader doesn't; violate an expectation; open a sequence whose outcome is unknown (V6 5:03; V46 2:16). Three nouns that recur in the body make the piece feel coherent from the first line (V46 2:55).
- **Steps:**
  1. Write the obvious descriptive title.
  2. Ask what question you want the reader to have.
  3. Draft one variant per trigger.
  4. Keep one that raises a question the piece really answers.
  5. Optionally add a colon and the three key-term nouns; check they recur in the introduction and section openings.
  6. Delete any title that only makes sense after reading the piece (V46 1:24).
- **Symptoms:** A topic label; the conclusion stated so the reader can skip the piece; "How to..." by default; a coined phrase from page 3.
- **When not to use:** The piece must close the loop it opens, or the title is bait (inferred). Reference material people search for may still need a descriptive title (inferred).
- **Example:** Before: "Communication in Hybrid Teams". After: "Why Your Best Engineers Quit First: Autonomy, Recognition and Drift".
- **Sources:** V6 2:43–5:27; V46 1:04–2:55.

### 3.14 Headings and chapter titles as promises
- **What:** Each chapter title or section heading states what the reader will gain, built from the section's key concepts: what they will be able to do, or which new idea replaces which old one. No riddles, no inside references.
- **Why it works:** A chapter makes a promise: give this your attention and here is what you get (V18 7:12). Riddle titles break the match between promise and contents, so trust goes before page one (V18 2:38–4:21). Headings set the frame for reading what follows, so they must make sense right away (V27 35:23).
- **Steps:** 1. Find each unit's central idea (often in its last paragraph, V18 6:05). 2. Ask what the reader can do, or what idea they'll hold, by the end. 3. Rewrite the title as that. 4. Use the same wording in the contents and at the top of the chapter. 5. For headings, list the concepts that recur in the section and build the heading from them (V27 34:45–35:23).
- **Symptoms:** Vague noun phrases ("The Lens", "The Game"); clever or cryptic headings; a table of contents that says nothing about what readers gain.
- **When not to use:** For a true ideas book, the "action" is swapping an old idea for a new one (V18 5:10).
- **Example:** "Chapter 4: The Inbox" becomes "Chapter 4: Answer email twice a day without missing what's urgent". Heading "Picking the Lock" becomes "Why Password Resets Drive Support Costs".
- **Sources:** V18 2:38–7:12; V27 33:44–35:23.

## 3D. The body: index, order, signals, key terms

### 3.15 Index and discussion at every scale
*(his terms: index; discussion; fractal)*
- **What:** Every unit opens with a short, easily grasped **index** that states its point and names its key concepts, followed by a longer **discussion** that delivers on it. The pattern repeats at every scale:

  | Unit | Index | Discussion |
  |---|---|---|
  | Sentence | short subject and verb | the rest of the predicate |
  | Paragraph | first one to three sentences | development |
  | Section | opening paragraph(s) | the section |
  | Document | introduction | body |

- **Why it works:** Readers look to the opening of any unit to learn what the rest is for, whether or not you meant it as an index; put a detail there and they misread everything after it (V46 9:08–10:50). Context changes meaning: picture "red, yellow, blue", then "blue, yellow, red", and yellow shifts (V18 20:19–21:12). The index works like a table of contents that promises what the discussion delivers (V27 37:51–38:15). He insists he isn't prescribing it; readers see the pattern intuitively (V46 9:53).
- **Steps:**
  1. At the top of each unit, state what it's doing.
  2. Frame the index as a problem the discussion addresses, not a topic (V46 11:27).
  3. Aim for one sentence; use two or three when opening with an opposition ("X; however, Y") or a question and its answer. Three is the limit for a paragraph (V46 12:43).
  4. End the index on the key terms the discussion will develop (V27 33:44–37:34).
  5. Close each discussion by returning to the point and leading into the next (V27 42:07).
- **Symptoms:** "Wait, I thought this was about..." partway through (V46 10:50); a paragraph opening with method details or raw data (V27 39:59–41:10); the point in the middle; a topic sentence that names a subject but no problem.
- **When not to use:** Point last (3.3) is the controlled exception.
- **Example:** Before: "Forty volunteers wore step counters for eight weeks. In weeks one to four, steps rose..." After: "Wearing a step counter raised volunteers' activity for about a month; then the effect faded. Forty volunteers wore counters..."
- **Sources:** V18 20:19–25:41; V19 51:23–54:13; V27 37:51–43:21; V37 7:25; V46 8:20–12:43; V47 19:13; V50 14:49–15:06.

### 3.16 Organize sections by the reader's problems and questions
- **What:** Structure the body around the main problem and its subproblems, in the order the reader will meet them. Each section answers a question your point raises in the reader's mind, at the moment it would arise. Move each section's point into its index.
- **Why it works:** Without problem-solution structure, readers can't see why your ideas matter (V19 33:26). Problems nest (V19 33:49). Readers expect answers when the question occurs to them, not in the order ideas came to you (V50 13:57). When structure works it's invisible (V50 12:16). Readers follow problem-solving logic far more easily than random characters on stage (V19 48:19).
- **Steps:**
  1. Ask what problem you've solved and where the reader is now.
  2. List the subproblems or questions they must pass through to accept your point (V19 34:34).
  3. Order sections by when those questions arise.
  4. Assign each idea from the thinking draft to one subproblem (V19 34:54–35:19).
  5. Draw lines between sections, underline each section's point (up to three sentences), and move it to the top. If you can't find one, the section needs rewriting (V50 14:49–15:06).
- **Symptoms:** Sections named by topic ("History", "Theory", "Applications"); order of discovery; a shapeless, "flabby" document, like pudding with raisins somewhere in it (V50 12:16); "first, second, third" used as a principle (V50 13:57).
- **When not to use:** He admits the problem frame is "a bit of a conceit" imposed in the reader draft, not how thinking happens (V19 34:34). Avoid rigid imposed orders such as chronology (V50 13:22).
- **Example:** Problem: "Nobody reads our internal docs." Sections: "People can't find them"; "They're out of date when found"; "They answer the writer's question, not the reader's."
- **Sources:** V19 29:54–35:19, 47:22–49:20; V20 22:49–23:28; V50 12:16–15:50.

### 3.17 Transitions that round up and push off
- **What:** Between the introduction and first section, and between sections, place a transition (its own paragraph or the section's last sentence) that rounds off the point just made and pushes into the next. A real question can work here as a palate cleanser.
- **Why it works:** Most prose is declarative; a question breaks the mood and clears the palate, and if it is the question the reader is already asking, they feel understood (V50 15:50–16:32). Asking questions the reader might ask brings them into the conversation (V22 20:49–21:41).
- **Steps:** 1. Restate what's now established. 2. Pose or imply the next question. 3. Keep real questions for transitions.
- **Symptoms:** Sections that stop dead; or rhetorical questions everywhere.
- **When not to use:** Writers overuse rhetorical questions; never ask one the reader isn't asking (V50 15:50).
- **Example:** "So shared plots do build trust. Why, then, do most city gardens assign private ones?"
- **Sources:** V22 20:49–22:29; V50 15:50–16:32.

### 3.18 The skim test
*(his term)*
- **What:** Read only the index positions (first one to three sentences of each paragraph, opening of each section) in order. They should tell the argument's story on their own.
- **Why it works:** If readers use indexes to follow the argument, the indexes alone should storyboard it (V46 12:12). The skim test amounts to a reverse outline of the reader draft (V47 19:13).
- **Steps:** 1. Highlight all indexes. 2. Read only them. 3. Coherent? 4. If not, move the governing claims into the indexes or reorder units. 5. Check the indexes are the major turns on the reader's route (V47 22:07).
- **Symptoms:** Highlighted sentences that are disconnected, repetitive, or mostly transitions and anecdotes.
- **When not to use:** For the reader draft, not the writer's draft.
- **Example:** Skim: "Remote hires struggle. / In 2019 our office moved. / Chat has many channels. / Written norms fix this." Indexes two and three don't advance the story; rewrite them as "Remote hires can't absorb unwritten norms by watching" and "Chat scatters norms across channels."
- **Sources:** V46 12:12; V47 18:15–22:07.

### 3.19 The map before the terrain
*(his terms: conceptual map before detailed terrain; the four signals of coherence)*
- **What:** Give an overview before detail, like directions that give the heading across town before the turns. Make coherence visible with four signals: clear section boundaries with headings built from key concepts; a short introductory segment; a point sentence at its end; and repeated key concepts, named near the end of the point sentence and reused throughout.
- **Why it works:** Coherence is an experience built in the reader's head. Your own document feels coherent to you because you know how it fits; readers depend only on your signals (V27 33:17–33:44). Motivated readers can struggle through clotted sentences, but incoherence defeats everyone (V27 32:42).
- **Steps:** Apply the four signals to every section and subsection (V27 37:34).
- **Symptoms:** A long run-up before any point; key terms that appear only late; readers who can't follow the line of thought.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** A section opener ending: "...and most resets trace back to three design choices: forced expiry, hidden rules and silent lockouts." Those three terms then open the section's paragraphs.
- **Sources:** V22 32:40–34:30; V27 31:36–37:51.

### 3.20 One theme, two or three points
*(McCarthy's advice, as he reports it: the thread and the needlework)*
- **What:** Decide the theme of the piece and the two or three points every reader must remember. Everything else is needlework that stitches that thread.
- **Why it works:** Sentences and paragraphs are only tools for a larger pattern. In the article McCarthy helped an economist revise, nothing could be cut because everything served the theme (V22 17:32–19:00). Most professional writing fails because it's about too many things at once (V24 14:39).
- **Steps:** 1. Write the theme in a line. 2. List two or three points. 3. For each paragraph, name which point it serves; cut or move anything that serves none.
- **Symptoms:** You can't name what a reader walks away with; more than three "key takeaways"; good tangents.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Theme: "Remote teams fail through silence, not distance." Points: decide in writing; give quiet people a written channel; replace status meetings with async updates. The paragraph on desk ergonomics goes.
- **Sources:** V22 17:32–19:00; V24 14:39.

### 3.21 Key terms, held stable
*(his terms: key terms; constellation; train readers in your naming system)*
- **What:** Name the concepts a unit develops in its index, then return to them by exact repetition, a close synonym, or a member of a named category. Don't give one concept several names for variety. If you need variants, establish them at first mention.
- **Why it works:** When nearly every noun in a discussion links back to a concept in the index, readers sense that everything fits; that feeling is coherence (V46 13:26–15:51). Variation either brings too many characters on stage or gives one concept several names (V24 12:20–14:39). New labels mid-text ("the professor", "the YouTuber") make readers wonder whether a new person has appeared (V19 46:28–47:22). Previewed terms need exact repetition, because readers are holding those slots open (V48 20:31).
- **Steps:**
  1. List the two to four key terms of the unit.
  2. Name them in the index.
  3. Reuse them; allow a category member ("scooters" after "transport rules") or a form of the same word (V32 21:13; V48 20:31).
  4. If you need a second name, show the equivalence at first mention and stick to it (V19 47:22).
  5. Check whether any noun in the discussion fails to link back; if a new concept is needed, announce it in the index (V46 15:51).
- **Symptoms:** "users... customers... clients... members" for one group; synonyms introduced to avoid repetition; readers asking "wait, who is 'the vendor'?"
- **When not to use:** Synonyms can continue a topic string (4.4) but must not rename a previewed or central term (V48 13:14 vs 20:31).
- **Example:** Before: "Our retention strategy... keeping subscribers... loyalty initiatives... reducing attrition." After: "retention" throughout, with "churn" introduced once as its opposite.
- **Sources:** V19 45:45–47:22; V24 12:20–15:13; V27 33:44–37:34; V32 21:13–21:41; V46 13:26–15:51; V48 20:31.

### 3.22 Story, then named concepts, then redeploy
*(his terms: chunking; concepts as chess pieces)*
- **What:** Ask a question; tell a familiar story these readers already know; sum up its lessons in one sentence each; package each lesson as a short named concept; then move those names through the rest of the piece like chess pieces.
- **Why it works:** The name compresses the story into something readers can carry, and each reuse links back to the story, which holds the piece together (V22 23:32–28:15).
- **Steps:** 1. Pick an example your readers know. 2. Tell it briefly. 3. One sentence per lesson. 4. A stable name per lesson. 5. Reuse exactly those names.
- **Symptoms:** Examples told and never summed up; concepts introduced abstractly with no story; names that drift ("lock-in", "entrenchment", "stickiness").
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Story: two bakeries, the one that opened thirty minutes earlier took the commuter trade despite worse bread. Concepts: "first-hour advantage", "habit lock". Later: "Your onboarding email is your first hour."
- **Sources:** V22 23:32–28:15.

## 3E. The argument

### 3.23 Make the claim arguable
- **What:** A claim worth writing is significant (it develops understanding), non-obvious (readers want to see how you got there) and debatable (someone could disagree). A point implies somebody else is wrong.
- **Why it works:** Readers spend effort only for a payoff; an obvious claim costs attention and gives nothing back (V3 8:00–8:57). Nobody needs a book proving water is wet (V17 13:18). A general statement at the top of the abstraction scale is "just true", not a point; one level down it becomes falsifiable (V50 22:13–23:11). Arguments exist because each of us sees only part of the room, and ideas that survive attack become knowledge (V3 4:37–7:30).
- **Steps:** 1. Write the claim as one sentence. 2. Ask: could an informed person disagree? Would readers need evidence? Does it change understanding? 3. If any answer is no, narrow or sharpen it. 4. Name who would disagree (V50 17:54).
- **Symptoms:** Thesis statements that are definitions, truisms or topic announcements; a central idea nobody could dispute; claims hedged until they say nothing (V17 13:18).
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Weak: "Social media affects teenagers." Arguable: "Private group chats, not public feeds, cause most of the social anxiety teenagers report."
- **Sources:** V3 4:05–8:57; V17 13:18–13:36; V25 9:25; V50 17:54, 22:13–23:11.

### 3.24 The anatomy of a point: claim, reason, evidence, bridge
*(his terms: point, reason, evidence; the warrant or bridge)*
- **What:** Every point needs a reason ("point *because* reason"), evidence for the reason that this reader accepts, and an explicit bridge (warrant) explaining why this evidence supports this claim.
- **Why it works:** People who disagree attack your reason, so it must rest on evidence (V50 17:54). Evidence doesn't speak for itself: researchers who accept the same temperature data offer different explanations (V25 10:14). What counts as evidence depends on the reader and genre: citations for some, a well-chosen story for others (V25 9:25; V50 18:24). Cold readers notice the gap between evidence and point that the writer thinks is obvious (V50 19:43–20:20). A claim built this way is open to challenge, which is where writing gets its power (V38 8:29–9:12).
- **Steps:**
  1. Write "[point] because [reason]".
  2. Ask who would disagree; if nobody, there's no point.
  3. List evidence this reader and genre accept.
  4. Check evidence supports reason and reason supports point.
  5. Where a link is only plausible, write the bridge sentence (V50 19:43–21:04).
- **Symptoms:** A "because" clause that points at evidence without connecting it; a quote dropped in as if it proves the claim; evidence of a kind these readers discount; bold claims with no evidence (V3 8:57; V19 1:04:23).
- **When not to use:** The three parts needn't be three sentences, but all three must be present (V50 18:24).
- **Example:** Claim: "Short daily standups hurt senior engineers more than juniors." Evidence: "Seniors log their longest focus blocks in the morning." Bridge: "A 9:30 standup cuts that block in two, and seniors depend on long blocks for design work."
- **Sources:** V3 8:57; V17 6:42–7:38; V19 1:04:23–1:05:09; V25 8:27–11:00; V38 8:29–9:12; V50 17:24–21:38.

### 3.25 Take a position at each contested point
*(his phrase: make the judgment call)*
- **What:** Where the evidence is contested, say what you think it supports. If you lack the standing to be believed, borrow it by quoting someone who has it, but don't skip the judgment.
- **Why it works:** A survey without judgment is an encyclopedia entry that passes on information without evaluating it (V39 3:20–4:58). Readers sometimes want exactly the writer's opinion (V14 1:46). A question-driven piece forces judgment calls that a topic-driven one lets you avoid (V39 3:20).
- **Steps:** 1. At each contested point, decide. 2. Say so plainly. 3. Back it with your reasoning or a borrowed expert.
- **Symptoms:** "Some say X; others say Y" with no verdict; long neutral surveys; a piece that never tells readers what to make of anything.
- **When not to use:** Not stated; calibrate how strongly you say it (6.11).
- **Example:** Before: "Some studies suggest standing desks help; others find no effect." After: "The studies claiming big gains are small and short. Treat them as hints, not findings."
- **Sources:** V14 1:46; V39 3:20–4:58.

### 3.26 Answer objections before they are raised
*(his phrases: good writing is like telepathy, after Stephen King; beat readers to their objections)*
- **What:** For each major claim, anticipate the reader's objections and answer each where it would occur to them. Objections almost always fall into three buckets: other causes; counterexamples or omitted evidence; different definitions of terms. A fourth, for new ideas: "this isn't new".
- **Why it works:** People dislike changing their ideas and even more their behavior, so they look for reasons to doubt (V19 15:13, 57:59). A one-sided argument falls flat; the objection you miss is the one that lets readers off the hook, as in a sales script (V19 58:53–59:30). Considering objections shows the claim has survived attack (V3 8:57–9:31). Stress-testing is what separates an essay from a speech (V43 6:49–7:08). Pre-empting "this is old news" shows a real advance and a fair stance (V22 26:27–27:16).
- **Steps:**
  1. List major claims.
  2. For each, write a response to other causes, counterexamples, and definitions.
  3. For a new idea, name the nearest prior idea and say where it fell short; position yours as extending it.
  4. State the strongest version of each objection fairly; answer it or narrow the claim.
  5. Place each answer just before the reader would raise it.
- **Symptoms:** No "one might object", "this doesn't apply when", or "critics would say" anywhere; long stretches proving the uncontroversial.
- **When not to use:** Idea books and bold positioning can leave some qualification to readers (V17 12:13); even then, answer the objections that come to mind the moment people hear the idea (V17 10:53).
- **Example:** Claim: "Four-day weeks raise output." Other causes: the trial firms were already strong performers, so address selection. Counterexamples: some firms dropped the trial; explain why. Definitions: say whether "output" means billable hours or projects delivered.
- **Sources:** V3 8:57–9:31; V17 10:53, 13:18–13:36; V19 15:13–16:59, 57:59–1:00:28; V20 22:49–23:28; V22 26:27–27:16; V43 6:49–7:08.

### 3.27 Use research to build tension
- **What:** Bring in sources to create conflict, contradiction and complexity, not to show you've read them. For each citation, name what you expect the reader to do with it.
- **Why it works:** "Smith says, Jones argues" shows reading but gives the reader nothing to do; readers don't care what books you've read (V19 41:02–41:54). A literature review succeeds by synthesizing and prioritizing, showing an organizing mind (V1 10:05–11:33; see 8.20).
- **Steps:** 1. For each source, ask its function on the path from A to B. 2. Place sources so they conflict or complicate. 3. Make the situation more problematic, not just better informed.
- **Symptoms:** "As X writes..." followed by a quote nothing follows up; "I read an interesting book" openings.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Before: "Author A says open offices boost collaboration." After: "Author A found open offices increased chance conversations; a later study of similar firms found face-to-face interaction fell by two-thirds. Which should a manager planning a move believe?"
- **Sources:** V1 10:05–11:33; V19 40:04–42:56.

### 3.28 Analysis, not description
*(his image: the watch)*
- **What:** Description names the parts (this is the minute hand, this is the second hand). Analysis shows how it works, why, and what follows for the reader's problem (how the gears move the hands and match our experience of time).
- **Why it works:** Asked about a watch, a school essay tours the parts; a university essay explains how it works (V3 2:32–3:43). It is analysis, not information, that makes writing valuable (V12 47:33).
- **Steps:** 1. After any list of facts, ask how they connect, why, and what it means for the reader. 2. Write that answer. 3. Organize around those answers, not the parts.
- **Symptoms:** One paragraph per component; topic sentences of the form "X is..." or "X has..."; no "because", "which means" or "so"; sentences a reader could get from a spec sheet.
- **When not to use:** When the assignment asks for a summary. Analysis is not a call to write more (V3 2:32).
- **Example:** Before: "The dashboard shows signups, churn and revenue." After: "The three lines tell one story: signups rise every month, churn rises faster, so revenue stays flat."
- **Sources:** V1 10:05–10:40; V3 2:32–3:43; V12 46:41–47:33.

## 3F. Holding it together and ending

### 3.29 Filter every element by value
*(his term for the last pass: a value audit)*
- **What:** Keep or cut each paragraph, quote, story and section according to what it does for the reader, not because it is true, interesting or hard-won. Ask of each section: what should the reader understand, feel or do differently after it?
- **Why it works:** Weak content dilutes your best ideas and wastes attention, the most precious resource you have (V19 1:07:30). The purpose chain is the razor (V18 13:23–14:32). If you can't answer the section test, cut the section (V17 38:16).
- **Steps:** 1. For each element, ask why this reader should care and what problem it solves. 2. If you can't name the value, rewrite or delete. 3. Watch for your own defenses: "but it's true", "it's interesting", "I spent a week on that".
- **Symptoms:** Tangents that interested the author; a fascinating aside in a practical guide.
- **When not to use:** Not during drafting; cutting early starves the draft (6.1).
- **Example:** A lovely paragraph on the etymology of "deadline" in a guide to project scoping. True, interesting, and it goes.
- **Sources:** V12 37:20; V17 38:16; V18 13:23–14:32; V19 1:05:58–1:07:30; V22 17:32–19:00.

### 3.30 Coherence at every level: the orphan check
*(his image: the 3D cube puzzle)*
- **What:** Writing is like a 3D cube puzzle: each piece must fit its neighbors at every level (sentence, paragraph, section, chapter) for the whole to hold. Any unit that links neither back nor forward is an orphan.
- **Why it works:** A sentence can be fine alone yet leave the reader lost if it doesn't link backward and lean forward (V18 15:57–18:31). Readers assume every piece is there for a reason; if the link is missing, they invent one, blame themselves, or skip (V18 18:31–20:19).
- **Steps:** 1. At each level, check that each unit links back and sets up what comes next. 2. Mark any unit that could be moved elsewhere without anyone noticing. 3. Add the link or cut the orphan.
- **Symptoms:** "No thread; I kept forgetting what I'd read"; a vivid paragraph with no link to the chapter's promise.
- **When not to use:** Exploratory "pantser" drafting is fine for thinking; compile with structure (V18 16:48–17:41).
- **Example:** A paragraph about a monk's daily routine dropped into a chapter on pricing, with no sentence tying it to pricing.
- **Sources:** V8 8:59; V18 15:26–20:19.

### 3.31 Plant and call back
*(his terms: setup and payoff; the chord; reprise with variations)*
- **What:** Introduce an image, term or concept early and bring it back later with new meaning. A motif that recurs across a piece is where its real music comes from.
- **Why it works:** A payoff is impossible without a setup (V39 16:37). A closing metaphor that would be merely clever on its own lands because the piece prepared it (V39 14:14–14:33). Each echo makes earlier material resonate; he cites a Joyce motif that recurs in three different forms (V42 2:12–3:31). Callbacks reward attention and show change without announcing it (V21 8:40–9:25, 33:46–34:19). Returning to earlier material, changed, gives an essay a sense of wholeness (V43 7:55).
- **Steps:** 1. List everything you planted. 2. Make each return at least once, transformed. 3. Cut plants that never pay off. 4. Plant anything the ending relies on.
- **Symptoms:** A striking early image never mentioned again; an ending that depends on something the reader never met.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** An essay on hospital efficiency opens on a nurse's stopwatch, returns to the countdown in the scheduling software, and ends on a patient counting minutes to discharge.
- **Sources:** V21 8:40–9:25, 22:33, 27:01, 33:46–34:19; V39 13:36–16:37; V42 2:12–3:31; V43 7:55.

### 3.32 The conclusion that pushes off higher
- **What:** Don't summarize. Return to the problem you posed, show briefly that your idea resolves it, then push off to a broader question the reader cares about, ending at a higher level of generality than you began.
- **Why it works:** Summaries treat information as the value; if value is solving a problem, show the solution working and what it opens up (V46 16:18). Ending higher gives forward thrust rather than "see, told you so" (V46 18:11–18:38). The same rule governs each section's end (V50 27:00; see 4.3).
- **Steps:** 1. Restate the problem at a high level, not word for word. 2. Show how the idea resolves it. 3. Ask what the solved problem changes about a larger issue. 4. End there.
- **Symptoms:** "In summary..."; points restated at the same level as the introduction; the piece stops at "so the thesis is true".
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Before: "In summary, remote onboarding fails for three reasons." After: "Once the norms are written down, onboarding stops depending on who sits near whom. Which raises a larger question: how much of any company's culture has lived only in its hallways?"
- **Sources:** V18 24:03–25:41; V43 7:55; V46 16:18–19:48; V50 27:00–27:22.

# Stage 4. Paragraphs

How a unit is developed and how its sentences hang together. He says around 80% of clarity comes from organizing information within sentences and paragraphs (V19 42:56), and that readability is an organizational property, not a grammatical one (V48 3:00).

### 4.1 One paragraph, one job
*(McCarthy's rule, as he reports it: a paragraph is limited to one message)*
- **What:** A paragraph is the unit of one idea. You should be able to label it in a few words, and underline the sentence that states its point.
- **Why it works:** A paragraph doing two jobs splits the reader's attention; a list of labels read alone should hold together (V22 19:00–20:27). A paragraph can be a single sentence when its job fits one sentence, usually a transition. Putting every sentence on a new line, a common online habit, makes each look like a new idea (V22 20:27–21:00).
- **Steps:**
  1. Write a label in the margin for each paragraph.
  2. Two labels: split. Two paragraphs with one label: merge.
  3. Underline the main point. If you can't, rework the paragraph. If you can, move it to the top and rewrite the rest to follow from it (V18 22:07–22:33).
  4. Read the labels alone in order.
- **Symptoms:** Nothing underlinable, or two competing points; a run of one-line paragraphs that are really one idea; the best sentence sits sixth.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** A paragraph whose sixth sentence is "The cheapest fix is a checklist." Promote it to first and cut what doesn't support it.
- **Sources:** V18 22:07–22:33; V21 15:32; V22 19:00–21:00.

### 4.2 Value at the top of every paragraph
*(his exercise: circle the value words)*
- **What:** State each paragraph's and section's value, why it matters to the reader, at its start, where readers look for it. Diagnose by circling value words (opportunity, cost, urgency, turn words, community code words).
- **Why it works:** Readers look in particular places for particular information: the action in the verb, and at paragraph level, the value at the start (V19 51:23–52:56). It's a social contract, not a copywriting trick: you reassure readers up front that their attention will be repaid (V19 52:56–53:46). Readers don't value topics; they value problems (V46 11:27).
- **Steps:**
  1. Put the value in the paragraph's index.
  2. Circle every value word in the draft (V19 54:13).
  3. You want a dense cluster at the beginning and at least one in the first sentence of each paragraph.
  4. A long stretch with nothing to circle marks a problem.
- **Symptoms:** Paragraphs opening with a date, a name or a transition, with the stakes buried mid-paragraph; paragraphs with no "but", no stakes words, nothing at risk (V19 51:23).
- **When not to use:** Putting the value at the end is an advanced storytelling choice. Never bury it in the middle (V19 53:46–54:13).
- **Example:** Before: "In 2021 we moved to a new ticketing tool, and after some adjustment response times fell." After: "A new ticketing tool cut our response times by a third, but only after we changed one habit."
- **Sources:** V19 49:20–54:13; V46 11:27.

### 4.3 The uneven U
*(term he credits to Eric Hayot; his scale of specificity from 5, most abstract, to 1, raw evidence)*
- **What:** Develop a paragraph or section by descending and climbing a scale of specificity: open with an arguable point (level 4), explain it (3), prepare the reader for the evidence (2), give the evidence (1), interpret it (3), return to the level of the point (4), and push off to the larger question the unit serves (5). Ending higher than you began is what makes the U uneven.
- **Why it works:**
  - Level 5 is so general it's "just true"; level 4 is a point someone could dispute (V50 22:13–23:11).
  - Very specific information is hard to process without context, so level 2 sets it up, for example by echoing the evidence's key term (V50 24:01–24:47).
  - Opening a point with a surprising statistic or quote is a poor way in, since readers have no context for it (V50 24:25).
  - Climbing back up prevents the **hit-and-run quotation** *(term he credits to* They Say / I Say*)*: evidence dropped and left for the reader to deal with (V50 24:47–25:19).
  - Ending higher gives momentum into the next unit instead of circling back (V18 24:55–25:41), and links each section to the larger argument it serves (V50 27:00).
- **Steps:**
  1. Open with a level-4 point. Test: could someone say "how can you claim that?"
  2. Explain what you mean.
  3. Set up the evidence.
  4. Give it. For several pieces, alternate 2-1-2-1 (V50 29:31).
  5. Interpret: what it shows and how it bears on the point.
  6. Return to the level of the point, not word for word. A closing sentence can echo the paragraph's opening idea (V37 21:04).
  7. Push off to the larger question.
- **Symptoms:** A paragraph that ends on a quote, number or anecdote; evidence never mentioned again; paragraphs that end where they began; a platitude as the opening sentence.
- **When not to use:** He admits the model reflects his training in literary criticism; adapt it to your field (V50 28:54).
- **Example:** [4] "Hospital handoffs fail less from missing information than from information in the wrong order." [3] "A nurse starting a shift needs the next hour's risks first." [2] "You can see this in how handoff sheets order their lines." [1] "One ward's sheet reaches the 8 p.m. dose on line fourteen." [3] "The urgent item is there, but a tired reader meets it last." [4] "The fix is a different order: risks first, history after." [5] "Many failures we blame on memory may really be failures of sequence."
- **Sources:** V18 23:07–25:41; V37 21:04; V46 17:26–18:11; V50 21:38–30:18.

### 4.4 A consistent cast in the topic position
*(his terms: topic; topic position; topic string; strategic repetition; the two skis; the camera; the stage)*
- **What:** Readers take whatever comes first in a sentence as what it is about. Keep a small cast of two or three characters or concepts in that position across a paragraph, by repetition, pronoun or close synonym. Change the topic only on purpose. Never vary sentence openings just for variety.
- **Why it works:**
  - Readers ride two skis at once: what this sentence is about, and what the passage is about. When sentence topics jump, the skis split (V9 1:55–4:24).
  - The subject position is a spotlight or camera; a scene holds two or three characters, four at most, and a new subject each time is a new character acting each time (V18 28:19–29:43; V19 44:22–45:45; V22 12:10–13:04; V24 13:14–18:11).
  - Changing topic for no reason is like a guest who answers news of a friend's new business with a remark on the weather (V26 6:30–7:29).
  - "Avoid repetition" is grade-school advice that looks only at the sentence (V9 5:35; V24 12:49; V26 5:48–6:30; V32 0:00–0:23). A passage where one character does one thing after another reads smoothly (V19 43:50–44:22).
- **Steps:**
  1. Decide what the paragraph is about.
  2. Choose which characters go in topic positions, by what the reader cares about (5.9) and what is already familiar (5.7) (V19 47:22–49:20).
  3. Make them the grammatical subjects; move other information into objects or subordinate clauses (V22 11:17–13:04).
  4. Keep topics short, concrete and familiar (V9 4:24–5:35).
  5. Use the passive, *since*, or an opening phrase to get the right character to the front (5.10, 5.12).
  6. Check with the diagnostics in 4.5.
- **Symptoms:** Each sentence opens with a different kind of thing (a date, an abstraction, a name, a participle); synonyms brought in to avoid repeating a word; readers calling the prose dense or disorganized, "like watching a tennis match" (V32 3:50).
- **When not to use:** A deliberate switch is fine (V26 9:19); the whole-to-parts pattern varies topics legitimately under a named umbrella (4.8). Most sentences, not all (V27 20:05).
- **Example:** Before: "The committee met in March. A decision was reached by members. Budget limits shaped the outcome. Staff were told in April." After: "The committee met in March. It decided to cut the pilot, citing budget limits, and told staff in April."
- **Sources:** V9 1:55–6:01; V15 12:29; V18 28:19–29:43; V19 43:50–49:20; V22 11:17–13:04, 16:29–17:32; V24 12:20–18:11; V26 5:48–9:19; V27 15:07–21:11; V32 0:00–7:46, 13:05, 24:26; V37 21:54–22:52; V48 3:00–4:02.

### 4.5 Topic diagnostics
*(his tests: underline the first seven or eight words; the movie-poster test; the topic-only test; colors and lines)*
- **What:** Mechanical checks for the topic string, because rereading by feel won't catch it (7.1).
  - **Underline test:** underline the first seven or eight words of every sentence or clause, up to the verb. Read only those.
  - **Movie poster:** who is the star of this passage? Could you draw them?
  - **Colors and lines:** mark characters in one color, actions in another, function words in gray; draw lines linking each mention of the same thing. Lines should run down the left side (the topics), not zigzag.
- **Why it works:** Reader attention trains on sentence openings (V32 4:29). Neighboring openings that share a small concrete cast let readers zoom out and see the whole (V32 5:46). Fronting different entities changes whose story readers think they're in (V32 1:14–2:14). Crossing lines are a sign something has gone wrong (V37 4:53).
- **Steps:**
  1. Underline openings up to the verb.
  2. From those words alone, could someone predict what the passage is about? (V27 20:05; V32 4:49; V48 3:00)
  3. Could you draw them? (V32 5:16)
  4. Would your readers, not just you, see them as related, and do they name the most important characters? (V27 20:05)
  5. If any check fails, rewrite so subjects name the passage's recurring characters.
- **Symptoms:** Underlined fragments share nothing; they are mostly abstract nouns; the poster could only show an abstraction; lines zigzag between starts and ends of sentences.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Openings "Budget overruns / In Q3, a vendor review / Delays / Staff turnover" share nothing. Rewritten: "The project / The project team / Its vendor / The team".
- **Sources:** V27 17:46–21:11; V32 0:23–5:46; V37 4:53–5:39, 21:54–22:52; V48 1:02–4:02.

### 4.6 End each sentence on what the next picks up
*(his images: the relay race; the swimmer pushing off the wall; sentences shaking hands; the Busan taxi driver)*
- **What:** Begin each sentence with something the reader already has, often from the end of the previous sentence, and end it on what the next sentence will pick up. When revising a sentence, look ahead at the next one.
- **Why it works:** Readers read a passage, not isolated sentences; each has to show how it fits, like pieces of a wooden puzzle (V8 8:59). The end of a sentence is still in the reader's mind when the next begins, like a ripple (V26 4:55). Without handoffs, prose lurches like a taxi driver pumping the accelerator: sentences fine alone, sickening together (V27 11:43–13:56). He sums the principle up with a German proverb about the beginning and end shaking hands (V15 17:20). Flowing prose lets readers lose themselves (V8 11:53).
- **Steps:**
  1. Read sentences in pairs.
  2. Does the start of B pick up the end of A (same word, pronoun, synonym, summary phrase, or a category member)?
  3. If not, reorder B, often with the passive (5.10), so the shared element comes first.
  4. Check that B ends on what C needs.
  5. When an interrupting clause could go at either end of a sentence, place at the end whichever element the next sentence picks up (V12 49:26–51:02).
  6. Put contrast transitions such as "however" at the head of the sentence, between the two ideas (V15 11:34).
- **Symptoms:** A sentence that opens on a noun the reader has never met, right after one that ended on something else; choppy prose where each sentence reads well alone.
- **When not to use:** Handoffs alone can produce a smooth chain that goes nowhere (4.7).
- **Example:** Before: "In March the clinic began texting patients the day before appointments. The manager says missed visits had been costing thousands." After: "In March the clinic began texting each patient a reminder the day before. Those reminders cut missed visits by a third."
- **Sources:** V8 8:46–11:53; V9 6:01–9:17; V12 49:26–51:02; V15 7:58–12:29, 17:20; V26 4:55–5:48, 13:21; V27 11:43–15:07; V32 8:32–10:31, 21:41–22:37.

### 4.7 Cohesion is not coherence
*(his terms: cohesion; coherence)*
- **What:** Cohesion is the pieces fitting together; coherence is the pieces forming the picture on the box. A passage can hand off perfectly and still be about nothing.
- **Why it works:** His example drifts from a city to coffee to machines to a typewriter to a professor, each link valid (V27 15:42–16:34; similar demo V48 10:24–11:15). He names three causes of incoherence: subjects unrelated to each other; no shared family of theme words; no point that unifies the passage (V27 16:34).
- **Steps:** After checking handoffs (4.6): 1. Do the subjects form one small cast? 2. Do the key content words come from one family? 3. Is there a stated point? Fix whichever fails.
- **Symptoms:** Smooth, linked prose whose subject nobody can name.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** "...caught by gluten. Gluten reminds me of the paste we used in art class. My art teacher banned glitter. Glitter still turns up in my car." Each link holds; the passage has no subject.
- **Sources:** V27 15:07–21:11; V48 10:24–12:20.

### 4.8 Choose a topic-progression pattern by purpose
*(two of the four are his terms: the linking pattern and the theme preview; the other two he describes without naming)*
- **What:** Four patterns for how topics move through a passage. Each suits a purpose and has a typical failure. They work at paragraph scale, scale up to sections, and nest inside each other (V48 21:39).
- **Why it works:** All four rest on topic and comment: the topic is a doorstep, the comment the room the reader steps into (V48 4:02).

  **(a) One topic held constant.** The same element (or a pronoun for it) stays in every topic slot while each comment adds a facet: the reader stands on the doorstep and looks in each direction (V48 4:02–4:58).
  - Use for: describing, characterizing, developing a many-sided idea about one thing.
  - Failure: it can feel stagnant; reserve it for when you really need to unpack one entity (V48 5:58).
  - Example: "Mangroves line tropical coasts. They blunt storm surges. They shelter young fish. They lock carbon into the mud faster than most forests."

  **(b) The linking pattern.** Each new topic picks up something from the previous comment, so the reader moves from room to room (V48 6:53–8:35).
  - Use for: movement through space, methods, processes, causal chains (V48 9:29).
  - Failure: like railroad tracks, it can carry you far off course. Test: list the topics and ask whether they could all appear as characters on one stage (V48 11:15–12:20). Looping back to the start is a nice touch, not a requirement.
  - Example: "Every loaf starts with a starter. The starter is a paste colonized by wild yeast. That yeast releases gas as it feeds, and the gas is trapped by gluten."

  **(c) The whole first, then its parts.** An early clause names a whole or category; later clauses take its parts as topics. Once you're inside a cathedral, you aren't surprised by its nave and crypt (V48 14:52).
  - Use for: taxonomies, categorizing, breaking one thing into parts (V48 14:52–16:01).
  - Failure: the missing umbrella. If the category is only implied, the clauses read as disjointed; one index clause naming the whole makes them snap into place (V48 16:01–18:16).
  - Example: add "Every station in our kitchen is laid out the same way, so a cook never crosses the room mid-task" before four sentences about the board, knives, towel and bin.

  **(d) The theme preview.** An index clause's comment names A, B and C; each is then taken up in turn as a topic, like a tour guide naming the rooms ahead (V48 18:16–20:31).
  - Use for: weaving several strands into one thread. At section scale, give each strand several paragraphs and use "first, second, third" (V48 21:39; enumerators pointing back to an index also in V37 11:33–12:26).
  - Failure: synonyms for the previewed terms. Readers holding three slots must work out whether a new word is a new strand. Only forms of the same word are allowed (V48 20:31).
  - Example: "Hospitals lose nurses for three reasons: pay, scheduling and isolation." Units open "Pay...", "Scheduling...", "Isolation...", not "Compensation...", "Rostering...", "Loneliness...".
- **Steps:** Name the passage's purpose (characterize, move, decompose, weave), pick the pattern, write the index clause it needs, then check for that pattern's failure.
- **Symptoms:** "Nothing is wrong but something is off" (V48 2:00); a topic list naming a new element in almost every clause.
- **When not to use:** Don't mix patterns within one short paragraph without reason (inferred); nest them across levels instead.
- **Example:** One per pattern, above. Nested: a section on nurse turnover opens with a theme preview (pay, scheduling, isolation); the scheduling paragraphs then use the linking pattern to trace how one rota change cascades into missed childcare.
- **Sources:** V9 1:55–5:35 (constant topic); V15 10:09–12:29; V37 7:25, 11:33–12:26; V48 0:00–21:39.

### 4.9 Signal the zoom-in; enumerate heavy lists
- **What:** Use signposts sparingly, but use them where structure really needs navigation: when a passage leaves its main thread to illustrate it ("for example"), and when a list of heavy, complex items hangs off the paragraph's index ("first... second... third...").
- **Why it works:** Dropping into an example breaks the topic string; a signpost tells readers this is an illustration, not a new thread (V32 17:01–17:37). Numbered links keep heavy information attached to the governing idea (V37 11:59–12:26). Transitions lose force when overused (V32 17:37; 6.18).
- **Steps:** 1. Find stretches where a key concept drops out of the topic string. 2. If it's an example, open with "for example" or similar. 3. For heavy lists, put the category at the end of the first sentence and open each item with an enumerator.
- **Symptoms:** A run of specific detail with no marker, after which readers ask "why are we talking about this?"; or transitions on every sentence.
- **When not to use:** He dislikes scattered signposts generally (V37 11:59).
- **Example:** "Teams that skip retrospectives repeat mistakes. For example, our payments team shipped the same rounding bug three times last year."
- **Sources:** V32 17:01–18:32; V37 11:33–12:26.

### 4.10 Make logical links explicit
*(he cites Grice's maxims; his terms from V24: logical bridges; cores)*
- **What:** When you put two statements side by side, readers assume a relationship. State it with a connector (because, therefore, however, although, for example) unless it is painfully obvious.
- **Why it works:** Readers can read only on the assumption that each sentence follows for a reason (V18 18:31–19:21). If you don't state the link, they won't shrug; they'll guess, blame themselves, or stop reading (V18 19:21–20:19; V24 18:11–20:59). If smart people feel stupid reading your work, the problem is the writing (V24 18:11). A clear character plus a clear action makes a core clause, and logical bridges join the cores (V24 21:04).
- **Steps:** 1. For each adjacent pair, say aloud why B follows A. 2. Add the connector that states it: cause, condition, contrast, comparison, example. 3. If you can't state a link, move B. 4. When rebuilding a tangled sentence, decide how its clauses relate before joining them (V8 6:33–8:06).
- **Symptoms:** Sentences simply lined up; readers who "don't see the connection" or misread the point.
- **When not to use:** When the relationship is painfully obvious (V18 23:07).
- **Example:** "Sleep debt accumulates. Most offices keep the lights on until 9 p.m." becomes "Sleep debt accumulates, and office design makes it worse: most offices keep the lights on until 9 p.m."
- **Sources:** V8 6:33–8:06; V18 18:31–23:07; V24 18:11–21:04.

### 4.11 Every sentence has a nameable job
*(his image: every sentence is a tile in a path)*
- **What:** Beneath the words is a layer of functions. Name what each sentence and paragraph does on the path from A to B: introduces a problem, contrasts with the last idea, raises an objection, supplies evidence. If you can't name the job, cut it.
- **Why it works:** The same sentence does different jobs in different places: "sales fell 15%" can be the problem, the evidence or background (V19 19:59). Text built from well-patterned words that answer no reader question looks like language but talks to nobody (V19 19:38–20:33; V20 21:22).
- **Steps:** 1. Tag each sentence or paragraph with its function. 2. Delete or rewrite any you can only describe by topic.
- **Symptoms:** Sentences that are "interesting" but do nothing; paragraphs whose role can only be described by their subject matter.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** "Churn rose to 8% last quarter" as problem (opening a pitch), as evidence (supporting "the price rise backfired"), or as one number among many (background). Know which it is.
- **Sources:** V19 18:46–21:25; V20 17:08–21:38.

### 4.12 Order examples same, same, different
*(his name: AA-B, from the comedy rule of three)*
- **What:** When developing an idea with examples, give two similar examples that establish it, then a contrasting one that challenges or deepens it.
- **Why it works:** With A-B-A, the contradiction muddies the idea before the reader has it. Two setup beats build a base, so the twist lands harder, like a punchline (V12 45:38–46:41).
- **Steps:** Order examples same, same, different; put the complication last.
- **Symptoms:** An example qualified at once and then returned to, leaving the reader unsure of the point.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** "Short onboarding worked at Firm 1 and Firm 3. At Firm 2, with a far more complex product, it failed. So the method depends on complexity."
- **Sources:** V12 45:38–46:41.

### 4.13 The intuition pump
*(his term)*
- **What:** Instead of a plain example that supplies evidence, give a scenario readers can run in their heads, so they reach the conclusion themselves. Extend it with a contrast where helpful ("not A but B").
- **Why it works:** It puts the reader in the detective's seat and links the abstract point to a situation they can reason through (V22 28:15–30:25). His examples from an economics article: a flight's safe arrival depending on the captain rather than the crew; the new economy as a casino, not a factory, where choosing which game to play is part of the skill.
- **Steps:** 1. State the question. 2. Find a familiar situation with the same structure. 3. Describe it vividly. 4. State the implication.
- **Symptoms:** Abstract assertions readers must take on trust; examples that illustrate but don't let the reader reason.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Does a three-person startup need a style guide? "Picture a family cooking dinner: nobody writes down who chops the onions. Now picture a kitchen serving 200 covers with no ticket rail." Rules pay off at scale.
- **Sources:** V22 28:15–30:25.

# Stage 5. Sentences

His sentence teaching rests on a small model of reading: readers move left to right, hold only three or four items in working memory, carry a compressed "mental briefcase" from one sentence to the next (the last words, the topic, sensory impressions, open expectations), and picture what they read (V26 1:32–4:55; V6 8:49; V9 1:55). Every entry below serves that model. Two principles do most of the work: characters as subjects with actions as verbs, and old before new (V37 3:13–4:53).

## 5A. Who does what

### 5.1 Ask what the sentence is for
*(his line: great sentences are successful sentences)*
- **What:** Judge a sentence by whether it achieves its intended effect on this reader at this point, not by rules. When a sentence feels wrong, stop inspecting its grammar and ask what it should do and what the reader should see.
- **Why it works:** Readers read to understand the world, not to admire grammar (V10 6:04). Asked about their best meal, people describe the occasion, not the ingredients; grammar is a necessary ingredient but not what makes a sentence work (V10 2:27–4:40). A deliberately terrible cover song works on a show about annoying music because it plays against context (V10 4:40–5:18). Rule-based advice is like flat-pack instructions for a window frame when the point is the view (V10 6:04).
- **Steps:** 1. Say in one line what the sentence should do (define, give evidence, turn the argument). 2. Say what the reader should picture. 3. Rewrite toward that.
- **Symptoms:** Correct but empty sentences ("It is important to note that..."); fussing over comma versus semicolon in a sentence whose purpose is unclear.
- **When not to use:** A break that confuses the intended reader fails his own test, since meeting expectations is part of success (inferred from V10 4:40). Mechanics still matter at the final proofread (6.20).
- **Example:** Before: "It is important to note that there are a number of considerations regarding the timeline." What should the reader see? Two risks. After: "Two things could delay us: the permit and the steel supplier."
- **Sources:** V10 0:00–6:56.

### 5.2 Characters as subjects, actions as verbs
*(his terms: agent, process, goal; characters; storytelling at the level of the sentence)*
- **What:** Every sentence, even in academic writing, is a small story of someone or something doing something. Put the character in the grammatical subject and the action in the verb.
- **Why it works:** Syntax (subject, verb, object) and meaning (agent, action, what is acted on) line up in a plain active sentence; when they don't, readers must rebuild the story (V6 6:50–9:42). Readers expect the doer first and can't picture a scene until the agent arrives (V6 8:49). Subject and verb are the top of the sentence's hierarchy (V12 32:10–33:24). Readers call a sentence clear when they find the someone in the subject and the something done in the verb (V27 4:54). Characters and actions let readers see a movie in the mind's eye (V6 11:07; V12 48:04–48:28).
- **Steps:**
  1. Ask who or what the sentence is about, and what happens.
  2. Find where that character sits grammatically; move it to the subject. Check it can actually perform the verb (V27 3:08).
  3. Check the action is in the main verb, not hidden in a noun or after "is/was" (5.3).
  4. Watch openings like "That is the reason...", "This is why...", "There are many factors...": an abstract pronoun or dummy subject with "be" has the same flaw (V6 10:41; V18 33:25–34:19).
  5. An abstraction can be a character if readers know it well or if you make it the subject of verbs that tell a story (V9 12:44–13:32; V27 9:21).
- **Symptoms:** Characters buried in "on the part of X", "by X" or "X's [noun]"; abstract nouns in most subject slots; "There was worry that..."; sentences nobody can picture.
- **When not to use:** When the character would be new information and a familiar non-human topic is old, keeping the old topic can win (V37 13:39–14:29; see 5.9). Dummy subjects are not always wrong (V18 34:19). Don't confuse this with big-picture storytelling frameworks, which he sets aside for clarity work (V27 0:41–1:35).
- **Example:** Before: "An evaluation of the vendor's proposal on the part of our team resulted in its rejection." After: "Our team evaluated the vendor's proposal and rejected it."
- **Sources:** V6 6:03–12:04; V8 2:52–6:33; V9 12:44–13:32; V12 32:10–33:24, 47:33–48:28; V18 33:25–34:19; V24 15:13–18:11, 20:59–21:04; V27 0:41–5:34; V37 3:13–4:21.

### 5.3 Zombie nouns and empty verbs: the three-step fix
*(his terms: zombie nouns; nominalizations; empty verbs; his "three-step formula")*
- **What:** A zombie noun is an action turned into a noun (*implementation, reduction, detection*): the action's soul has left its body. It needs an empty verb to carry it (*occurred, took place, resulted in, was undertaken*). Put the action back in a verb.
- **Why it works:** The verb is the beating heart of the sentence; readers look there to learn what's happening (V8 3:48). When actions become nouns, flabby verbs carry no action and nobody is visibly doing anything (V27 4:16). Nominalizations combined with negatives and passives make the hardest sentences of all (V29 18:00).
- **Steps (his three steps):**
  1. **Find each action and make it a verb.** Suffixes *-tion, -ment, -ence, -ance* are a good sign, not a complete test (V8 4:39). Swap vague verbs for precise ones ("was the result of" → *caused*). Drop verbs that only mean "be" (V8 4:39–5:50).
  2. **Ask who does each action and make that character the subject.** If the agent is unknown, use the passive (V8 5:50–6:33).
  3. **Decide how the clauses relate, and join them** with a word that shows the logic: *because, since, as, although,* an *-ing* phrase for a consequence, a modal like *could* for "allowed X to" (V8 6:33–8:06).
  Delete the empty verbs that were carrying the nouns (V27 3:08–5:34). If an empty verb was carrying a causal link, move that meaning into a connector or adverb (V32 11:43).
- **Symptoms:** "X of Y occurred"; "There is a need for an X of the Y"; "of" chains; *resulted in, led to, took place*; the real action in object position (V26 9:19–11:54; V32 10:31–11:43).
- **When not to use:** Some nominalizations do legitimate work (5.4). Some suffix words aren't zombies at all (V8 4:39).
- **Example:** Before: "The implementation of the new rota resulted in a reduction of overtime costs." Step 1: implement, reduce. Step 2: managers implemented; costs fell. Step 3: cause and effect. After: "When managers introduced the new rota, overtime costs fell."
- **Sources:** V8 2:52–8:06; V12 47:33–48:28; V24 20:59–21:04; V26 9:19–11:54; V27 3:08–5:34; V29 18:00–19:02; V32 10:31–11:43, 18:32–19:16.

### 5.4 Four good uses of nominalizations
- **What:** Nominalizations are the main cause of unclear prose but not always wrong. Keep one when it:
  1. **links back** as a subject that sums up the previous clause ("That sale paid for the lab");
  2. **replaces "the fact that"** or "the idea that" (though a character-and-verb rewrite often works as well);
  3. **serves as an object** of a verb ("approved her proposal"). The key rule: nominalizations work as objects, not as subjects;
  4. **names a concept so familiar to these readers** that it acts as a character.
- **Why it works:** Languages keep only forms that serve a purpose, so nominalizations must be useful for something (V27 6:11). In the subject slot they pretend to be characters doing things they can't do: proposals don't arrive, people send them (V27 8:31–9:21). A transitive verb needs an object, and a clause there is awkward, so a noun can be the better choice (V29 13:34).
- **Steps (his triage):** 1. Circle each nominalization. 2. Does it hide an important action? Make it a verb and ask who does it. 3. Is it familiar enough to act as a character? Keep it. 4. Does it link back, replace "the fact that", or serve as an object? Keep it. 5. Is it a subject pretending to act? Rewrite with a real character.
- **Symptoms:** A nominalization as subject with a verb it can't perform ("The recommendation reached the committee").
- **When not to use:** The skill is telling hidden actions apart from familiar concepts (V27 11:03).
- **Example:** "There was a requirement for a review of the vendor contracts" becomes "We had to review the vendor contracts"; "contracts" stays. For software engineers, "Deployment failed at the last step" is fine as it is.
- **Sources:** V27 5:34–11:43; V29 13:34.

### 5.5 Short subject, early verb
*(his images: the tunnel; the locomotive; the runway)*
- **What:** Keep the grammatical subject short so the main verb comes early, usually within the first several words. Put the long, heavy, technical material after the verb.
- **Why it works:** Readers hold their breath until the verb, like children in a car tunnel; a long subject is a long tunnel (V8 0:42–2:27). They must hold the subject in short-term memory until the verb tells them how the parts relate (V12 0:41–1:31). Readers build momentum over the first nine or ten words; obstacles on the runway keep the sentence from taking off (V27 21:35–22:16). Working memory holds only three or four items (V26 3:37). With a core clause up front, details hook on behind like train cars (V12 0:41). He insists this is how readers read, not his taste (V27 24:06).
- **Steps:**
  1. Find the main verb; count the words before it.
  2. If the subject is long, find the character (often hidden in a possessive such as "the CEO's belief") and make it the subject.
  3. Turn the buried noun into the verb ("conclusion" → *concluded*); turn an evaluative adjective into an adverb if needed ("an inevitable one" → *inevitably*) (V37 17:02–19:15).
  4. Move qualifying material ("of why...", "which...") after the verb.
  5. Default shape: a short opening phrase, a short concrete subject, a specific verb, then everything long and new (V27 24:06).
- **Symptoms:** Subjects of ten to fifteen or more words; verbs arriving in the last third; technical terms in the opening words; endings like "...is presented here" or "...was carried out".
- **When not to use:** Short sentences don't need it (V12 1:31). Length after the verb is fine; a sentence can run for lines if well built (V27 24:45).
- **Example:** Before: "The CEO's belief that the company could not let a competitor ship an AI feature first was the reason for the rushed launch." After: "The CEO rushed the launch because she believed the company couldn't let a competitor ship an AI feature first."
- **Sources:** V8 0:42–2:27; V12 0:41–2:26; V26 1:32–4:55; V27 21:11–25:12; V37 16:40–19:35.

### 5.6 Move long openers; don't split verb from object
- **What:** Two placement fixes. First, when a sentence opens with a long "because..." or "since..." clause before its main point, move the clause after the main clause or into its own sentence. Second, don't wedge an interrupting clause between a verb and its object; move it to the start or end, choosing by what the next sentence picks up.
- **Why it works:** A long introductory clause makes readers hold details while waiting for the point (V12 48:28–49:26). An interruption between verb and object turns the sentence into a mental obstacle course (V12 49:26–50:23). Ending on whatever the next sentence starts with keeps the handoff (V12 50:23–51:02; see 4.6).
- **Steps:** 1. Find the main clause and put it first. 2. Spot anything between a verb and its object. 3. Read the start of the next sentence. 4. End this sentence with whichever element the next picks up.
- **Symptoms:** Sentences whose first fifteen words are a subordinate clause; commas splitting a verb from its object.
- **When not to use:** When the opening clause is short and links back to the previous sentence, keeping it first can help (see "since" in 5.12).
- **Example:** Before: "We should, if we want to keep our best engineers, fix the on-call rotation." If the next sentence is about the rotation: "If we want to keep our best engineers, we should fix the on-call rotation."
- **Sources:** V12 48:28–51:02.

## 5B. Old, new and emphasis

### 5.7 Every sentence needs old and new, in that order
*(his terms: old to new; topic and comment; his battery image)*
- **What:** Each sentence should carry something the reader already knows (to attach to) and something they don't (the payoff), like a battery with two ends. Put the old, familiar, simple information first and the new, complex information last.
- **Why it works:** Readers learn by attaching new leaves to branches they already have (V15 2:09–2:58). All old and readers tune out; all new and they get lost, like a paving tile with no path (V15 4:19–7:03). Reverse the order and readers keep backtracking; the references become spaghetti with the sauce underneath (V15 12:00). A familiar start gives a foothold for complexity later (V32 7:46; V37 19:15). The topic runs from the sentence's start to the end of its subject; the comment is everything from the verb on (V37 2:34).
- **Steps:**
  1. Mark what's old and what's new in each sentence.
  2. No new element: cut or merge the sentence. No old anchor: add familiar context (a known name, place or earlier term) (V15 7:58).
  3. Keep the content; change only the order: familiar element to the front, new concept to the end (V15 10:09–12:00).
  4. Remember that "old" depends on the reader (V15 5:16).
- **Symptoms:** "I know this, why am I reading it?" or "I have no idea what this means" (V15 3:45); sentences opening with a heavy new noun phrase and ending on something already mentioned; runs of unanchored figures.
- **When not to use:** A deliberate reveal can put the striking particular first (5.15).
- **Example:** Only new: "A p=reject DMARC policy cut spoofing 60%." Both, in order: "Most companies already verify email with a record called SPF. Adding a second setting, DMARC, tells receiving servers to reject forgeries, and in our tests it cut spoofing by 60%."
- **Sources:** V8 10:03; V15 0:50–12:29; V27 24:06–24:45; V32 7:46; V37 2:14–4:53, 19:15, 22:52; V48 3:00–4:02.

### 5.8 The stress position
*(his terms: prime real estate; the stress position)*
- **What:** The end of a sentence carries natural emphasis. Put the word or idea that deserves weight there, and keep weak material (pronouns, prepositions, attributions, stock phrases) out of it.
- **Why it works:** The period gives the last word emphasis, and a small grammar word wastes it (V2 9:38–10:48). The end is also the freshest item in the reader's briefcase and sets up the next sentence (V26 13:21). An award show announces that the award goes to... and then the name (V14 5:51–6:43). The split-infinitive rule gets emphasis backwards: to highlight an adverb, move it to the end (V14 14:44–16:03). A known element in the end slot sounds odd ("The bill was paid by me"), while a surprising new one sounds natural (V14 3:30–4:37).
- **Steps:** 1. Read the last two or three words of each sentence. 2. If they are a pronoun, preposition, attribution or filler, rearrange so a meaningful new word ends the sentence. 3. For formal writing, you can move a final preposition up to its relative pronoun ("on which") or restructure (V2 8:47–10:48).
- **Symptoms:** Sentences ending "from him", "for them", "in this regard", "according to the report", or on a dangling preposition in a formal text.
- **When not to use:** Speech and casual prose, where final prepositions are natural; he treats this as style, not correctness (V2 8:47). Split infinitives are fine when you want the adverb bound tightly to its verb (V14 15:45).
- **Example:** Before: "Investors wanted a clear plan from her." After: "Investors wanted her to present a clear plan."
- **Sources:** V2 6:59–10:48; V14 3:03–6:43, 13:50–16:03; V26 13:21; V37 16:03.

### 5.9 Choose the subject by what the reader cares about
*(his framing: bees make honey versus honey is made by bees; the reader-question test)*
- **What:** Neither "Bees make honey" nor "Honey is made by bees" is better in the abstract. Put in the subject position what this reader cares about, even if the sentence becomes passive or longer. To find it, phrase the question your sentence answers in the reader's voice; the subject of their question should be the subject of your sentence.
- **Why it works:** If the topic isn't the reader's concern, they must translate the sentence into their terms, which lowers clarity, persuasiveness and perceived value (V33 3:04). Readers describe the result as dull, passive or indirect, which reports a feeling, not the cause (V24 15:50). His cases: B2B copy that makes the product the subject when clients care about their own business; a finance sentence led by an abstract factor when investors care about the ratio it affects (V33 4:54–6:43).
- **Steps:**
  1. Take a sentence. Write the question it answers, as this reader would ask it.
  2. Note that question's subject.
  3. Rewrite so that is the subject, using the passive if needed.
  4. If readers care about ideas, put the idea in the subject, not "you" (6.14; V18 33:25).
  5. **When principles conflict** (the human character would be new information, a familiar topic would be old), keeping the familiar topic often wins (V37 12:26–14:29).
- **Symptoms:** "Our product / this feature / this study / [famous person]" as subjects in sentences aimed at readers who care about their own outcomes; the same sentence trying to serve two reader groups.
- **When not to use:** Some readers do care who said or did something (V33 11:10–12:10). If rules already work for a writer, he won't meddle (V33 8:08). Ignore all of this in the first draft (V33 14:11).
- **Example:** "The city council cut Sunday library hours to save $80,000." For parents asking when they can still go: "Sunday library hours were cut by the council to save $80,000." For council-watchers, keep the original.
- **Sources:** V18 33:25–34:19; V19 47:22–49:20; V24 15:13–18:11; V33 0:00–14:11; V37 12:26–14:29.

### 5.10 Active or passive: choose by position and agency
*(his framings: the passive as a tool; the relay race; agency control)*
- **What:** Default to characters as subjects, but use the passive when it does a job. His legitimate uses:
  1. **Focus** on the thing acted upon; the subject is a spotlight (V14 4:37–5:17).
  2. **The actor doesn't matter** (V14 5:17–5:51).
  3. **The actor is unknown** (V8 6:33; V14 5:51).
  4. **Land the key word in the stress position** (V14 5:51–6:43).
  5. **Keep old information first and hand the next sentence its topic**, keeping a run of shared topics intact (V9 6:01–9:17; V15 13:05–16:16; V27 13:56–14:45; V32 5:46–7:46).
  6. **Control agency:** decide deliberately whether readers see who acted. Leaving the agent out can keep a divided audience with you; naming it pushes each reader further toward where they already lean (V42 6:16–8:28).
  7. **It simply sounds better** (V14 6:43–7:04).
  8. **Show powerlessness** in narrative, as when things are done to a character (V13 13:57–14:17).
- **Why it works:** English has fixed subject-verb-object order; the passive exists so writers can put the thing acted upon first (V15 15:15–16:16). Languages don't keep forms with no function (V6 11:07). Blanket "use the active voice" rules push sentences into the wrong order (V15 13:05).
- **Steps (his conversion, V15 16:16):**
  1. Move the thing acted upon (the goal) to the subject.
  2. Move the doer (the agent) to a "by" phrase at the end, or drop it.
  3. Add a form of "be" and use the past participle.
  4. Check: old, simple information first; new, complex information last.
  Before converting any passive to active, check its neighbors: if it keeps the topic string or sets up the next sentence, it stays.
- **Symptoms:** A mid-paragraph active sentence whose subject is a long new noun phrase and whose object is the established topic; a writer who turned every passive active and now the paragraph zigzags.
- **When not to use:** The passive remains a minority tool; he cites David Foster Wallace for about one sentence in twenty (V6 11:47; V15 13:34–13:51). A passive that links to nothing is still flabby (V6 9:42). Flag passives that hide responsibility the reader needs, the "mistakes were made" dodge (V14 5:51; V42 7:12).
- **Example:** "Our team built a new triage tool last spring. Nurses at four clinics, from night-shift veterans to first-week graduates, now use it daily." Better: "...It is now used daily by nurses at four clinics, from night-shift veterans to first-week graduates." In an incident review, the reverse: "The load test was skipped" hides a decision; "Our team skipped the load test to hit the launch date" shows it.
- **Sources:** V6 6:03–12:04; V8 6:33, 11:15; V9 6:01–9:17; V13 13:57–14:17; V14 3:03–7:04; V15 13:05–17:20; V24 16:49; V26 10:24; V27 13:56–14:45; V32 5:46–7:46; V33 0:00–7:18; V37 13:39–14:29; V42 4:27–9:28.

### 5.11 Identify the passive before "fixing" it
- **What:** A true passive has two marks: a form of "be" plus a past participle, and the receiver of the action in the subject slot, with the doer missing or in a "by" phrase. Many sentences that feel passive aren't.
- **Why it works:** Of the four "passive" examples in a famous style manual, only one is passive; the others are an existential "there were" sentence, a plain past tense, and an adjective after "became" (V14 8:25–9:04). Misidentifying the passive causes pointless edits and worse decisions, and grammar checkers repeat the error (V14 7:44–9:31).
- **Steps:** 1. When a tool or reviewer flags "passive", check for be plus past participle. 2. Check whether the receiver of an action is the subject. 3. If either fails, it's a false alarm; leave it. 4. If it is passive, decide with 5.10.
- **Symptoms:** Rewriting "there were..." sentences, linking-verb sentences or past-perfect sentences because a tool called them passive.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Passive: "The file was deleted by the script." Not passive: "There were errors in the file." "She had left early." "The office became quiet."
- **Sources:** V14 7:44–10:59.

### 5.12 Match grammar to importance
- **What:** Give main-clause weight to what matters for the passage and subordinate the rest.
  - When one clause interprets, defines or states the effect of another, don't join them with "so" or "and" as equals. Make the interpretation a ", which..." clause, then reduce it to an *-ing* phrase (V37 9:04–10:48).
  - Order clauses to mirror real sequence, cause then effect; if the effect is secondary, reduce it to an *-ing* phrase (V32 9:08–10:31).
  - Use *since* to state a cause the reader already knows, so it can come first while reading as old information (V32 19:16–20:19); use it also to demote a less relevant cause to background (V37 14:29–16:03).
  - When a sentence holds too many characters and actions, split it into cause and effect first (V37 14:29).
  - Move attributions ("according to some historians") out of emphatic slots into an opening phrase (V37 19:35–20:28).
- **Why it works:** Grammar should signal importance; coordinating conjunctions give an event and its meaning equal weight (V37 9:04–9:27). Language rarely keeps two words with identical functions, and *since* marks its clause as given (V32 20:19).
- **Steps:** 1. Find "X, so Y" where Y interprets X; rewrite as "X, which [verb] Y", then "X, [verb]-ing Y". 2. Find effect-then-cause order and swap. 3. Decide which clause serves the passage's focus; subordinate the other. 4. Move attributions to the front as framing.
- **Symptoms:** Chains of equally weighted clauses; a less important event getting main-clause emphasis; an attribution at the end of a sentence.
- **When not to use:** When two clauses really are equally important (inferred). *Because* isn't wrong; *since* is a finer tool (V32 20:19).
- **Example:** Before: "The supplier raised prices by 30 percent, so our budget was no longer workable." After: "The supplier raised prices by 30 percent, making our budget unworkable."
- **Sources:** V8 6:33–8:06; V32 9:08–10:31, 19:16–20:19; V37 9:04–10:48, 14:29–16:03, 19:35–20:28.

### 5.13 Give caused changes a cause
- **What:** Replace intransitive "becoming" verbs (*became, grew, emerged, arose*) used for social or organizational change with a transitive verb that names what caused it, or an *-ing* phrase from the cause.
- **Why it works:** Few things are truly agentless: the sun rises, leaves fall, but historical and social changes are caused by someone's action (V37 10:48–11:33). Naming the cause shows readers the story.
- **Steps:** 1. Find "became", "grew", "emerged" for human affairs. 2. Ask what made it happen. 3. Make that the agent.
- **Symptoms:** Problems that "emerged", issues that "arose", morale that "became a concern", with no cause given.
- **When not to use:** Natural processes that really are agentless (V37 11:12).
- **Example:** Before: "Morale became a problem in the spring." After: "The spring layoffs made morale a problem."
- **Sources:** V37 10:48–11:33.

### 5.14 Noun-phrase suitcases
*(his metaphor)*
- **What:** When a sentence trails a string of prepositional phrases, or a modifier drifts away from what it modifies, repack the material into one compact noun phrase. Inside a phrase, put the familiar entity first and the new one last, ready to become the next topic.
- **Why it works:** A single noun phrase makes long information nimble, reduces the nouns competing for prominence, and keeps a member next to its category so the modifier attaches correctly (V32 12:05–13:05). Familiar-first inside a phrase applies old-to-new at small scale (V32 15:35–16:34).
- **Steps:** 1. Spot chains like "by X before Y-ing, including Z". 2. Convert the verbal part to a noun phrase. 3. Put "including..." right after its category. 4. Reorder "New person, relation of Familiar person" as "Familiar person's relation, New person, who..."
- **Symptoms:** Three or more prepositional phrases in a row; "including" attached to the wrong noun.
- **When not to use:** He says the awkward version is not wrong, just less than ideal (V32 12:05). Don't collapse into noun stacks (see 6.9).
- **Example:** "...was replaced by Maria, a protégé of the chair, who cut the budget" becomes "...was replaced by the chair's protégé, Maria, who cut the budget."
- **Sources:** V32 12:05–13:05, 15:35–16:34.

### 5.15 Context first or reveal: the camera shot
*(his cinematographer analogy)*
- **What:** Word order decides what the reader "sees" first and where they end up. Context first, then focus, moves from wide shot to close-up and orients the reader. Particulars first, context last, builds a reveal. Choose deliberately.
- **Why it works:** The image forms left to right; until context arrives, details float unattached, like the Cheshire Cat's smile (V26 1:50–2:51). Neither order is better; the point is that you can choose (V26 2:51).
- **Steps:** 1. Decide what the reader should see first and be left looking at. 2. For exposition, lead with context and end on the focus. 3. For surprise, lead with the striking particular. 4. Read slowly and notice where the picture snaps into place.
- **Symptoms:** A scene you can't picture until the last clause; the focus buried mid-sentence.
- **When not to use:** The reveal order costs working memory; use it sparingly in dense exposition (inferred).
- **Example:** Reveal: "Two unsigned consent forms turned up in the box of files the auditor carried up from the basement." Establishing: "In the box of files the auditor carried up from the basement, she found two unsigned consent forms."
- **Sources:** V26 1:50–3:37.

### 5.16 Open an expectation, then pay it off
*(his term: expectations, chosen over "cliffhangers")*
- **What:** Plant a word or claim that raises one specific open question, then answer it at a deliberate point, usually soon. It works at sentence scale, not only between chapters.
- **Why it works:** The reading brain is always anticipating what comes next; setup and payoff is the engine of good writing (V26 11:54–12:53). Open expectations are one of the four things readers carry forward (V26 4:16).
- **Steps:** 1. Plant a specific promise: an unexplained result, a named but unrevealed item. 2. Pay it off soon. 3. In revision, map each expectation to its payoff; cut unpaid promises.
- **Symptoms:** Flat prose where nothing is pending; promises never paid; signposts ("next I discuss...") doing the job an open question should do.
- **When not to use:** Hype words are not the device; he later rejects hooks that promise without offering value (V28 0:00). The device is a specific open question the text answers.
- **Example:** "The pilot did save money, just not in the budget line anyone was watching." Next: "The savings showed up in overtime, which fell by a third."
- **Sources:** V26 4:16, 11:54–13:21.

## 5C. Concrete and memorable

### 5.17 Concrete language readers could stub their toe on
*(his phrase for the standard: something readers could stub their toe on)*
- **What:** Ground abstractions in things readers can see, touch, hear or smell, in people, and in physical actions. Give each abstraction a concrete stand-in and reuse it whenever the abstraction returns.
- **Why it works:** The visual brain is far older than the language brain, so readers understand what they can picture; clear writing is visual (V9 4:24, 9:17–12:44; V22 13:48–14:40). Memory favors images, human action and sensation over bare information, which is why faces are easier to remember than names (V9 9:17–12:44). Sensory words pack a whole experience into one "file" and make readers supply surrounding context for free (V26 9:19–11:32). A concrete image works as set design against which abstract points make sense (V22 15:28).
- **Steps:**
  1. Circle abstract nouns (*process, framework, dynamics, factors, stakeholders*).
  2. For each, add a visible image, a person who embodies it, or a place (V22 13:48–15:28).
  3. Give places their sights, sounds and smells, not just names (V9 12:44).
  4. Write about flesh-and-blood people (V9 13:32).
  5. Make an unavoidable abstraction the subject of verbs that tell a story (V9 13:32).
  6. Picture the sentence; if no image forms, it's still abstract (V26 11:32).
- **Symptoms:** Paragraphs with nothing you could draw; generic nouns; no people in a passage about human affairs; "X of Y occurred".
- **When not to use:** Visual detail that doesn't serve the question is out (V39 9:54). Concreteness is not a license to pad (inferred). His concrete rewrites are sometimes passive: the fix is concreteness, not active voice (V26 10:24).
- **Example:** Before: "The factory closure had significant negative effects on the local community." After: "When the mill shut, the diner across the road cut its breakfast shift and the Little League lost its sponsor."
- **Sources:** V9 9:17–13:32; V22 13:48–15:28, 27:16; V26 9:19–11:54; V29 15:21; V30 13:05; V32 5:16.

### 5.18 Move up and down the ladder of abstraction
- **What:** Picture a ladder: concrete things at the bottom, abstractions (freedom, trust, the American dream) at the top. Good writing moves between them, grounding the abstract and lifting the concrete to what it means. A general truth lands best when a specific moment carries it.
- **Why it works:** Writing that stays at the top floats "in the clouds"; writing that stays at the bottom is mundane, facts with no larger meaning (V12 11:52–12:09). His model is a novel's green light at the end of a dock that becomes a symbol of a whole dream (V12 10:56–11:52). A lesson delivered through a scene lands as recognition rather than lecture (V21 27:01–28:33).
- **Steps:** 1. Label each paragraph high or low. 2. Where you've run high, drop to a concrete case. 3. Where you've run low, climb to what it means. 4. Put a specific moment in front of any sentence of general wisdom; consider cutting the abstract sentence.
- **Symptoms:** All-abstract prose (vague, preachy) or all-concrete prose (a list of facts with no point); lessons stated as "I learned that patience matters".
- **When not to use:** He asks for balance, not a ratio (V12 12:09). For development within a paragraph, see the five-level scale in 4.3.
- **Example:** High only: "Healthcare must rebuild trust through continuity of care." Moving on the ladder: "A nurse writes her mobile number on the discharge sheet. That small act of continuity is how trust gets rebuilt."
- **Sources:** V12 10:56–12:09; V21 27:01–28:33.

### 5.19 Characters, not labels
- **What:** Show people through specific actions instead of adjectives. "Passionate" is a label; the scientist who retook a course because an A-minus felt like not mastering it is a character.
- **Why it works:** Science and business don't happen on their own; people ask questions and make decisions, and if readers can see people they will follow you anywhere (V39 21:35–22:57). "Show, don't tell" is familiar; the hard part is doing it (V39 21:00–21:35).
- **Steps:** 1. Find every adjective describing a person (dedicated, brilliant, visionary). 2. Replace it with a concrete action that shows it. 3. Look for a biographical detail that echoes the piece's question. 4. Cast widely: patients, customers, neighbors, not only experts.
- **Symptoms:** People described with adjectives; experts appearing only as sources of quotes; nobody in the piece with a motive.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Before: "Dr. Lee is deeply committed to her fieldwork." After: "Dr. Lee drives four hours each way, every weekend, to check the soil sensors herself, because the one time she trusted the telemetry, it lied."
- **Sources:** V39 20:04–22:57.

### 5.20 Metaphors from the body
*(image he credits to Ocean Vuong: metaphor as a portal)*
- **What:** A metaphor should take the reader from something unfamiliar to something familiar, and the familiar thing should change how they understand the unfamiliar one. The best come from bodily, lived experience, including trade slang.
- **Why it works:** Metaphor is how minds make sense of the world, not decoration; everyday phrases reveal the concepts that organize thought (V36 7:50–9:00, drawing on *Metaphors We Live By*). You share a body with your readers, so metaphors from your physical experience speak their language (V36 9:58). AI has no body, so its metaphors have the right form but pair images that feel a few degrees off (V36 6:22–6:48). A metaphor can reach a general reader better than technical exposition (V43 6:17–6:49). An idiosyncratic image shaped by the writer's own life is a sign of trust in the reader (V39 7:57–8:57).
- **Steps:** 1. Name the unfamiliar thing. 2. Search your own sensory experience, work life or trade talk for a familiar thing with the same shape. 3. Test: does it tell the reader something new? If it only decorates, cut it. 4. Test: would someone who had held both things say this? 5. As a thinking tool, notice the hidden metaphors in your field's ordinary phrases.
- **Symptoms:** "A tapestry of", "a symphony of", "navigating the landscape of"; images no one could picture; comparisons readers must decode rather than feel.
- **When not to use:** It concerns metaphors you choose to use; it doesn't require adding them (inferred).
- **Example:** Before: "Onboarding is a rich tapestry of interconnected learning." After: "Onboarding a new engineer is like handing someone the keys to a house where every light switch is wired to a different room."
- **Sources:** V36 5:55–9:58; V39 7:57–8:57; V43 6:17–6:49.

### 5.21 Replace clichés with a fact or a fresh image
- **What:** Flag stock phrases and replace each with a specific fact, a fresh image, or nothing.
- **Why it works:** Following Orwell, clichés are dying metaphors used because they save the trouble of thinking of a phrase (V12 37:58–38:43). Writers have a civic job of keeping language alive and giving the culture new images (V12 38:43). His counterexample of a living image is New York garbage collectors' slang for maggots (V12 37:40; V36 7:15).
- **Steps:** 1. Mark phrases you've heard a thousand times. 2. Ask what specifically happened. 3. Write that, or a new image, or cut.
- **Symptoms:** "Game changer", "move the needle", "at the end of the day", "low-hanging fruit".
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Before: "At the end of the day, the new tool was a game changer." After: "The new tool cut contract review from three days to one."
- **Sources:** V12 37:40–38:43; V36 6:48–7:50.

### 5.22 Figures for lines readers remember
- **What:** Classical figures of speech for the few lines that must stick: a thesis, a section's last line, a title. He presents them as tools from literature, film and song (V12), and calls shareable lines "bangers" (V17 14:08).

  | Figure | Move | Why it works | Example (mine) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | **Antithesis** (V12 12:59–14:37) | State a familiar claim, then its inversion, joined by a semicolon | The sudden contrast covers both sides at once; the semicolon suits two halves of one coin | "Meetings are easy to schedule; decisions are not." |
  | **Alliteration** (V12 9:14–10:56) | Key words share an opening sound | Phrases roll off the tongue and even sound truer | "Small, steady, shipped beats big, bold, buried." |
  | **Polyptoton** (V12 12:09–12:59) | Reuse a word as a different part of speech | Readers do a double take; the line hangs in the air | "Writers who won't be edited rarely edit well." |
  | **Diacope** (V12 21:21–22:50) | Repeat a word after a brief interruption | The repetition etches the line into memory | "Start small. Really, start small." |
  | **Epistrophe** (V12 22:50–24:22) | End successive clauses with the same phrase | Like a chorus, it holds readers on the core message while details pile up | "When users get lost, it's the design. When support overflows, it's the design." |
  | **Ironic juxtaposition** (V12 7:54–9:14) | Place two things side by side so each changes how you see the other | The tension jolts readers into asking why they belong together | "The all-hands opened with a slide celebrating record engagement. The next announced layoffs." |
  | **Forward-pointing pronoun opening** (V12 24:22–25:40; captioned as "prolapsus") | Open with a pronoun whose noun comes later | Readers can't tolerate the ambiguity, so they read on | "They told us the migration would take a weekend. It took eleven months." |
  | **Piling up** (V12 25:40–27:40; captioned as "conju") | A flood of specific, first-hand images under a repeated frame | The mosaic immerses readers in the scene | "I have sat in kickoffs where no one could name the customer. I have read roadmaps with forty top priorities..." |
  | **Synesthesia** (V12 18:45–21:21) | Describe one sense, or an idea, in terms of another | Perception is multi-sensory; smell ties to memory | "Technical debt has a sound: the half-second pause before an engineer says 'it depends.'" |

- **Why it works:** See the table. Memorable lines are how ideas travel (V6 2:43).
- **Steps:** 1. Choose the one or two lines that must be remembered. 2. Try one figure on each. 3. Keep it only if it sharpens the claim.
- **Symptoms:** A key takeaway nobody can repeat; a closing line with no snap.
- **When not to use:** Use one tool at a time, not fifteen (V12 51:02). Alliteration makes claims sound truer than they are, so keep it for claims you can support (inferred). Semicolons are often better avoided outside antithesis (V12 13:51). Overuse reads as sloganeering (inferred). Forward-pointing openings can frustrate readers scanning a report for facts (inferred).
- **Example:** One per figure in the table. Applied to a thesis: "Most teams measure effort" becomes the antithesis "Effort is easy to count; progress is not."
- **Sources:** V6 2:43; V12 7:54–27:40, 51:02; V17 14:08.

### 5.23 Break rules on purpose
*(his phrase: learn the rules to break them properly)*
- **What:** Once you know a convention, you may break it to create an effect: a strange verb where the expected one belongs, a fragment for emphasis, a deliberate grammatical "mistake", punctuation or capitalization that carries meaning.
- **Why it works:** Amateurs cling to right and wrong; experts bend rules to wake readers up (V12 2:45–3:46). An odd verb at the end of a novel stayed in his ear for years; an AI assistant would have "corrected" it (V36 9:58–13:25). Straining against a form carries human energy (V36 12:32–13:06). A poet's missing punctuation and pointed capitalization carry meaning (V7 15:56–19:31). A "bad" sentence can be great in context (V10 4:40–5:18).
- **Steps:** 1. Know the rule first, as a chef learns technique before improvising (V36 12:09). 2. Name the effect you want. 3. Keep it rare enough to register. 4. Protect deliberate breaks from editors, checkers and AI tools.
- **Symptoms:** Prose that is fully correct and completely inert; or breaks that read as ignorance, not choice.
- **When not to use:** Not before you know the rule. A break the reader doesn't read as deliberate is just an error (inferred). Rare in routine reports (inferred).
- **Example:** Before: "We collected data for three years and ended up with only one chart we could use." After: "Three years of data. One usable chart."
- **Sources:** V2 19:54–20:42; V7 15:56–19:31; V10 4:40–5:18; V12 2:45–3:46; V36 9:58–13:48.

### 5.24 Sentence length follows the load
- **What:** Stop engineering sentence length for variety or "music". A sentence is long because it carries a lot and short because the point has landed.
- **Why it works:** Length has no link to readability; long sentences can be clear if well coordinated, and short ones can be baffling (V42 1:18–2:12). Lengthening a sentence because three short ones came before it is padding, not music (V42 3:31). The popular meme is really word-count advice, and readers care about value, not word count (V42 3:31–4:27). What matters is where complexity sits, not how long the sentence is (V27 24:45; V29 2:15–2:36).
- **Steps:** 1. Ask what the sentence must carry: how many ideas, relations, qualifications. 2. One thing: keep it short. 3. Linked parts: let it run, but coordinate and balance them. 4. For musicality, use recurring motifs (3.31).
- **Symptoms:** Filler added to lengthen; feedback like "vary your sentence length" (V44 30:08); rhythm tinkering while the argument is unclear.
- **When not to use:** He allows no exception.
- **Example:** "Revenue fell. Costs rose. Staff left. The board acted only once losses had run three quarters, the lead investor had threatened to walk, and the founders had stopped speaking." The last sentence is long because it carries three causes.
- **Sources:** V27 24:45; V29 2:15–2:36; V42 0:00–4:27; V44 30:08.

# Stage 6. Compression and tone

Late-stage work on the reader draft: cutting what costs the reader effort without paying back meaning, setting how strongly and how personally you speak, and finally mechanics.

## 6A. Compression

### 6.1 Cut late, and cut for load
*(his terms: compression; writing anorexia)*
- **What:** Compression means removing redundancy and bloat to reduce the reader's cognitive load, not making sentences short. Do it in revision, when you have plenty of material, not while drafting.
- **Why it works:** You can only afford to cut when you're rich: cutting 10 pages from 20 is a disaster, cutting 50 from 200 is nothing (V23 34:19–35:22). Pruning early slows you down and may throw away the wordy paragraph that holds the next idea; done too early it becomes self-punishment disguised as virtue (V23 34:19–35:22). Length doesn't matter if sentences are well shaped; load does (V29 2:15–2:36, 15:56). He presents compression as the start of polishing, after which you "spruce things up" (V29 20:02).
- **Steps:** 1. Finish the thinking draft and the structural passes first (0.4). 2. Then run 6.2 to 6.9, one at a time. 3. Look through the words to the ideas: count ideas, count words (V29 15:56).
- **Symptoms (in process):** Deleting sentences as you type; a thin first draft with nothing to choose from.
- **When not to use:** During generation.
- **Example:** A writer trims every paragraph of a first draft as she goes and ends with 600 words and no idea. Next time she drafts 3,000 loose words, finds the argument, and compresses to 1,200.
- **Sources:** V6 1:08–2:43; V23 34:19–36:18; V29 2:15–2:36, 15:56, 20:02.

### 6.2 "Do you need that?"
*(McCarthy's question, as he reports it; his framing of Adams's point as "kill your darlings")*
- **What:** For every section, paragraph, sentence, word and punctuation mark, ask whether you need it. Work from large to small. The "darlings" to kill are the extra words of an early draft you have grown attached to.
- **Why it works:** Every extra element costs processing effort; less effort means better writing, and even complex ideas can be carried in spare prose (V22 2:24–3:19). Simple writing persuades; the bare word is usually stronger than the intensified one (V6 1:08–2:43). A compressed, memorable phrase travels (V6 2:43).
- **Steps:** 1. Sections, then paragraphs, sentences, words, marks. 2. Ask whether the theme or a key point needs each one. 3. Delete on trial and reread; restore only if the meaning truly changes. 4. The harder you want to keep a word, the harder you should test it (V6 1:48).
- **Symptoms:** Intensifiers (*very, really, quite*); stacked qualifiers; phrases that restate; sections that are interesting but serve no point.
- **When not to use:** Keep a qualifier when removing it changes what the claim asserts ("rarely" versus "never") (inferred). Not during drafting (6.1).
- **Example:** Before: "It is important to note that, in many cases, although not all, teams that adopt code review early tend, on the whole, to ship fewer defects." After: "Teams that adopt code review early ship fewer defects."
- **Sources:** V6 1:08–2:43; V22 2:24–3:19.

### 6.3 The punctuation X-ray
*(his image: an X-ray or thumbprint of style)*
- **What:** Strip a passage down to its punctuation marks and look at the pattern. Few marks between periods means simply built sentences; dense clusters show sentences doing too much.
- **Why it works:** It makes sentence construction visible in a way reading the words hides, so "write simply" becomes checkable (V22 1:34–3:19).
- **Steps:** 1. Delete every letter and digit (a find-and-replace on `[A-Za-z0-9]` works), keeping line breaks at periods. 2. Scan for clusters of commas, semicolons, dashes and brackets. 3. Simplify those sentences.
- **Symptoms:** Lines like `, , ; — ( ) , .` repeating.
- **When not to use:** A diagnostic, not a rule; not every cluster is wrong.
- **Example:** `, , ; , ( ) — .` beside `. . , . .`: the first is one sentence doing too much.
- **Sources:** V22 1:34–3:19.

### 6.4 Cut filler words
*(his terms: filler words; verbal tics; throat clearing)*
- **What:** Delete words that feel natural in speech but add almost nothing in writing: *actually, basically, certain, various, particularly, really, virtually, individual, generally, given, practically*, and the vague phrases they prop up.
- **Why it works:** In speech, fillers keep you fluent while you search for the next word; in writing they are static that makes the message bloated and imprecise, and cutting them makes you sound precise and authoritative (V29 3:28–4:44). "Write the way you speak" imports them (V29 2:36–3:28).
- **Steps:** 1. Search for the listed words. 2. Delete each and check whether meaning is lost. 3. Cut the vague phrases around them.
- **Symptoms:** Soft, hedgy prose; several listed words per paragraph; sentences that sound like a transcript.
- **When not to use:** Keep a word where it carries meaning: "certain" meaning sure, "individual" contrasted with "group" (inferred; he grants every word has a purpose, V29 4:44).
- **Example:** Before: "The new policy basically aims to address certain issues that generally affect various departments." After: "The new policy addresses problems shared across departments."
- **Sources:** V29 2:36–5:39.

### 6.5 Cut doublets
*(his term)*
- **What:** Paired near-synonyms such as *each and every, first and foremost, full and complete, true and accurate, any and all*. Keep the stronger word.
- **Why it works:** Writers think doublets sound thorough; modern readers hear overkill. The habit dates from when writers paired a French or Latin word with an English one to look learned (V29 5:39–6:19).
- **Steps:** Scan for "X and Y" pairs that mean the same; keep the more exact word.
- **Symptoms:** Ceremonial or legalistic pairs.
- **When not to use:** Fixed legal terms in legal documents (inferred).
- **Example:** Before: "We are fully and completely committed to true and accurate data." After: "We are committed to accurate data."
- **Sources:** V29 5:39–7:18.

### 6.6 Cut redundancy
*(his three types)*
- **What:** Delete words already implied by other words.
  - **Redundant modifiers:** *future plans, end result, past history, totally transform, free gift* (V29 7:36–8:48).
  - **Redundant categories:** *large in size, round in shape, intuitive in nature, in a logical manner* → *logically* (V29 8:48–10:32).
  - **Spread-out implications:** *trying to learn* when "learn" implies trying; *the art of poetry* (V29 10:32–11:34).
- **Why it works:** Every redundant word is work with no return. Readers use it to judge whether you are paying attention; catching implied redundancy separates competent from exceptional writers (V29 6:19–7:36).
- **Steps:** 1. For each modifier, does its word already imply it? 2. For "X in nature/size/shape/manner" or "X of Y", does one already name the category? 3. For whole sentences, ask what they say in the fewest words.
- **Symptoms:** Sentences that feel padded though you can't point to one bad word.
- **When not to use:** He admits there's no firm rule, especially for categories; it takes an ear (V29 10:06).
- **Example:** Before: "Participants were asked to try to recall events from their past history of early childhood years that were significant in importance." After: "Participants recalled important childhood events."
- **Sources:** V29 6:19–11:34.

### 6.7 Replace a phrase with the exact word
*(the French* le mot juste*)*
- **What:** When several words carry one idea, find the single verb or noun that carries it all. Swap stock spoken phrases: *the reason for → why; despite the fact that → although; in the event that → if; in a situation where → when; concerning the matter of → about*.
- **Why it works:** Past a point, trimming a drive won't make it much shorter; you change vehicle, from road trip to flight (V29 12:41). Vocabulary is for compression and precision, not display (V29 13:07). A precise rare word is fine if nothing common does the job; so is a blunt everyday one if it lands with these readers (V12 5:57–7:54).
- **Steps:** 1. Count ideas and words. 2. Replace long phrases with one verb or noun. 3. Fix the word order the new verb requires. 4. Swap the stock phrases above.
- **Symptoms:** "The thing to do is..."; "the way in which..."; a clause stuffed where an object should be.
- **When not to use:** No formula; don't reach for rare words to impress (V29 13:07, 15:21).
- **Example:** Before: "Due to the fact that the meeting was moved to a later date, we are not in a position to respond at this point in time." After: "Because the meeting was postponed, we can't respond yet."
- **Sources:** V5 2:05–3:55; V12 5:57–7:54; V29 11:34–16:20.

### 6.8 Write in the affirmative
- **What:** Replace "not + word" with a single positive word and untangle stacked negatives: *not different → similar; not allow → prevent; not include → omit; not often → rarely; not many → few*. Watch words that are negative without "not": *prevent, lack, fail, deny, avoid, without, unless, except*.
- **Why it works:** A negative makes the reader process a statement and then reverse it, sometimes several times per sentence; combined with passives and nominalizations, comprehension collapses (V29 16:20–20:02).
- **Steps:** 1. Translate simple negatives. 2. For tangled sentences: move actions into verbs, split into clauses, turn each negative positive, drop clauses that become unnecessary (V29 18:20–19:02).
- **Symptoms:** Two or more negatives in a sentence; "unless... not..."; readers rereading a rule to work out what to do.
- **When not to use:** Keep the negative when the warning is the point (V29 20:02). A deliberate double negative can grant a quality without endorsing it, as in "not unpromising" (V2 3:17–4:24).
- **Example:** Before: "Claims will not be processed unless receipts are not missing for amounts that are not under $50." After: "Attach receipts to every claim of $50 or more." Keep: "Do not mix bleach with ammonia."
- **Sources:** V2 2:12–4:24; V29 16:20–20:02.

### 6.9 Thin out function-word chains
- **What:** Words are content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives) or function words (articles, prepositions). Two versions can share every content word and differ widely in function words; the heavier one is harder to take in. Collapse chains of *of the / in the / for the*, especially in introductions.
- **Why it works:** Syntax is like DNA that copies an idea from writer to reader; complex code causes mutations (V5 4:43). He reports that introductions with fewer articles and prepositions draw more citations (V5 4:43–5:53). Gray function words carry no content (V37 4:53).
- **Steps:** 1. Find "of" chains and runs of prepositional phrases. 2. Turn "the X of the Y" into "Y's X" or a direct verb. 3. Check every content word survives.
- **Symptoms:** Long noun phrases strung on "of"; sentences where most words are small grammatical ones.
- **When not to use:** Stop before creating noun stacks like "farm yield fertilizer effect analysis" (inferred). He ties the citation finding to introductions specifically (V5 5:53).
- **Example:** Before: "an examination of the effects of the use of fertilizer on the yields of farms in the region". After: "how fertilizer affects regional farm yields".
- **Sources:** V5 4:43–5:53; V32 12:05–13:05; V37 4:53.

### 6.10 Package simply; never dumb down
- **What:** Replace words chosen to sound learned with plain ones, and package complex ideas so they're easy to process, without simplifying the ideas themselves. Aim to make the reader feel smart, not to look smart.
- **Why it works:** Readers will do only so much work to unpack a message; complex wording keeps the idea on the page (V5 2:58–3:55). In the study he cites, the same content in more complex language was rated worse (V5 2:05–3:55). Experts don't need to dumb down; they need to trust readers and carry them there (V39 0:00, 8:17).
- **Steps:** 1. Find words that display the writer rather than inform. 2. Replace with the plain word. 3. Keep every idea intact.
- **Symptoms:** Sentences you have to read twice; vocabulary shown off in the introduction.
- **When not to use:** Plain packaging is not the "fifth-grade level" advice he rejects: a community's code words carry meaning and signal membership, so keep them for insiders (2.14; V12 3:46–5:57; V47 13:39).
- **Example:** Before: "Implementation of the intervention resulted in a substantial diminution in infection incidence." After: "The intervention cut infections by a third."
- **Sources:** V5 2:05–4:43; V12 3:46–5:57; V39 0:00, 8:17; V47 13:39.

## 6B. Tone, persona and presence

### 6.11 Calibrate certainty
*(his terms: hedges and boosters; hedged, calibrated, strident)*
- **What:** Set each claim's strength to match the evidence and the reading community, somewhere between over-hedged and strident. Fix two slots: choose one precise verb instead of a stack of modals, and narrow the subject noun instead of loading it with qualifiers. Back confident claims with evidence the reader accepts.
- **Why it works:**
  - Claims stated without regard to their limits get attacked (V3 10:07–11:39).
  - Stacked hedges make prose nearly meaningless: readers sense nothing is at stake and lose confidence in you (V50 32:08). Academic hedging suits peer review but reads as uncertainty elsewhere (V19 1:02:55–1:03:38).
  - Stacked absolutes and "obviously", "of course", "common sense" usually cover a gap in the argument and read as arrogance (V50 30:57–31:47).
  - An unedited persona can sound cocky; a few qualifiers help, in balance (V18 32:35–33:25).
  - Boldness belongs to certain genres: idea books make their boldest case and let readers qualify it (V17 12:13); "gift" books speak with calm authority (V17 35:19).
- **Steps:**
  1. Mark every intensifier and absolute (*every, never, only, obviously*) and every hedge (*might, arguably, somewhat, perhaps, at least partly*) (V50 33:26).
  2. Decide how sure the evidence makes you.
  3. Verb slot: replace a modal stack with the one verb that fits; a thesaurus or AI can help find it (V50 33:26–34:15).
  4. Subject slot: scope the noun precisely instead of "every X" or "some X, in certain cases" (V50 34:15–35:11).
  5. Where force is needed, earn it with a reason (V19 1:04:23–1:05:09).
  6. One well-chosen hedge, never a stack (V3 10:07).
- **Symptoms:** "It could perhaps be suggested that the policy may have contributed"; "Every manager who skips this will obviously fail"; bold claims with no evidence.
- **When not to use:** Fields such as science and peer-reviewed academia expect more caution (V19 1:02:55; V50 32:08). Positioning books can be deliberately bold (8.24).
- **Example:** Hedged: "It might be argued that some managers may risk somewhat higher turnover without exit interviews." Strident: "Every manager who skips exit interviews will obviously lose their best people." Calibrated: "Managers who skip exit interviews lose their earliest warning of turnover."
- **Sources:** V3 9:31–11:39; V14 1:46; V16 2:43–3:01; V17 12:13, 35:19; V18 32:35–33:25; V19 1:02:00–1:05:09; V50 30:18–35:11.

### 6.12 Voice from lived experience, not surface rules
*(his framing: voice as emergent; style as integrity)*
- **What:** Voice isn't a set of sentence habits you can list. It emerges when language meets a particular blend of experience, expertise and perspective. Style is a by-product of struggling honestly with your own inquiry: when what you think and what you write line up, the words you reach for become your style.
- **Why it works:** You can catalog a writer's grammar and vocabulary and still not capture their voice; it behaves like an ant colony, not a machine (V36 1:10–3:20). Readers come to connect with a person; AI averages thousands of voices into something smooth and hollow (V36 5:02–5:55). No great stylist was planning fancy sentence structures (V38 10:50–12:34). Readers respond to a writer who found something true and said it so it resonated (V38 12:57).
- **Steps:**
  1. Don't build voice from banned-phrase lists or "avoid AI tells" edits (V36 5:02).
  2. For each section, ask what could only have come from you: a specific experience, a professional scar, a field's perspective.
  3. When a sentence feels hollow, ask whether you believe it and have seen it yourself (V38 15:41–16:38).
  4. Judge the draft by whether it shares something of you with a real reader, not whether it "passes as human".
  5. First-date test: before editing surface features, ask whether you actually want to communicate with this person; polish can't rescue an absent spirit (V45 1:08–2:37).
  6. Talk-back test: for each claim, imagine the reader asking "why?" or "compared with what?" If you can't answer beyond what's on the page, you have fluency without understanding. His story: a memorized phone order that collapsed at the first question from the shop (V45 9:06–11:41).
- **Symptoms:** Smooth, correct prose anyone in the field could have signed; decorative phrasing; borrowed flourishes; edits aimed at removing tells rather than saying something.
- **When not to use:** Voice-seekers often need a real relationship with readers more than technique (7.4, 7.10; V44 31:19–32:40).
- **Example:** Before: "Leading a team through change requires resilience and adaptability." After: "The first time I announced a reorg, I rehearsed in the car park for forty minutes and still opened with the wrong sentence."
- **Sources:** V36 1:10–5:55; V38 10:50–16:38; V40 3:21–4:00; V44 31:19–32:40; V45 1:08–2:37, 9:06–11:41.

### 6.13 Put a person on the page, where it helps
*(his terms: the author function "I" and the flesh-and-blood "I")*
- **What:** Two kinds of "I": one that points inside the text ("I argue in section 3") and one that points to a real person who noticed, tried, was surprised. Use personal language, humor and anecdotes to build a relationship with readers, matched to each section's job: personal in the abstract, introduction and framing; impersonal where readers come to examine methods and data.
- **Why it works:** People relate to people; a reader who connects with a character follows them through details to conclusions (V4 11:22–12:15; V5 6:47). He reports citation data: personal language in the front matter draws more citations, in methods and results fewer (V5 5:53–8:01). Owning your ideas makes you seem authoritative; impersonal front matter leaves ideas floating in the ether (V5 9:01). Abstract, impersonal prose sounds authoritative but writing is social (V19 1:01:27). "I" and "we" put writer and reader on the same side of the table (V22 21:41–23:32).
- **Steps:** 1. In openings and framing, let a real researcher or practitioner appear: what you noticed, what surprised you. 2. Use the author-function "I" freely for signposting claims. 3. Keep methods and results focused on procedures and data. 4. Don't overdo it (see 6.14).
- **Symptoms:** A voice so impersonal nobody seems to have done or thought anything; "it was found that" in an abstract; or the reverse, methods sections narrating feelings.
- **When not to use:** Avoiding "I" can be right on a controversial topic where naming people would inflame it (V4 13:03–13:24). The early "Who are you?" lesson still stands: "I think" is no substitute for evidence (V3 0:51). He changes his mind on one exception: for your own research, own it rather than hide (V4 13:03 vs V5 9:01).
- **Example:** Abstract before: "It was found that sleep restriction was associated with reduced recall." After: "We show that losing two hours of sleep cuts next-day recall by a fifth." Methods stay: "Participants were assigned to one of three sleep schedules."
- **Sources:** V3 0:51; V4 8:45–14:02; V5 5:53–9:53; V19 1:00:28–1:02:00; V22 20:49–23:32.

### 6.14 Partner, not preacher
*(his framings: the author persona; the stage and spotlight)*
- **What:** The author on the page is a persona made of language choices, not the real person. Read the draft as a stranger would and adjust until the persona sounds like a partner. In particular, don't habitually make "you" the grammatical subject of sentences that are really about ideas.
- **Why it works:** Readers don't have the real person as context; they reject a preachy or arrogant voice even when they like the ideas (V18 25:41–27:28). Whatever sits in the subject position is the actor in the spotlight; putting "you" there drags the reader out of the audience onto the stage, which feels like a lecture (V18 28:19–31:22). A string of aphorisms and grand labels for your own ideas ("my teachings") talks down (V18 25:41–26:30). Too much "you" reads like a journal of notes to yourself (V19 1:01:27–1:02:00).
- **Steps:** 1. Underline every "you" (V18 32:35). 2. For each, ask whether the sentence is about the reader or about an idea. 3. If about an idea, make the idea the subject; use the passive to focus the right thing. 4. Keep the "you"s that truly address the reader's situation. 5. Replace grand labels and chains of aphorisms with plain claims.
- **Symptoms:** Pages dense with "you need to..."; imperatives addressed to nobody; every sentence an aphorism; a finger-wagging tone.
- **When not to use:** Advice columns, where you really are addressing the reader's situation (V18 29:43). Personal presence is still good (6.13); this is about habitual "you" as actor.
- **Example:** Before: "You need to stop checking email before 10." After: "Email checked before 10 sets the agenda for the whole day." Before: "Here is the first teaching: the undisciplined mind is a slave." After: "Distraction usually isn't a character flaw. It's what happens when nothing on the desk is clearly more important than the phone."
- **Sources:** V18 25:41–35:20; V19 1:01:27–1:02:00.

### 6.15 Conversational moves instead of metadiscourse
*(his term: metadiscourse; also signposting)*
- **What:** Metadiscourse is writing about the writing: "This chapter discusses...", "The preceding section demonstrated...", "The final topic to be addressed is...". Replace most of it with the moves of a conversation: introduce a topic with the question it answers; use visual language ("as we've seen", "notice"); use the language of moving together ("let's look at", "we arrive at", "let's finish with").
- **Why it works:** "Tell them what you'll tell them" is a leftover of oral rhetoric, when speakers had to stop listeners' minds wandering; in writing it bores readers, like a driver who pulls over every fifteen minutes to announce the route (V9 13:32–15:37). Seeing implies someone who sees, so writer and reader become companions looking together rather than units of text (V9 15:37–16:45). Inviting language keeps readers off the back foot, especially when overturning their view (V22 20:49–23:32). Heavy signposting often patches a private structure (V49 10:14–11:14).
- **Steps:** 1. Find sentences whose subject is a unit of the document or that announce future moves in agentless passives. 2. Replace with a question, "notice", "as we've seen", or "let's". 3. Then reorganize what remains; deleting signposts isn't enough (V9 16:45). 4. Keep functional signals made of content: headings, point sentences, key terms (3.19).
- **Symptoms:** "This section examines"; "will be discussed"; "firstly... secondly..." scaffolding; frequent previews and recaps.
- **When not to use:** Signpost sparingly, not never (V9 15:37). Speech and video scripts can use more (V9 14:50). Enumerators earn their place for heavy lists (4.9). Rhetorical questions can be misused; use the ones the reader is really asking (V22 21:41).
- **Example:** Before: "Section 3 examines the causes of turnover. The previous section demonstrated that pay is not the main factor." After: "Why do staff leave? As we saw, pay isn't the main reason. Let's look at what happens in their first month."
- **Sources:** V9 13:32–16:45; V22 20:49–23:32, 26:27; V26 0:38; V49 10:14–11:14.

### 6.16 Anchor the text in the classic scene
*(his terms: joint attention; the classic scene; blending; also the park bench and the window)*
- **What:** Keep the real, complex situation, but write as though you and one reader are side by side, looking at the same thing. A distant or large audience becomes one person beside you; an abstract topic becomes something you can point at; a past event is shown as if unfolding now; a complex purpose (argue, analyze) becomes showing: show the problem, then show the solution.
- **Why it works:** Clear prose rests on joint attention: two people attending to one visible thing, each aware of the other (V30 2:44–3:20). Professional writing is far from that scene (complex audience, abstract purpose, time gap), which is why it gets hard; blending anchors it there (V30 10:12–13:05). His model is a novelist's letters to her sister, written alone and read days later, which say she has been "talking" to her all along (V30 11:13–13:05). Good writing is a window onto the world (V12 38:43; V45 14:10). A reader invited to sit on the same bench, looking outward, feels more intimate than one asked to watch the writer (V43 4:05, 8:50–9:46). "Write the way you speak" is right about the scene of conversation, wrong if taken to mean casual words (V30 1:38–2:09).
- **Steps:** 1. Map the situation (2.8). 2. Write a paragraph of your real material in the scene: one reader beside you, something to point at, show rather than argue (V30 14:03). 3. Read it aloud. 4. Expect it to feel artificial at first; repeat until automatic. 5. In revision, find where the text leaves the scene: a sudden lecture, an unseen abstraction, a shift into talking at the reader.
- **Symptoms:** Past-tense reports of abstractions; argument with no picture; readers unsure whether they're addressed; drifting in and out of the scene without control (V30 15:01).
- **When not to use:** Strict genres (a methods section) may limit tense and address; keep the shared object and the showing anyway (inferred). The drill that trains it is 1.31.
- **Example:** Before: "Analysis of the 2023 inventory records indicated that stock discrepancies were attributable to manual entry." After: "Look at the March sheet. The warehouse counts 212 boxes; the system says 180. Every gap traces back to a number someone typed by hand."
- **Sources:** V9 15:37–16:45; V10 6:04; V12 38:43; V30 1:38–15:41; V43 3:08–4:56, 8:50–9:46; V45 14:10–14:53.

### 6.17 Understate the serious
- **What:** Match tone against the weight of the subject: restraint for grave material, exaggeration allowed for light material.
- **Why it works:** Restraint makes the audience imagine the worst and fill the gaps; let the weight of the story do the work (V12 39:24–40:20). He compares a restrained film treatment of atrocity with an explicit one: both work, restraint engages the imagination more.
- **Steps:** 1. With a grave subject, strip intensifiers and give plain facts. 2. With a light one, you may inflate for comedy.
- **Symptoms:** "Devastating", "catastrophic", "horrific" stacked on a subject already grave.
- **When not to use:** The explicit approach also works; it's a choice (V12 40:20).
- **Example:** Before: "The devastating, catastrophic layoffs shattered hundreds of lives." After: "On Friday at 9 a.m., 212 badges stopped working."
- **Sources:** V12 38:43–40:20. (The narrative version is 8.19.)

### 6.18 Emphasis economy
- **What:** Emphasis devices (em dashes, *however*, signposts, bold) lose force the more often they appear. Reserve each for the moments that matter most.
- **Why it works:** Three *howevers* in a paragraph aren't grammatically wrong, but at the level of meaning each weakens the others (V34 8:51–9:42). Reserve *but* and *however* for real turns, especially the problem turn in an introduction (V28 11:00). Transitions and signposts are overused (V32 17:37; V37 11:59).
- **Steps:** 1. Count dash pairs, *however/furthermore* and signposts per page. 2. For each, ask whether this is one of the most important moments. 3. If not, downgrade.
- **Symptoms:** Dashes all over the page; undergraduate-style transitions on every sentence.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** A page with nine *howevers* keeps two: the problem turn in the introduction and the counterargument in section 3.
- **Sources:** V28 11:00; V32 17:37; V34 4:36–9:42; V37 11:59.

### 6.19 Em dashes and the loudness scale
- **What:** Three ways to set off an interruption, from quiet to loud: parentheses, commas, em dashes. Use a pair of dashes to give emphasis to an interruption that is an example, an aside, or a mitigating hedge. Put definitions in a ", which..." clause instead. Judge punctuation by reading silently.
- **Why it works:** A dash marks where a statement pauses and resumes, so what's inside needn't follow the host sentence's grammar, which mirrors how people think (V34 7:45–8:09). Commas doing several jobs hide a sentence's structure and can produce comma splices (V34 4:12–4:36). A definition isn't the main focus, so it belongs in a subordinate clause; a dash wastes emphasis on it (V34 9:42–11:48). Reading aloud lets your voice supply emphasis the page doesn't (V34 2:31). Dropping dashes because they "look like AI" trades a visible risk for invisible damage (V34 1:42).
- **Steps:** 1. Find interruptions. 2. Classify: example, aside, mitigation, definition, minor note. 3. Emphasized example, aside or mitigation: dashes. Definition: ", which". Minor note: parentheses. 4. Read silently to compare.
- **Symptoms:** An important interruption lost among commas; "term—definition of the term—" glossary dashes; dashes on every line.
- **When not to use:** Comma versus semicolon worries don't matter as correctness (V31 10:11); this is about emphasis and structure, not rules.
- **Example:** Example: "The three costs of a bad hire—lost time, lost morale, lost clients—rarely show up in a budget." Mitigation: "Remote teams can match office output—not in every role, but in most desk jobs—if managers measure results." Definition: "We use triage, which sorts patients by urgency, to decide who is seen first."
- **Sources:** V31 10:11; V34 0:00–12:17.

### 6.20 Mechanics last
- **What:** Proofread grammar, punctuation, spelling and formatting; check house style; match the unwritten conventions of the platform; handle presentation. Do it last. A short list of rules he treats as real, with reader-based reasons (V2):
  - **Pronoun case:** remove the other person to test ("awarded to Dr. Osei and me"); answer who/whom with he/him (V2 4:56–6:23, 14:45).
  - **Agreement:** count real entities; several names for one person take a singular verb (V2 12:33–14:45).
  - **Run-ons:** two full sentences joined only by a comma lose the boundary in writing (V2 10:48–12:33).
  - **Dangling participles:** make the doer of an opening *-ing* phrase the subject (V2 18:04–19:54).
  - **Oxford comma:** use it consistently to avoid ambiguous lists, unless house style forbids it (V2 15:13–18:04).
  - **Final prepositions:** not a rule; a style choice about emphasis (5.8).
- **Why it works:** Typos break a serious reader's trust, like a sharp suit with the fly down (V24 21:36). But most writers obsess over grammar while the writing serves no clear purpose (V24 21:58).
- **Steps:** Run after all structural and clarity passes (0.4).
- **Symptoms:** Grammar fixes on a draft whose point is unsettled; or a strong piece with careless typos.
- **When not to use:** Don't start here. Tools help, but a careful human eye matters (V24 21:58). Online readers may not care about comma splices as errors (V34 4:36).
- **Example:** "Using a larger sample, the error rate fell" becomes "Using a larger sample, we cut the error rate."
- **Sources:** V2 4:56–19:54; V10 6:04; V24 21:04–22:56; V31 10:11–14:34.

# Stage 7. Feedback

Why writers can't judge their own drafts, how to read feedback, and how to get useful responses from real readers.

### 7.1 You can't edit your own draft by feel
*(his terms: the coffee stain problem; the briefed reader)*
- **What:** Rereading your own text is remembering, not reading. Replace "does it read well to me?" with explicit checks of the places where readers look for information: title, end of the introduction, section and paragraph indexes, topic positions, key terms, conclusion.
- **Why it works:** A coffee stain on your draft reminds you of the moment you spilled it and means nothing to anyone else; your sentences work the same way, triggering the idea you already had (V24 0:52–1:40; V49 1:49–3:40). In a study he cites from Carnegie Mellon, readers briefed on a topic caught fewer structural errors than cold readers, because they filled gaps from what they knew; the writer is the most briefed reader possible (V50 2:00–4:24). So "rest it for a few days and read it as a stranger" doesn't work (V50 2:00). You know the text too well to feel its missing links (V27 17:46, 33:17).
- **Steps:** 1. Stop trusting your gut on clarity. 2. Run location checks: 3.4, 3.18, 4.5, 4.2, 3.21. 3. Use real readers to find friction (7.3).
- **Symptoms:** The writer is confident; readers are confused. Key phrases carry meaning for the writer that the text never supplies.
- **When not to use:** It's a reason for the method, not a technique on its own.
- **Example:** You reread "the migration finally fixed drift" and see a week of debugging behind "drift". A reader sees an undefined word. The key-term check finds the index never introduced it.
- **Sources:** V24 0:52–1:40; V27 17:46–18:19, 33:17–33:44; V49 0:52–3:40, 7:34–8:21; V50 2:00–4:24.

### 7.2 Treat complaints as symptoms
- **What:** Words like "unclear", "dense", "disorganized", "dull", "passive" describe how the text made a reader feel, not what caused it. Locate where the friction happened and diagnose the cause with reader-pattern knowledge.
- **Why it works:** Untrained readers can report the experience but not its cause; they lack the vocabulary to say "your writing patterns interfere with my reading patterns" (V47 4:44–7:16; V19 26:31). What readers say they want and what they want differ (V47 5:33). "Dull" and "passive" often trace back to what sits in the subject position (V24 15:13–15:50).
- **Steps:** 1. Collect the words and note where each points. 2. Treat each as a friction flag at that spot. 3. Diagnose: is the point missing from the index? Do topics wander? Is shorthand unpacked? Is the order the writer's discovery order? Is value missing at the top? 4. Fix the cause. Making "dense" text shorter is not automatically the fix.
- **Symptoms:** A writer about to swap words or add explanation because a reviewer wrote "unclear".
- **When not to use:** Readers remain good sensors of *where* friction is, even if poor at saying why (V47 4:44).
- **Example:** "Dense" beside a methods paragraph. Diagnosis: five undefined acronyms, and the paragraph's purpose sits in its last sentence. Move the purpose up; unpack two acronyms; leave sentence length alone.
- **Sources:** V19 26:31; V24 15:13–15:50; V32 3:24–3:50; V35 12:09; V47 4:44–8:19.

### 7.3 Test with real readers
- **What:** Watch real readers try to use your text, ideally people from your own audience. Pitch the core idea to strangers and watch their faces. Hold unstructured one-to-one conversations with readers to hear what they care about in their own words.
- **Why it works:** Until you see someone using the text, you are guessing (V19 1:11:07). Editors and writer friends aren't who you're writing for (V19 1:12:01). Puzzled looks mark what to sharpen; when strangers get it in one or two sentences, freeze that wording (V16 5:28–7:47). Recurring themes and phrases from reader calls give you topics and language (V17 34:36–35:19). Signs of success: readers act on the piece, you can predict objections, people who disagree still find it useful, it gets shared (V19 1:13:02–1:14:43).
- **Steps:**
  1. Say the point aloud in one sentence to a real person; stop talking; watch.
  2. Adjust until a new person gets it at once.
  3. Offer short calls to readers; listen without an agenda; log recurring phrases.
  4. Share early, messy work with a small audience and watch where they struggle.
  5. Talk a struggling piece through with one trusted person; share work while it's struggling, not only when proud of it (V23 24:09–25:45). Students can book a writing-center tutor before the first paper, which is how he learned what his professors wanted (V3 1:07–1:47).
- **Symptoms that you skipped it:** You can't predict readers' questions; your picture of the reader is a guess; polite silence after publishing.
- **When not to use:** Groups larger than three or four tend to give polite, vague feedback (V23 24:59). Read responses as symptoms (7.2).
- **Example:** After twenty calls with new managers, twelve use the phrase "I'm still doing my old job." It becomes a chapter title.
- **Sources:** V3 1:07–1:47; V16 5:28–7:47; V17 34:36–35:19; V19 1:11:07–1:14:43; V23 24:09–25:45.

### 7.4 Early readers in a community of practice
*(his terms: early readers, preferred to "beta readers"; community of practice)*
- **What:** Find a handful of people you trust, respect, and want respect from, and share a practice for giving feedback (7.5 to 7.8), with deadlines. Write the next piece with those people in mind.
- **Why it works:** You never have block or worry about voice when talking to someone you respect; your word choices flow from the relationship (V44 30:39–32:40). Without a shared protocol, peer feedback is unqualified and falls back on bogus online rules (V44 29:49–30:39). Writing for people whose judgment you care about creates the stakes that produce clarity (V36 17:19–18:08). In his cohort, both block and flat voice improved without a lesson on either (V44 32:40–34:31).
- **Steps:** 1. Name three to five people whose respect you want. 2. Agree on a protocol. 3. Set submission deadlines. 4. Draft to those people.
- **Symptoms:** Writing for "everyone"; a flat, generic voice; feedback that quotes rules.
- **When not to use:** A community bolted onto the end of a course isn't enough; it has to be a real shared practice (V44 33:37). Participants also wanted actual lessons; his next version adds curriculum (V44 32:58–33:37).
- **Example:** A policy researcher stops drafting for "the public" and drafts for three former colleagues in city government whose respect she wants. The flatness goes.
- **Sources:** V36 17:19–19:06; V44 0:00, 28:00–34:31.

### 7.5 The letter of response
*(his term)*
- **What:** A letter of about two pages, addressed to the author, that moves through fixed stages: first, state as precisely as you can what the piece is trying to do; next, say what you would steal from it (a technique you'll try); only after that, say what was lacking or missing. He says there were four or five stages and on camera recalls these three.
- **Why it works:** If you misread the piece, the author has no reason to accept your criticism; if you state its aim so well that the author agrees, they're ready to hear it (V44 17:02–17:54). "What I'd steal" turns attention to craft and teaches people to read like writers (V44 18:13, 24:17–24:38). A shared format makes feedback qualified (V44 29:49–30:39).
- **Steps:**
  1. Practise the format on professionally published essays for two weeks before workshopping members' own work (V44 18:55).
  2. Build the reading list from the group: each member brings two essays they admire, creating a shared culture of taste (V44 19:31).
  3. Write the letter: aim, what you'd steal, then what's missing.
- **Symptoms it fixes:** Feedback that misreads the piece; defensive authors; rule-quoting; praise sandwiches.
- **When not to use:** It is analytical, not a book club about feelings (V44 16:29).
- **Example:** "Your essay is trying to convince engineering managers that onboarding fails in week five, not week one. I'd steal the way you open each section with a real chat message. What I missed: what a manager should do on day 35."
- **Sources:** V21 3:09 (the class response paper); V44 15:30–21:35.

### 7.6 Two readings: gut, then body scan
- **What:** Read the piece twice, days apart. First as an ordinary reader: skim, put it down if bored, and record a gut reaction without writing anything. Second, analytically: take each reaction and find where in the text it lives, where it starts and stops, and study how that effect is produced.
- **Why it works:** The first reading captures a real reader's response; the second turns a vague verdict into knowledge of how the text works. His analogy is a meditation body scan: when pain arises you don't get up; you look at where it is, where it's strongest, where it ends (V44 22:00–24:38).
- **Steps:** 1. Read normally. 2. Voice-note your gut reaction. 3. Wait a day or more. 4. Reread and locate each reaction precisely. 5. Study that spot.
- **Symptoms it fixes:** Feedback like "boring" with no location; admiring a piece without learning from it.
- **When not to use:** He had AI turn his voice notes into bullets for his own use (V44 22:48); keep any such use private (0.5).
- **Example:** Gut: "Lost interest halfway." Scan: interest drops at paragraph 7 where the legislative history starts, bottoms out at a list of dates, and returns when the named nurse reappears in paragraph 11. Lesson: history with no character in it loses readers.
- **Sources:** V44 22:00–24:38.

### 7.7 Read for sameness and difference
- **What:** A reading rule from a former professor: look for where things are the same and where they are different. Use it when two readers compare responses, and when reading a source against your own views.
- **Why it works:** One simple instruction turns two monologues into a comparison; the discussion grows from agreements and divergences (V44 21:35–22:00). Applied to reading, it draws out your own ideas (V40 10:14–10:37).
- **Steps:** 1. While a peer reads their response, listen for agreement and difference with yours. 2. Start discussion from the agreements, then the differences. 3. In notes, write how a source is the same as or different from what you think (1.23).
- **Symptoms it fixes:** Feedback sessions that are parallel monologues; notes with no reaction.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Two readers both flag the opening as ambiguous, but one reads it as about hiring and the other as about retention. That difference is the most useful finding of the session.
- **Sources:** V40 10:14–10:37; V44 21:35–22:48.

### 7.8 The silent-author rule
*(from a writing seminar with Junot Díaz that a friend attended)*
- **What:** While a piece is discussed, its author says nothing. Readers talk about the text in the third person. The author gets a short slot at the end for questions.
- **Why it works:** It mirrors publication: once a piece is out, you can't explain or defend it (V44 26:04). It frees readers to say what they really think without the feedback sandwich (V44 26:24–26:45).
- **Steps:** 1. Set a fixed discussion period with the author muted. 2. Discuss the text, not the author. 3. End with ten to fifteen minutes for the author's questions.
- **Symptoms it fixes:** Authors explaining what they meant; readers softening their responses.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** An author listens silently while two readers disagree about what the opening promises, and learns what she most needed to know: it's ambiguous.
- **Sources:** V44 24:38–27:14.

### 7.9 Mine the sympathetic reviews
- **What:** To diagnose a published or beta-read piece, set aside the angriest reactions and read the middling ones from people who wanted to like it. Group recurring complaints and trace each to a cause: promise, purpose, structure or voice.
- **Why it works:** Disappointed fans report real friction rather than hostility, and a complaint that recurs points to a structural cause (V18 1:47–2:38).
- **Steps:** 1. Collect middle-rated reactions. 2. Group by theme. 3. Trace each theme to a cause.
- **Symptoms it catches:** "Hard to say what it's about", "disjointed", "needed an editor".
- **When not to use:** Better still, test before publishing (7.3).
- **Example:** Beta comments "lost me in chapter 3", "felt like separate blog posts", "not sure what to do with this" are three phrasings of one cause: no through-line.
- **Sources:** V18 1:47–2:38.

### 7.10 Finisher or voice-seeker, and the accountability partner
- **What:** Writers who come for help usually have one of two problems. Finishers start pieces and never publish; they need a deadline and an accountability partner. Voice-seekers publish, but their prose feels flat or patched together from tricks; they need deeper craft and a real relationship with readers.
- **Why it works:** Help built for one group leaves the other unserved (V44 4:35–10:18). Daily check-ins with a partner were what one popular course's participants valued most, and they solve getting started, though they teach no craft (V44 6:53–8:01).
- **Steps:** 1. Ask: what have you published? What sits unfinished? Does your published work sound like you? 2. Finishers: a date and a partner who checks in daily. 3. Voice-seekers: early readers (7.4) and targeted craft.
- **Symptoms:** Finishers: a folder of half-drafts. Voice-seekers: steady output that "doesn't sound like me".
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Nine half-finished essays: set a Friday date and pair with a partner. Forty published posts that all sound like corporate social media: find three readers you respect and study one essayist closely.
- **Sources:** V44 4:35–10:18.

### 7.11 Write with stakes
- **What:** Put your work, under your name, in front of people whose judgment you care about. At work, replace slides with a memo that states the issue, shows your reasoning and grounds the proposal in evidence, read closely before discussion. Revise several times before submitting.
- **Why it works:** Accountability produces clarity and depth (V36 17:19–18:08). He cites a company that starts meetings with silent memo reading, and a novelist-teacher who required two or three revisions before anything could be submitted (V36 17:19–18:08). When stakes disappear, writing becomes a chore, and chores get handed to AI (V36 19:06). Publishing brings contacts and opportunities you couldn't have planned (V44 0:51, 13:07–14:02). Being evaluated teaches more than more polishing (V23 27:37).
- **Steps:** 1. Publish something you wouldn't be embarrassed for respected peers to read (V36 19:06). 2. For work decisions, write the memo. 3. Revise before you submit.
- **Symptoms:** Writing that feels like a chore; reaching for AI to get it done; a private draft that has never faced a reader.
- **When not to use:** Stakes belong to reader-facing work; exploratory writing needs safety, not judgment (inferred from V36 22:01–22:59 and V38 18:28).
- **Example:** Instead of "Pricing is confusing; maybe simplify", a one-page memo to leadership: "Customers abandon checkout at the plan-comparison step. I propose three tiers instead of five because... Here is what would prove me wrong."
- **Sources:** V23 27:37–29:19; V36 13:48–19:29; V44 0:51, 13:07–14:02.

# Stage 8. Genre modules

Techniques he teaches for specific genres. The general stages still apply; these entries add or override. Where a module conflicts with the default expository pipeline (for example, point placement), the entry says so.

## 8A. The essay

### 8.1 The essay: a reader's definition
- **What:** An essay helps specific readers understand something better that they want to understand. He sets this against the writer's definition, "write about whatever interests you". Each part does work: specific readers; understanding (not persuasion) as the genre's function; something *they* want to understand; "better" by *their* measure; and argument as the method.
- **Why it works:** Questions of fact (who did it?) belong in reports; essays deal in judgments (better, worse, what to do, what it means), which exist only by consensus within a group, so the reader is the batter, not the icing (V25 0:45–2:24). Every genre exists to get something done; a white paper persuades stakeholders to act, an essay helps readers understand, and chasing action first risks failing the essay's function (V25 2:24–3:18). "Essay" comes from the French for "to try": a writer attempting a hard problem (V19 3:19). Intending to help without the ability can do harm; the ability is argument (V25 8:27–9:25).
- **Steps:** 1. Is your question one of fact (write a report) or judgment (write an essay)? 2. Name the community whose consensus you're entering. 3. State in one line what the reader will understand afterward. 4. If your line is "they will do X", you may be writing a proposal. 5. Build the argument with claim, evidence and warrant (3.24).
- **Symptoms:** "I don't know my reader yet"; a topic chosen because it interests you; calls to action crowding out explanation.
- **When not to use:** Some pieces aim at both understanding and action; understanding comes first (V25 3:18). Earlier he framed an idea article as persuasion (V22 22:52); the V25 framing is sharper for essays.
- **Example:** Writer's definition: "I'm fascinated by medieval guilds." Reader's definition: "Freelance designers want to understand why they keep undercutting each other; guilds show how a trade set a price floor without a boss."
- **Sources:** V19 3:19; V22 22:52; V25 0:00–11:00.

### 8.2 The dream test and the impersonal turn
*(his terms: sublimate; be in relation, not relate)*
- **What:** A personal essay built from private symbols is like describing last night's dream: vivid to you, meaningless to others. Turn it outward. Change the question from "what does this mean to me?" to "what does it reveal about the world?" Keep the passion; aim it at an object the reader can see, and write as a guide pointing through a window.
- **Why it works:** Each private symbol is crammed with the writer's own associations, so readers get the surface but can't get inside (V43 0:21–1:11). Essay readers mainly want to use the piece to understand the world, not the writer (V43 3:08). He tells how Zadie Smith struggled at university writing personal, chaotic essays and changed course after reading writers who aimed their anger and wit at class and power (V43 1:26–3:08). Convictions come through as arguments, not confessions (V43 10:09). Ideas and the world are open to anyone; private experience only to those who share it (V43 8:50).
- **Steps:** 1. List key images; for each, does its meaning depend on memories only you have? 2. Find the object your passion attaches to (a text, a practice, a system). 3. Ask what it reveals about the world. 4. Turn each confession into a claim about the object. 5. Test: can you finish "after reading, they'll see that..." without "me"? (V43 10:09)
- **Symptoms:** Early readers who say "nice" and nothing else; "I" as the subject of most sentences; stakes that amount to "this mattered to me"; openings that establish the writer's identity before showing anything.
- **When not to use:** Not a call for bland objectivity; the passion stays (V43 9:46). Readers who come for the person, as in memoir, may be different (inferred).
- **Example:** Personal: "Quitting my job made me feel free for the first time." Impersonal: "The two-week notice period quietly shows who owns an employee's time: the company can end things today; the worker must give back fourteen days."
- **Sources:** V43 0:21–4:56, 8:50–10:09.

### 8.3 Zadie Smith's six arrows
*(her framework as he presents it)*
- **What:** Picture the essay as a rectangle of six arrows running clockwise, title in the center. Write the opening and closing lines first, then fill the four arrows between.
  1. **Introduction:** state your position and the promise the essay will keep; don't waffle.
  2. **Development:** the first layer of the argument; don't fear metaphor.
  3. **Devil's advocate:** stress-test it: steelman the other side, bring the strongest counterargument, or admit where the claim runs out.
  4. **Wind down:** no new threads; weave the existing ones together.
  5. **Reprise with variations:** earlier material returns, changed by everything since.
  6. **Conclusion:** arrive where arrow 1 promised.
- **Why it works:** The title in the center anchors you to the question when you drift; the arrows only go forward, so the only decision left is the next one, which frees thinking (V43 5:43, 7:55–8:50). Arrow 3 is what separates an essay from a speech: a speech asserts, an essay tries (V43 6:49–7:08). The reprise gives a sense of inevitability and a closed loop (V43 7:55).
- **Steps:** As listed.
- **Symptoms:** Essays that wander or double back; hedging introductions; no counterargument; new ideas near the end; an ending unconnected to the opening.
- **When not to use:** Writing the first and last lines first assumes you already know your position, so use it once the thinking phase has produced one, or as a revision check (inferred; he doesn't limit it, and in V42 he rejects templates imposed before thinking).
- **Example:** Title: "Why Meetings Multiply." Opening line: "Meetings multiply because they're the only work nobody can be blamed for." Closing line: "Cancel a meeting and you haven't saved an hour; you've made someone accountable." Arrow 3: some meetings prevent costly mistakes, so the claim holds for recurring status meetings.
- **Sources:** V43 4:56–8:50.

## 8B. Creative nonfiction from ordinary life

All from his close reading of one model essay (V21). He presents these as what he admires in creative nonfiction, not as rules for expository writing.

### 8.4 Creative nonfiction: aim beyond the facts
- **What:** Creative nonfiction is anchored in real events but aims past them at something deeper and more widely true. An ordinary experience becomes worth reading when you look at it closely enough to draw out something that holds beyond your own situation.
- **Why it works:** Readers read only when they perceive value; a piece that stays at "what happened" is a glorified journal entry (V21 2:32–3:09). You don't need a dramatic life; the craft is in probing the ordinary (V21 0:00, 35:33).
- **Steps:** 1. Write in one sentence the deeper truth the events point to. 2. Treat the events as material for it. 3. Check the ending lands somewhere a stranger could use.
- **Symptoms:** Strict chronology; feelings reported but never examined; an ending that only says how things turned out.
- **When not to use:** Memos, manuals and reports aim at plain information transfer (V21 1:30).
- **Example:** Before: "I switched from nursing to software. The bootcamp was hard; I got a job in March." After: "In the ICU, monitors beeped when something went wrong. In my first codebase nothing beeped, and I had to learn that the dangerous errors are the silent ones."
- **Sources:** V21 0:00–3:09, 35:33.

### 8.5 The opening image as seed
- **What:** The first image or scene introduces every main character and theme of the piece, like a musical's opening number; each later section expands something already planted.
- **Why it works:** The opening teaches readers what to track, and coherence comes from developing what they've already met (V21 4:20, 7:53–8:40).
- **Steps:** 1. After drafting, list every character and theme. 2. Rewrite the opening so each appears, even as a hint. 3. Plant or cut anything that first appears later.
- **Symptoms:** A key person or idea arriving halfway; scene-setting unrelated to where the piece ends up.
- **When not to use:** Expository and professional writing leads with the point (3.3).
- **Example:** "Dad's reading glasses sit on the hall table beside the car keys he's no longer allowed to use." The glasses stand for his mind, the keys for his independence.
- **Sources:** V21 4:20–8:40.

### 8.6 Selection is meaning
*(his image: pointing the camera)*
- **What:** Even plain reporting reveals a theme through which details you notice and which words describe them. Keep only details that bear on the theme, and describe them in its vocabulary.
- **Why it works:** Readers infer what matters from what the writer attends to; selection is an argument absorbed without being stated (V21 5:47–7:07).
- **Steps:** 1. Decide the theme. 2. Keep only relevant details. 3. Draw adjectives and verbs from the theme's vocabulary. 4. Cut inventory, even true inventory.
- **Symptoms:** Descriptions like lists; generic adjectives; a reader couldn't guess the theme from the opening.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Theme, waiting. Before: "The DMV had plastic chairs, a ticket machine and a TV." After: "The ticket machine had printed 212. The screen said 147. The man beside me had folded his ticket into a paper crane."
- **Sources:** V21 5:47–7:07.

### 8.7 The web of signification
- **What:** Build the theme into word families: a cluster of words for the theme set against a cluster for its opposite, with images drawn from the story's own world. To make two subjects feel kin, describe each in the other's vocabulary.
- **Why it works:** Contrast between word families carries the emotional arc without naming it; an image system from one environment makes the piece feel whole (V21 11:55–15:32). Crossing vocabularies enacts a link instead of asserting it (V21 10:34–11:26).
- **Steps:** 1. Highlight theme words in one color, opposite words in another. 2. Check each section leans one way and the balance shifts. 3. Replace metaphors from outside the story's world. 4. Borrow one word from subject A to describe subject B, and the reverse.
- **Symptoms:** Mixed metaphors from unrelated domains; an emotional turn stated ("I felt unsettled") but not built in the diction.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** Grief on a farm stays in farm language: "the field I let lie fallow", not "it hit me like a freight train".
- **Sources:** V21 10:34–15:32.

### 8.8 No exposition breaks
*(related: teach inside the scene)*
- **What:** Bring facts, definitions and research in through the story, not by stopping it. A fact arrives because a character meets it (I looked it up that night; she pointed at the model on her shelf), does a specific job, and the scene resumes. Speak as someone learning, not lecturing.
- **Why it works:** Facts carry meaning when met at a particular moment, and quotes with a clear job advance the piece instead of interrupting it (V21 9:57–11:26, 16:18–19:47). Stopping the story to explain feels like clunky biopic dialogue that crams in information (V39 4:58–5:39). An explanation tied to an action and one vivid image trusts the reader more than paragraphs of definition (V39 6:54–8:17).
- **Steps:** 1. Mark each place the draft stops to define or explain. 2. For each fact, write its job in the margin; cut it if none. 3. Attach it to an action, object or gesture. 4. Give one concrete image. 5. Return to the scene.
- **Symptoms:** Wikipedia-style paragraphs; "X is a Y that does Z" interrupting a story; a drop in energy wherever a term appears; orphaned quotes.
- **When not to use:** Reports and explainers can let facts stand alone (inferred).
- **Example:** Before: "A load balancer distributes traffic across servers." After: "Priya pulled up the dashboard: twelve green bars and one red one spiking. 'That's the traffic cop asleep,' she said, tapping the box meant to spread requests across the servers."
- **Sources:** V21 9:57–11:26, 16:18–19:47; V39 4:58–8:57.

### 8.9 Artistic license, always to a purpose
- **What:** Creative nonfiction isn't journalism; you may shape a real moment (for example, rephrase what someone said) only if the shaping sharpens a theme already in play.
- **Why it works:** One shaped phrase can crystallize the tension the piece turns on (V21 12:38–13:58).
- **Steps:** Check that any reshaping serves a planted theme; never invent for decoration.
- **Symptoms:** Invented detail that serves nothing.
- **When not to use:** Reporting, or any genre where readers expect verbatim accuracy (inferred; he doesn't discuss where the ethical line falls).
- **Example:** A landlord said the lease "might not be renewed"; in an essay about impermanence it becomes "a short-term arrangement".
- **Sources:** V21 12:38–13:58.

### 8.10 Arc devices for the personal essay
- **What:** Four structural moves from the model essay:
  - **The subject as mirror:** the thing observed becomes the lens for the writer's self-understanding (V21 24:26–25:11).
  - **Change of setting for the climax:** keep one setting until the culminating moment, then move (V21 32:03).
  - **Two time scales:** zoom from the local story to a larger frame (history, biology, a profession's long arc) at a turning point, then return (V21 22:33, 35:10–35:33).
  - **Plant and call back:** see 3.31.
- **Why it works:** They show change and scale without announcing them.
- **Steps:** Identify your larger frame and your mirror; save the new setting for the climax.
- **Symptoms:** Long observation that never loops back to the narrator; a climax in the same place as everything else.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** A piece on one family's move zooms out to thousands of years of migration, then back to the kitchen where the grandmother refuses to throw out her old pot.
- **Sources:** V21 22:33–35:33.

## 8C. Narrative idea and science writing

Mostly from his reading of a magazine article on the science of identical twins (V39).

### 8.11 Foreground what you saw
*(his terms: foreground and background; research, interviews, reporting)*
- **What:** Put in the foreground what you personally witnessed: people, rooms, props, a face, a revealing detail. Push research (papers, statistics, history, definitions) into the background, in support. A rounded piece draws on research, interviews and reporting.
- **Why it works:** Anyone who knows a lot wants to teach; readers want to be shown (V39 5:39–6:00). Research is now nearly free, so readers can get it themselves; what they can't get is you in the room, noticing (V39 11:11–11:29). Many writers do no reporting at all, so their foreground holds only information (V39 10:16).
- **Steps:** 1. Sort material under research, interviews, reporting. 2. If reporting is empty, go and observe. 3. Build scenes from reporting and interviews. 4. Bring research in behind them. 5. Keep only visual details that help answer the question (V39 9:54).
- **Symptoms:** An author who never left the office; definitions and statistics up front; people appearing only as quote sources.
- **When not to use:** Not about metaphor or visual language for its own sake (V39 8:57).
- **Example:** Before: "Mycorrhizal fungi form networks connecting plant roots." After: "Dr. Ruiz knelt, peeled back the leaf litter and held up soil laced with white threads finer than hair. 'That,' she said, 'is the plumbing.'"
- **Sources:** V39 5:39–11:29. (The driving question: 1.3; the judgment call: 3.25; the bridge from what readers care about: 3.11.)

### 8.12 Orient the reader in time
- **What:** Structure the piece in time and mark every jump with a plain time or place marker at the start of the paragraph.
- **Why it works:** The writer sees the whole piece like a map; the reader walks it one sentence at a time with no idea what's around the corner (V39 11:29–12:27). Phrases like "by the time I was born" are dull but tell readers where they are, and a setup can pay off only if readers stay oriented (V39 13:36, 16:37).
- **Steps:** 1. Draw the timeline of scenes. 2. Add an explicit marker at every shift. 3. Don't worry that markers are boring.
- **Symptoms:** Readers confused about when things happen; unsignaled flashbacks; tense doing all the work.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** "In the spring of 2011, the plant still ran three shifts." ... "Eight years later, one line was running." ... "Back in 1987, when the founder bought the site..."
- **Sources:** V39 11:29–13:36.

### 8.13 The one-word hyperlink
*(his word for the effect)*
- **What:** Use one accurate word from the piece's emotional or human thread to describe a technical subject, so the dry section stays attached to what readers care about.
- **Why it works:** A single loaded word pulls the earlier story and its stakes into the technical passage (V39 18:44–19:04).
- **Steps:** 1. List the vocabulary of your human thread. 2. In technical sections, choose one apt word from it. 3. Check it's accurate, not forced.
- **Symptoms:** Technical sections that feel cut off from the piece's heart.
- **When not to use:** Only where the word is accurate for the technical subject (inferred).
- **Example:** After an opening about a family bakery handed down three generations, gene regulation described as "the family recipe" every cell copies but not every cell uses.
- **Sources:** V39 18:44–19:04.

### 8.14 Interview the person, not the source
- **What:** In interviews, ask how the person came to spend their life on this subject and what their most important experience was, not only what their study shows.
- **Why it works:** Those questions open doors that fact-gathering never will, and often yield the biographical detail that echoes your question (V39 21:58–22:57).
- **Steps:** Add both questions to every interview; listen for the detail that links their life to your question.
- **Symptoms:** Interview notes with only facts and quotable claims.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** "What made you care about soil?" "My dad's farm failed the year I turned twelve."
- **Sources:** V39 20:04–22:57.

### 8.15 The engine of the story
- **What:** Make sure your opening contains one moment that starts everything, the first domino, which raises questions that pull the reader through.
- **Why it works:** A single discovery that raises "will they, whose is it, will they get away with it" demands answers (V12 33:24–35:03).
- **Steps:** Point to the one moment in your opening that drives what follows; if there isn't one, find it.
- **Symptoms:** An opening that sets a scene but raises no question.
- **When not to use:** He frames it for stories; in expository nonfiction the equivalent is the problem turn (3.5) (inferred).
- **Example:** A case study opens on the finance lead discovering that 40% of revenue comes from one customer whose contract ends in sixty days.
- **Sources:** V12 33:24–35:03.

## 8D. Dialogue and scenes

From V13, aimed at fiction and creative nonfiction, and useful for any anecdote or case scene in expert writing.

### 8.16 Stop author-splaining
*(his term)*
- **What:** Author-splaining is the author stepping into a scene to explain every emotion or intention behind characters' words. Cut adverbs on speech verbs and labels for emotional states; let words, silences and body language carry the feeling.
- **Why it works:** Pure dialogue invites readers into the meaning-making and feels more real, because in life we don't know what others are thinking (V13 5:00). An author who keeps explaining is like a friend who pauses a film to explain it (V13 1:21). When you step back, readers step forward (V13 6:24). A related fiction technique lets the narration's word choice carry a character's inner life without "she felt" (V7 8:18–9:51).
- **Steps:** 1. Assume the reader has ideas of their own. 2. Delete "-ly" adverbs attached to "said". 3. Cut phrases that name emotions outright. 4. Put yourself in the emotion; choose the words, unsaid words and gestures that would come from it. 5. Trust readers to read the dialogue against its context (V13 5:54–7:18).
- **Symptoms:** "She said bitterly"; "he replied sheepishly"; "she felt betrayed" right after a line of speech.
- **When not to use:** In expository nonfiction, stating your own claims explicitly is still right; this concerns characters' inner states in scenes (inferred).
- **Example:** Before: '"Fine," she said bitterly, feeling betrayed by her cofounder.' After: '"Fine." She closed the laptop before he reached the last slide.'
- **Sources:** V7 8:18–9:51; V13 1:21–7:18.

### 8.17 Ask why the text switches to dialogue
- **What:** Dialogue and narration do different jobs. At each switch into dialogue, ask what it does that narration couldn't.
- **Why it works:** Flat dialogue is really narration of speech events; dialogue earns its place when it carries more than one meaning (V13 7:18–7:51).
- **Steps:** For each exchange, state its job. If it only transfers information, narrate it or add layers.
- **Symptoms:** Characters trading information with no tension.
- **When not to use:** Not stated.
- **Example:** '"The meeting's at three," he said. "I'll be there," she said.' Better as "They met at three."
- **Sources:** V13 7:18–7:51. (The same question applied to any sentence: 4.11.)

### 8.18 Charged ambiguity
- **What:** Write lines that can be read more than one way, so readers fill the emotional gaps. Useful moves: answer a question with a question, hesitate, hedge, press.
- **Why it works:** Readers become co-creators, and their interpretation becomes part of them, which deepens the bond and gives them something to debate afterward (V13 9:45–10:52).
- **Steps:** 1. Resist spelling out every emotion. 2. Leave deliberate gaps or small contradictions. 3. Ask of each line whether different readers could take it differently.
- **Symptoms:** Every line has one possible reading; characters say exactly what they mean.
- **When not to use:** Ambiguity must be charged, not vague and vapid (V13 10:21).
- **Example:** "Got a minute?" "Is this about the review?" "It can wait." "Then why ask now?"
- **Sources:** V13 7:51–10:52.

### 8.19 Silent dialogue (ellipsis)
*(his term: ellipsis)*
- **What:** At the most painful moment, especially shame or humiliation, leave the words out. Report the scene from outside, as if the camera stood beyond a shop window: arrivals, departures, objects, movement. Summarize speech ("words were exchanged"). Passive constructions can show a character being moved by events.
- **Why it works:** People hide embarrassment rather than announce it; the narrative looking away reads as sympathy, and readers left outside must piece it together (V13 11:44–15:28). The strongest impact often comes from what is left unsaid (V13 15:28).
- **Steps:** 1. Find the scene of deepest vulnerability. 2. Describe it from outside. 3. Summarize or omit the speech. 4. Use it once, at the key moment (V13 18:34).
- **Symptoms:** Shame scenes that narrate every painful word and label the feeling.
- **When not to use:** It is one tool, not enough on its own (V13 19:06); tied to embarrassment and vulnerability.
- **Example:** "HR asked him to step into the small room. The door was closed. Twelve minutes later he came out carrying a cardboard box someone had already assembled."
- **Sources:** V13 10:52–19:06.

## 8E. Academic writing

### 8.20 Literature review: scholar first, method chosen
*(his terms: researcher versus scholar)*
- **What:** Be a scholar before a researcher: understand the field's connections, limits and implications before designing the study, and ideally draft the review first. Choose the review's scope and organization on purpose, like a method: recent state versus full history, whether to cover controversies, critique versus report, and the organizing principle. Match the type to the purpose: narrative (background), systematic (reproducible selection criteria), meta-analysis (pooled data), methodological (prior designs), bibliographic (command of the field).
- **Why it works:** Good reviews show an organizing mind at work (V1 10:40). A review written after the fact can only list; one written from understanding already contains judgment (V1 3:13–5:23). How you gather and synthesize determines what the review can show (V1 5:23–6:24).
- **Steps:** 1. Decide in writing: scope, controversies, critique depth, organization. 2. Pick the review type. 3. For each source, note connections, shortfalls and implications for your study. 4. Check against reviewers' common complaints: listing without ranking; chronological order; selection not driven by the hypothesis; theory and empirical sections that never meet; strings of separate summaries; staying inside one discipline (V1 9:44–10:40). 5. Synthesize (patterns and tensions, not one author per paragraph), set priorities (which studies matter most), and look across disciplines (V1 10:05–11:33).
- **Symptoms:** Paragraphs that each open with a different author and year; equal space for every study; no link between the review and the design choices; the review written last.
- **When not to use:** Chronological order suits a textbook, not an introduction to your own research (V1 9:44).
- **Example:** Before: "Ruiz (2018) studied sleep and memory in teenagers. Chen (2020) studied older adults. Obi (2021) looked at naps." After: "At every age studied, lost sleep weakens memory consolidation (Ruiz, 2018; Chen, 2020). Whether a nap restores it is disputed, which is the question this study takes up."
- **Sources:** V1 3:13–11:33.

### 8.21 Literature review: story not plot, cast the studies
*(his terms: plot versus story; casting; major and minor characters; allies and adversaries)*
- **What:** A list of studies, even one sorted under headings, is only plot. Tell the story in which your research looks like the missing puzzle piece. Cast the literature: two to five main characters get prominence and integral citations; supporting studies appear beside them as allies (sharing methods or findings) or adversaries (complicating them), usually in parenthetical citations; cut studies that serve no part of the story. Map before matrix: put the topic at the center of a mind map, branch out, and place each study, instead of starting from a spreadsheet of year, country and field.
- **Why it works:** Story makes readers receptive; "first this study, then that one, now mine" shows no value (V4 1:26–3:40, 7:03–7:58). Casting forces the value judgments reviewers ask for (V4 3:40–5:47). A grid imposes a grid on thinking and hides the pattern across studies (V4 6:36–8:16).
- **Steps:** 1. State the story in two or three sentences: what the field believed, what complicated it, what's missing that your study supplies. 2. Mind-map the studies. 3. Mark main characters; label the rest ally, adversary or cut. 4. Build a paragraph or section around each main character with its allies and adversaries. 5. Keep the matrix for filing only.
- **Symptoms:** Headings by year, country or discipline; one paragraph per study; a phone-book feel.
- **When not to use:** A matrix is fine as a filing system (V4 6:36).
- **Example:** "For a decade the field assumed A. Baptiste's 2014 anomaly sat unexplained until Costa showed it depends on temperature. That leaves open how the effect behaves in the cold, which is where this study begins."
- **Sources:** V4 0:34–8:16.

### 8.22 Academic tone by section, present tense for findings
- **What:** In the abstract and introduction, use personal language ("we show", "I argue") with researchers as the subjects of active verbs; in methods and results, keep the focus on procedures and data. State what your own research shows in the present tense.
- **Why it works:** He reports citation data from an analysis of high-impact articles: personal front matter draws more citations, personal methods and results fewer, and present-tense reporting of one's own findings more (V5 0:55, 5:53–10:43). The present makes research feel current rather than old news; tense has nothing to do with time (V5 9:53). He also reports that a simpler wording of the same ideas is rated better (V5 2:05–3:55).
- **Steps:** 1. Rewrite abstract sentences like "it was found that" with "we" as subject. 2. Leave procedures impersonal and past ("samples were collected in May"). 3. Switch claims about your findings to the present ("our data show").
- **Symptoms:** "This study was conducted..." and "our results showed that X was" throughout the front matter; methods sections narrating the researcher's feelings.
- **When not to use:** Procedures naturally stay past tense (inferred). Peer-reviewed fields expect more hedging than general nonfiction (6.11).
- **Example:** Before: "Our analysis revealed that the policy reduced turnover." After: "Our analysis shows that the policy reduces turnover."
- **Sources:** V5 0:55–10:43. (General personal presence: 6.13.)

## 8F. Expert nonfiction books

### 8.23 Choose the book type by goal
- **What:** A busy expert needs a model, not a blank page: pick a proven book type that matches your definition of success, then adopt its reader, shape and stance.

  | Type (his terms) | Goal | Reader | Shape and stance |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Personal Brand Builder (V17 0:00–9:13) | Turn mid-career experience into thought leadership | A community you want to lead | Starts from questions; stories logged weekly; language bent to the community |
  | New Authority (V17 9:13–14:44) | Be the go-to voice for commentary | People invested in the status quo | One argument that flips the status quo; boldest case, few hedges; answers objections; shareable lines |
  | Lead Getter (V17 14:44–20:56) | Qualified consulting leads | Prospects facing the problem just before yours | One urgent problem; method tested with clients first; short enough to finish on a short flight |
  | Booking Agent (V17 20:56–28:04) | Podcast and stage invitations | Mass market | One idea built to spread; broadly useful; never pitches your firm |
  | Legacy Book (V17 28:04–32:47) | Pass on hard-won wisdom | One person (a child, a mentee) | Idiosyncratic; no lesson without the story behind it |
  | Gift (V17 32:47–38:16) | Show a truth others haven't yet seen | People living through the same moment | Narrative; calm authority; about the reader's transformation; section test: understand, feel, or do differently |

- **Why it works:** The type tells you who the reader is, what length and stance to take, and which moves to make (V17 0:00). A mismatch sinks a book: his post-mortem of a creator's book finds an ideas book sold as a practical guide, with riddle chapter titles, off-purpose content, orphaned ideas and a preachy voice (V18 2:38–35:20). Social-media conventions don't transfer to books (V18 1:47).
- **Steps:** 1. State your goal. 2. Match a type. 3. Adopt its reader, shape and stance. 4. Study its model book. 5. Run the purpose chain (2.5) and write chapter titles as promises (3.14). 6. Sharpen the axe: plan the book before drafting the book, while writing to think in the meantime (V17 38:16).
- **Symptoms:** An ideas book marketed as practical; a lead book stretched into mass-market self-help; a booking book that reads like a brochure; an untested method.
- **When not to use:** Don't write a method book before the method is proven; he says this of himself (V17 20:01).
- **Example:** A bookkeeper's lead book: not "Everything About Small-Business Finance" but "Close Your Books in One Afternoon a Month", the problem just before "we run your finance function".
- **Sources:** V17 0:00–38:16; V18 1:47–35:20.

### 8.24 Position the book: Vow, Victim, Villain
*(his terms: the 3V framework)*
- **What:** Word of mouth is social: people recommend books partly to say something about themselves. Give the book positioning that supplies credibility and emotion as well as argument:
  - **Vow:** a promise so clear the wrong readers opt out. Name the change in the reader's life after the last page; pitch it in one sentence to real people, watch their faces, and freeze the wording strangers get at once (V16 3:21–7:47).
  - **Victim:** one specific person in pain, described in their own words and situations (V16 7:47–12:21).
  - **Villain:** a shared enemy the reader already dislikes but may not have named: a broken system, a lazy trend, a harmful shortcut. Name it plainly and show how the book equips readers to fight it (V16 12:21–15:14).
- **Why it works:** Confused readers don't leave five-star reviews, and vague promises attract readers who punish you (V16 3:21–3:44). Naming pain precisely makes the right reader feel seen and infer you can fix it (V16 11:02–12:05). A shared enemy builds belonging and shows what you stand for (V16 12:21–12:48). Experts lean on logic alone; identity and trust run on emotion (V16 3:01–3:21, 14:23).
- **Steps:** 1. Name the change (behavior, or an old idea replaced). 2. Pitch it aloud until strangers get it; lock it in as subtitle and back-cover line. 3. Name one reader and the moment they're stuck; cut chapters written for other readers. 4. Name the villain as a system or trend, never an individual.
- **Symptoms:** A title that names a topic, not a position; "for anyone who wants to..."; pain in category terms; a book that argues for something but against nothing.
- **When not to use:** Broad positioning is right for a mass-market bestseller or a booking-agent book (V16 8:36–8:58; V17 25:25). If you need a community of peers to accept you, concede before you attack (3.7; V19 35:19–40:04). Boldness is not license to be inaccurate; claims still need evidence (3.24).
- **Example:** Before: "For leaders at every level." After: "For the engineer promoted to manager three months ago who still reviews code at midnight because delegating feels slower." Villain: "The 40-slide welcome deck is where new hires' enthusiasm goes to die."
- **Sources:** V16 0:22–15:14.

# Appendix C. Tensions and how this catalog resolves them

1. **Separate phases versus recursive revision.** He insists on separating the writer's draft from the reader's draft (1.1), yet calls separating writing from revision "completely backwards" (V23 36:18). Resolution: the separation he defends is between *writer patterns and reader patterns*, not between two moments in time. Reread and restructure freely while drafting (1.13); don't shape for readers or prune until the reader phase.

2. **Rewrite from scratch versus salvage.** In V19 he demands a blank document with no copy and paste; by V50 he extracts points from the messy draft and relocates them, keeping the writer's voice. Resolution: structural rebuild, not line editing, using the V50 method (2.4).

3. **Discipline versus letting go.** The 2025 habit container (1.21) and the 2026 rejection of discipline for creative work (1.15, 1.9) coexist. Resolution: small, forgiving habits for routine and structured writing; for a long, self-chosen creative project that won't move, stop forcing and diagnose.

4. **Bold versus modulated claims.** Positioning books pick fights and avoid hedging (8.23, 8.24); elsewhere he recommends concession and qualifiers (3.7, 6.11). Resolution: boldness belongs to stance and positioning; courtesy belongs to how you enter the conversation; claim strength is set by genre and community, and is always backed by evidence.

5. **Templates versus his fixed sequences.** He calls templates a trap (3.1) yet prescribes index-first paragraphs, problem-first introductions and the six arrows. Resolution: these describe where readers look and what functions a genre needs, not content slots; they belong to the reader phase, after the thinking is done (V19 51:23).

6. **"You" as subject versus personal presence.** He removes habitual "you" (6.14) and encourages personal pronouns (6.13). Resolution: warmth and presence are good; making the reader the actor in sentences about ideas reads as lecturing.

7. **Topic chosen by familiarity versus by reader interest.** V32 picks topics that are familiar and link back; V33 picks what the reader cares about. Resolution: use reader interest to choose the passage's cast (5.9), then keep that cast in topic position with old-before-new handoffs (4.4, 4.6). When a human agent would be new and a familiar topic old, the familiar topic often wins (V37 13:39).

8. **Synonyms.** He allows close synonyms to continue a topic string (V26 8:40; V48 13:14) but forbids them for previewed or central key terms (V48 20:31). Resolution in 3.21.

9. **Point placement.** Point first is the default (3.3); creative nonfiction can hold the thesis to the end (V21 34:19); point last is an advanced, previewed option (V50 9:37–11:36).

10. **Readers as feedback.** He calls readers poor diagnosticians (V47 4:44) yet builds a course around early readers (V44). Resolution: readers locate friction; the writer diagnoses it with reader-pattern knowledge; a protocol such as the letter of response makes feedback usable (7.2, 7.5).

11. **Hooks versus expectations.** He praises small open expectations (5.16) and condemns hooks (3.10). Resolution: an expectation is a specific question the text answers; a hook offers attention-grabbing with no problem or value.

12. **Signposts.** He cuts metadiscourse (6.15) but requires headings, point sentences, enumerators and "for example" (3.19, 4.9). Resolution: keep signals made of content that do real navigation; cut announcements about the text itself.

13. **"Writing is thinking."** His core slogan through early 2026; by V40 and V42 he allows that some thinking happens off the page (sleep, the shower). Resolution: treat writing as the best tool for discovering and making thinking visible (1.2), with rest and the night shift as partners (1.18).

14. **AI.** Early allowances (AI clean-up of a transcript, V20; AI bullets from a voice note, V44) versus the later ban on AI at any stage of communicative writing (V45). Resolution: follow V45 for anything a reader will see; AI may navigate notes, handle logistics, and help find a word (0.5).

15. **Persuasion versus understanding.** V22 frames an idea article as persuasion; V25 says an essay's function is understanding. Resolution: default to understanding for essays; persuasion and action belong to proposals, white papers and positioning books (8.1).
