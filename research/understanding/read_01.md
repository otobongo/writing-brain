## V1: How to Write a Strong Literature Review WITHOUT A.I. (2024-02-29)

### 1. Who it is for, and their problem
Graduate students and early-career academics who must write literature reviews. They are tempted by a wave of AI tools that promise to automate the review. The review feels thankless: nobody ever praises one, yet peer reviewers attack it more often than any other section (1:07). Their real problem is that they see the review as a list of past work written at the end. Because of that, it reads as a catalog and gets criticized.

### 2. Core argument
Good literature reviews share one quality. They show **"evidence of an organizing mind at work"** (10:40). AI tools produce language, not thought, and journal editors can tell glib AI text from real scholarship (2:25). To produce that quality you must first become a **scholar**: someone who understands the field's connections, limits and implications before designing a study, rather than a **researcher** who treats the review as an afterthought (3:13 to 5:23). Next, pick a review method that fits your purpose, as you would pick a research method (5:23). Finally, avoid the standard peer-review complaints by synthesizing, prioritizing and reaching across disciplines (10:05 to 11:33).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Scholar before researcher [his terms: "researcher" vs "scholar"]
- **What it is:** a self-diagnosis of your relationship to the literature, plus a rule about when to write the review. His motto: "In a world of researchers, be a scholar" (4:13). His university writing center adds that you must become a scholar before you can become a researcher (4:33 to 5:23).
- **Why it works:** researchers treat their own experiment as the "meat and potatoes" and the review as filler. Scholars know the connections, shortcomings, limitations and implications of past work, and they use that knowledge to design new work. A review written from that understanding already contains judgment, and judgment is what editors look for. A review written after the fact can only list.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Ask honestly: am I writing this review after my study, as a formality?
  2. If so, and if the project allows, draft the review *before* you design the study.
  3. As you read, note for each source: how it connects to the others, where it falls short, and what it implies for a new study.
  4. Let those notes shape your research question and design.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** the review was written last. It reads like a catalog. It has no link to the design choices in the methods section. The writer calls it "something I have to get through."
- **When not to use it:** not stated. He says, sarcastically, that if you see the review as a catalog, you may as well let AI write it, since nobody will read it (4:33).
- **Example (mine):** a student studying remote-work productivity collects her data first and then lists twenty studies. A scholar-first version drafts the review first and notices that nearly every study measures *self-reported* productivity. That observation becomes the reason her study uses logged output instead, and the review now justifies the design.
- **Timestamp:** V1 3:13 to 5:23.

#### 3.2 Choose the review's method before writing
- **What it is:** treat the review's scope and organization as a methodology decision, made on purpose before drafting (5:23 to 6:24).
- **Why it works:** in research, the method must fit the research question. He says the same holds for the review: how you gather and synthesize past work determines what the review can show.
- **How to apply:** decide each of the following in writing before you draft.
  1. Coverage: only the most recent studies (the current state of the field), or an exhaustive history?
  2. Will you give time to controversies and disagreements?
  3. Will you critique each study closely, or report major findings?
  4. How will you lay it out: chronologically, by point of view, or by some other grouping such as country?
- **Symptoms:** the scope seems accidental. Some studies get critiqued and others merely mentioned, with no reason given. The organization is whatever order the writer read things in.
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):** a planning note at the top of the draft says: *"Scope: studies since 2016, when the new imaging method arrived. Organize around the two rival explanations. Critique the method of the three largest studies only."*
- **Timestamp:** V1 5:23 to 6:24.

#### 3.3 The review-type menu
- **What it is:** a taxonomy of review types for choosing the right one (6:24 to 8:56).
  - **Narrative review** (the most common, and the type the rest of the video assumes). The writer picks whatever research seems relevant, with no attempt to be exhaustive, and arranges it into a coherent pattern that builds the background for the study.
  - **Systematic review.** It states its selection criteria, like a methods section, so another writer who followed them would produce essentially the same review. This reduces author bias, for example citing only one's friends. Common in health sciences, where clinicians need to fold research into practice.
  - **Meta-analysis.** It pools data from studies that asked the same question and uses statistics to draw findings across all of them, rather than just cataloguing them.
  - **Focused reviews** on one aspect of past research:
    - A *methodological* review examines the designs and methods used before, so you can spot holes and choose your own method.
    - A *bibliographic* review, which his university assigns before the dissertation, checks that the student has deep command of the field.
- **Why it works:** each type answers a different need. The focused types serve the scholar-first goal directly.
- **How to apply:** match the type to your purpose. Background for your own study means narrative. A reproducible evidence summary means systematic. Pooled effect sizes mean meta-analysis. Preparing your design means methodological. Proving command of the field means bibliographic.
- **Symptoms:** a narrative review that claims to be exhaustive. A "systematic" review with no stated criteria.
- **When not to use it:** he gives the use cases above but no exclusions.
- **Example (mine):** a PhD student about to design an eye-tracking study writes a methodological review first. She finds that most earlier studies used fixed-head setups and chooses a mobile setup to fill that hole.
- **Timestamp:** V1 6:24 to 8:56.

#### 3.4 The peer-review complaint checklist
- **What it is:** he reads real reviewer comments on literature reviews as a self-check (9:44 to 10:40). He announces five and reads six. As a diagnostic list:
  1. **Listing without ranking.** The review names many papers but never signals which matter most, so the reader cannot see the author's perspective.
  2. **Chronological order.** Fine for a textbook, but not for introducing *your* research.
  3. **Selection not driven by the hypothesis.** The studies chosen and how they are described should follow from your hypothesis. Reviewers ask for the relevant themes and issues to be brought forward.
  4. **Theory and empirical sections that never meet.** A block on classic theorists, then a block on recent empirical studies, with nothing connecting them.
  5. **A string of separate summaries.** Each study is described accurately, but nothing evaluates or interconnects them.
  6. **Staying inside one discipline.** Especially in interdisciplinary work, the review fails to draw links across fields, which is where new connections come from.
- **Why it works:** these are the objections gatekeepers actually raise, so checking for them in advance saves a round of peer review.
- **How to apply:** read your draft against each item and fix whatever matches. He also warns against chronological order and points to a separate video on patterns of organization (11:33).
- **Symptoms:** listed in the checklist itself.
- **When not to use it:** these comments assume a review that introduces the author's own research. He says chronological order suits a textbook.
- **Example (mine):** a draft opens with a section on classic attachment theory and then a separate section on five 2020s app-usage studies. The fix is one bridging paragraph that shows which part of the theory the app studies test and which they ignore.
- **Timestamp:** V1 9:44 to 10:40.

#### 3.5 Don't just summarize, synthesize [his phrasing]
- **What it is:** go beyond reporting what each study found. Analyze the studies, show how they connect to each other, and show how they bear on your study (10:40).
- **Why it works:** the defining quality of a good review is an organizing mind. AI tools are "language producing machines," not thinking machines, so connection-making is the part only the writer can supply.
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each group of studies, write what they have in common, where they disagree, and why that matters.
  2. End each group with its bearing on your question.
  3. Make each paragraph's subject a *pattern or tension*, not a single author.
- **Symptoms:** paragraphs that each open with a different author and year. There are no sentences that compare two studies. The reader cannot tell what the reviewer thinks.
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Before:* "Ruiz (2018) studied sleep and memory in teenagers. Chen (2020) studied sleep and memory in older adults. Obi (2021) looked at naps and recall."
  - *After:* "At every age studied, lost sleep weakens memory consolidation (Ruiz, 2018; Chen, 2020). Whether a nap restores it is still disputed, which is the question this study takes up (Obi, 2021)."
- **Timestamp:** V1 10:05 to 10:40.

#### 3.6 Set your priorities [his phrasing]
- **What it is:** make explicit value judgments about which research matters more and which matters less (10:40 to 11:33).
- **Why it works:** a tool can list everything relevant but cannot decide importance. Ranking is the visible sign of the writer's judgment, and reviewers complain when it is missing (checklist item 1).
- **How to apply:** decide which studies are central and give them the most space. Mention minor ones briefly in relation to the central ones. V4 later turns this into "casting" (see 3.2 under V4).
- **Symptoms:** every study gets the same amount of space. There are no words like "the most influential," "a smaller study," or "the key exception."
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):** "Two trials anchor this debate. Three smaller cohort studies point the same way but share a sampling flaw."
- **Timestamp:** V1 10:40 to 11:33.

#### 3.7 Push the boundaries [his phrasing]
- **What it is:** look for relevant work outside your own discipline (11:33).
- **Why it works:** combining insights from different fields leads to new understanding. Reviewers of interdisciplinary work also ask for it (checklist item 6).
- **How to apply:** ask which other field has faced a structurally similar problem, then search there.
- **Symptoms:** every source comes from one discipline's journals.
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):** a review on handoff errors in hospitals draws on aviation research about cockpit communication checklists.
- **Timestamp:** V1 11:33.

### 4. Stances and warnings
- **Against AI writing tools for literature reviews**, and against influencers who promote them for money (1:07 to 2:25). His reasons: academics read and write for a living, so they are not fooled, and AI tools can never give you deep understanding of a field (8:56).
- **Against the review as a list** or catalog of previous research (2:25).
- **Warning:** the review is one of the main targets of criticism in peer review (1:07).
- No change of mind is expressed in this video.

### 5. Sources named
- His university's Writing Center, and the motto that scholars come before researchers (4:33).
- Real (anonymous) peer-review comments on literature reviews (9:44).
- No books or named authors.

## V2: English Professor Breaks Down Grammar Nazis (2024-03-08)

### 1. Who it is for, and their problem
General viewers and writers who worry about grammar "rules" and pedants. The format is a reaction video to a comedy sketch. Their problem: they cannot tell real rules from inherited myths, and they do not know that some non-rules are still worth following for reasons that have nothing to do with correctness.

### 2. Core argument
He cares about "good and bad rather than right and wrong" (0:21). His field is cognitive linguistics: what effect a sentence structure has on the reader's mind and ability to process it (0:21 to 1:11). He agrees with descriptivists that language changes. But he holds that reader psychology still gives firm grounds to call one sentence better than another. Some prescriptive rules are historical accidents: the ban on double negatives, and the ban on ending with a preposition. Others are real: pronoun case, subject-verb agreement, run-ons in writing, dangling participles. Even a false rule can be worth following in formal writing when the reason is the reader. His summary: learn the rules so that you can break them properly (19:54).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Judge by effect on the reader, not by the rulebook
- **What it is:** his master frame. Evaluate a choice by what it does to the reader's processing, not by whether a rulebook permits it (0:21 to 1:11).
- **Why it works:** languages change, so rules alone cannot settle quality. The reader's mind can, and gives a stable basis for saying one sentence works better.
- **How to apply:**
  1. When a rule is invoked, ask where it came from and what it protects the reader from.
  2. If it protects nothing, it is optional.
  3. If a non-rule still helps the reader, follow it as a matter of style, not law.
- **Symptoms:** edits justified only by "that's the rule." Pedantic corrections that do not help the discussion, which is his definition of a grammar Nazi (0:21).
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):** a colleague insists you never split an infinitive. Ask what the reader gains. In "to quickly review," moving the adverb gains nothing, so you can leave it.
- **Timestamp:** V2 0:21 to 1:11 and 19:54 to 20:42.

#### 3.2 The intentional double negative (litotes)
- **What it is:** a double negative such as "not uninteresting" is a way to *avoid stating the positive form* of a claim. It signals a mild or partial positive (3:17 to 4:24).
- **Why it works:** it lets you acknowledge some merit without overcommitting. He also debunks the "two negatives make a positive" objection. "Aren't any" and "aren't no" are equally double negatives. The London dialect's form became standard only because the grammar books were written there, which he calls a complete historical accident (2:12 to 3:17).
- **How to apply:** when you want to grant *some* quality without endorsing it fully, negate its opposite.
- **Symptoms of misuse:** not discussed. *(My note: stacked negatives with no deliberate hedging purpose are a separate problem. The earlier system summary cites a later video that advises writing in the affirmative, so a skill should present both as tools and choose by intent.)*
- **When not to use it:** not stated. His framing implies using it only when the intent is to avoid the positive.
- **Example (mine):** "The pilot results were not unpromising." This says there is something there without claiming success.
- **Timestamp:** V2 2:12 to 4:24.

#### 3.3 The remove-one-person test for pronoun case (and who/whom)
- **What it is:** a pronoun must show whether it is a subject (nominative: *I, she, who*) or an object, including the object of a preposition (objective: *me, her, whom*). To check a pair, take the other person out and see whether the sentence still works (5:38 to 6:23). Who/whom is the same distinction (14:45 to 15:13).
- **Why it works:** once the second person is removed, your ear handles the single pronoun correctly.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find the pair, such as "X and I" or "X and me."
  2. Delete "X and."
  3. Read what is left and keep the form that sounds right.
  4. For who/whom, answer the question with he/him. "Him" means "whom."
- **Symptoms:** "me and her" as a subject. "Between you and I." "With she and I."
- **When not to use it:** he observes that people who know the rule sometimes break it on purpose so they don't sound pedantic (4:56). *(My inference: that is a register choice for speech and casual prose.)*
- **Example (mine):** "The grant was awarded to Dr. Osei and I." Remove "Dr. Osei and" and you get "awarded to I," which is wrong. Write "awarded to Dr. Osei and me."
- **Timestamp:** V2 4:56 to 6:23 and 14:45.

#### 3.4 Sentence-final prepositions: not a rule, but a useful style choice
- **What it is:** English has no rule against ending a sentence with a preposition. He says 17th-century grammarians modeled English on Latin, where the construction is impossible. English is analytic, marking grammar by word order, while Latin is synthetic, marking it by word endings, so the rule does not transfer (6:59 to 8:47). Still, in formal and academic writing he recommends avoiding the final preposition (8:47 to 10:48).
- **Why it works:** he gives two reasons.
  1. It keeps the preposition next to its object or relative pronoun, so related words stay together.
  2. The end of the sentence is "prime real estate." The period gives it emphasis, and a closed-class grammar word like a preposition wastes that spot. Give it to an open-class word with substance: a noun, a direct object, an adverb (9:38 to 10:48).

  Writing, unlike speech, gives you time to rearrange.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find sentences that end in *for, of, on, with, to, about* and similar words.
  2. Move the preposition up to join its relative pronoun ("on which," "for whom"), or restructure so a content word ends the sentence. *(The restructure option is my extension. His stated fix is the first.)*
- **Symptoms:** sentences whose last word is a small grammatical word, so the emphasis falls on nothing.
- **When not to use it:** in speech, where final prepositions are natural. He also admits the fronted version can sound stuffier (8:47). He treats this as style, not correctness.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Before:* "These are the assumptions our model depends on."
  - *His style:* "These are the assumptions on which our model depends."
  - *Restructured:* "Our model depends on three assumptions."
- **Timestamp:** V2 6:59 to 10:48.

#### 3.5 Run-ons (comma joins)
- **What it is:** joining two independent clauses with only a comma. (Many handbooks call this a comma splice. He calls it a run-on.) The comma has no grammatical power. Historically it marked a breathing pause for someone reading a message aloud to a crowd (10:48 to 12:33).
- **Why it works:** a period marks a sentence boundary and a comma does not, so the reader loses the boundary. In speech the idea does not apply, because we all speak in run-ons (12:33).
- **How to apply:** where two complete sentences are joined by a comma, use a period. *(Or, my addition, a semicolon or a linking word.)*
- **Symptoms:** "clause, clause" where both halves could stand alone.
- **When not to use it:** speech, or transcribed speech.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Before:* "The sample was small, the effect was large."
  - *After:* "The sample was small. The effect was large." Or: "Although the sample was small, the effect was large."
- **Timestamp:** V2 10:48 to 12:33.

#### 3.6 Subject-verb agreement with compound and co-referring subjects
- **What it is:** a compound subject joined by "and" takes a plural verb. But when several noun phrases in the subject all refer to *one* entity, the verb is singular (12:33 to 14:45). His illustration is the opening of Nabokov's *Lolita*.
- **Why it works:** the verb agrees with the number of *things*, not the number of noun phrases.
- **How to apply:** count the real entities in the subject.
- **Symptoms:** "My advisor and mentor are…" when advisor and mentor are the same person.
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):** "Our lead author, editor and chief fundraiser *is* stepping down" (one person). Compare "Our lead author and our editor *are* stepping down" (two people).
- **Timestamp:** V2 12:33 to 14:45.

#### 3.7 Use the Oxford comma, consistently
- **What it is:** his stated position (he expects pushback) is to put a comma before the final "and" in a list and to use it every time (15:33 to 18:04).
- **Why it works:** it is often redundant, but sometimes leaving it out makes the list ambiguous, because the last two items can read as a description of the first. He also values consistency: you don't want to switch styles midway when an ambiguous sentence turns up (17:23).
- **How to apply:** add the serial comma to every list of three or more, unless a house style you must follow forbids it.
- **Symptoms:** a list whose final pair could be read as an appositive.
- **When not to use it:** it depends on the style guide. He names Chicago versus AP (15:33), and he grants that some people reasonably drop it where there is no ambiguity (17:48).
- **Example (mine):** "The panel included two funders, a journalist and a former minister." That could be two people or four. With the comma, "two funders, a journalist, and a former minister" is clearly four.
- **Timestamp:** V2 15:13 to 18:04.

#### 3.8 Dangling participles
- **What it is:** an *-ing* phrase set off by a comma attaches to whatever it sits next to, normally the subject of the main clause. If that subject is not the doer, the phrase dangles (18:04 to 19:54). He calls this generally a good rule.
- **Why it works:** readers assign the action to the nearest subject, so a mismatch produces a false or absurd meaning.
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each opening *-ing* phrase, ask who is doing the action.
  2. Make that doer the subject of the main clause.
- **Symptoms:** an opening "-ing" phrase followed by a subject that could not perform the action.
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Before:* "Using a larger sample, the error rate fell."
  - *After:* "Using a larger sample, we cut the error rate."
- **Timestamp:** V2 18:04 to 19:54.

#### 3.9 Learn the rules to break them properly
- **What it is:** his closing principle. He attributes the saying to the Dalai Lama (19:54 to 20:42). Know the structures as tools. Then question each rule by asking "who says?" You will often find it isn't a rule at all. He names split infinitives as another example.
- **How to apply:** before breaking a convention, be able to state what it does for the reader and why breaking it serves this reader better.
- **Timestamp:** V2 19:54 to 20:42.

### 4. Stances and warnings
- **Against pedantry** that corrects people when it does not help the discussion (0:21).
- **Descriptivists are right that language changes, but** reader psychology still lets us rank sentences (1:11).
- **Against inherited myths:** the double-negative ban (a dialect accident), the final-preposition ban (Latin envy), and split infinitives (mentioned).
- **Sides with the pedants on style:** avoid final prepositions in formal writing, for reader reasons rather than rule reasons (9:56). He admits he is taking the grammar Nazis' side.
- **Oxford comma:** use it, consistently (17:48).
- **Treats as real rules:** pronoun case, subject-verb agreement, run-ons in writing, dangling participles.

### 5. Sources named
- The field of cognitive linguistics (0:21).
- 17th-century grammarians who admired Latin. An 18th-century "preacher" who invented the preposition rule (caption "lath"; almost certainly Robert Lowth) (7:52).

## V3: Big Writing Mistakes Freshmen Must Stop Making (2024-03-20)

### 1. Who it is for, and their problem
First-year university students, and anyone moving from school writing to academic writing. Their problem: high school rewarded writing well about anything, with feedback on spelling, organization and vocabulary. So they hand in descriptions and opinions, while professors want arguments (3:21). He opens with his own story: an early essay said "I think," and the professor wrote "Who are you?" in the margin (0:51).

### 2. Core argument
University writing is a different task, not a harder version of the same one. The professors did not want him to write something better. They wanted something different (1:47). Unless an assignment says "summary," it wants **analysis**: not what something is, but how it works. And analysis is **argument** (3:43 to 4:05). We argue because each of us sees only part of the picture (4:37). Science publishes so that others can attack the work, and whatever survives becomes accepted knowledge. For questions an experiment cannot settle, such as value, meaning, or better and worse, written argument plays the same role (5:14 to 7:30). An argument has:
- a claim that is significant, non-obvious and debatable,
- evidence,
- a response to counterarguments,
- certainty calibrated with hedges and boosters.

"Writing is thinking": school writing reports what you know, while university writing discovers and then shares the discovery with a truth-seeking community (11:39).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Summary vs analysis: the watch test [his image: "don't tell them how to build a watch," reversed]
- **What it is:** the proverb says that when someone asks the time, you should not explain how to build a watch. In academic writing the reverse holds (2:32). Asked to write about a watch, a school essay describes each part: hands, dial, numbers. A university essay explains *how it works* (2:32 to 3:43).
- **Why it works:** professors grade the intellectual work. A tour of parts shows only that you can describe.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Check the assignment's verb. Unless it literally says "summary," assume analysis.
  2. For every descriptive paragraph, ask how the thing works, why it is that way, and what follows.
  3. Organize the paper around those answers, not around the parts.
- **Symptoms:** one paragraph per component or feature. Topic sentences of the form "X is…" or "X has…". No "because," "which means," or "so."
- **When not to use it:** when the assignment asks for a summary. He also stresses this is not a call to write *more*, since professors set page limits (2:32).
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Descriptive:* separate paragraphs on a city bike-share's stations, its prices and its app.
  - *Analytical:* "Bike-share works downtown and fails in the outer districts because its per-minute pricing assumes short trips."
- **Timestamp:** V3 2:32 to 3:43.

#### 3.2 Why argue: the flashlight model and the publish-to-be-attacked model
- **What it is:** two rationales that change how a writer approaches a claim.
  - **Flashlight** (4:37): in the dark, each person's light shows only part of the room, so we share views to see the whole.
  - **Publish to be attacked** (5:14 to 7:30): we publish to invite critique. What nobody can break is accepted as knowledge. Arguments do the same for questions a lab cannot answer.
- **Why it works:** once the writer sees the reader as a potential challenger, they naturally supply reasons, evidence and responses. An argument shows not only what you think but why.
- **How to apply:** before drafting, name the smart person who would disagree. Write so that your reasoning survives them.
- **Symptoms:** claims resting on the writer's authority ("I think," "in my opinion") rather than on evidence and reasoning. *(My reading of the "Who are you?" story. He never interprets the comment directly. He resolves it by explaining what an argument is.)*
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):** instead of "I believe remote teams are less creative," write "Three of the four field studies of idea generation found that remote teams produce fewer novel proposals. The fourth differs in one telling way…"
- **Timestamp:** V3 4:05 to 7:30.

#### 3.3 The three-part claim test [his criteria]
- **What it is:** a claim is a truth claim that should be:
  1. **Significant:** it develops our understanding of the topic.
  2. **Non-obvious:** the reader wants to see how you got there.
  3. **Debatable:** someone could disagree.

  The reader-reaction check is whether they think "that's interesting, I'd like to know more" (8:00 to 8:57). "Water is wet" fails. It needs no evidence and gives the reader no reason to spend energy (8:21).
- **Why it works:** readers spend effort only when there is a payoff. An obvious claim costs attention and gives nothing back.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write your claim as one sentence.
  2. Ask: could an informed person disagree? Would the reader need evidence? Does it change understanding?
  3. If any answer is no, narrow it, sharpen it, or pick the more contestable version.
- **Symptoms:** thesis statements that are definitions, truisms or announcements of topic ("This essay will discuss…").
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Weak:* "Social media affects teenagers."
  - *Claim:* "Private group chats, not public feeds, cause most of the social anxiety teenagers report."
- **Timestamp:** V3 8:00 to 8:57.

#### 3.4 Back a non-obvious claim with evidence
- **What it is:** the more interesting the claim, the more the reader needs evidence before accepting it (8:57).
- **How to apply:** after each claim, show the evidence and the reasoning that connects the two.
- **Symptoms:** a bold claim followed by the next claim.
- **Timestamp:** V3 8:57.

#### 3.5 Address the critics (counterargument move)
- **What it is:** because a claim is by definition something someone could dispute, the next thing the reader wants is to see that you have considered limitations and objections (8:57 to 9:31). His template is roughly: critics would argue X, but they miss A, B and C, so the claim holds.
- **Why it works:** it pre-empts the reader's resistance. It also shows the claim has already survived attack, which fits the publish-to-be-attacked model.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Before moving on from a claim, list who disagrees and what they would say.
  2. State the strongest version of their point fairly.
  3. Answer it, or narrow your claim to account for it.
- **Symptoms:** no "critics," "however," "one might object," or "this does not apply when" anywhere in the paper.
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):** "Skeptics will say the drop in crime began before the program started. That is true citywide, but in the three districts where the program ran first, the drop was twice as steep."
- **Timestamp:** V3 8:57 to 9:31.

#### 3.6 Calibrate certainty with hedges and boosters
- **What it is:** careful academic writers adjust how strongly they state a claim. Hedges lower certainty (*sometimes, usually, generally*) and boosters raise it (*absolutely, strongly, certainly*) (10:07 to 11:39).
- **Why it works:**
  - A claim stated too strongly, without regard to its limits or to people who disagree, gets rejected or attacked.
  - He concedes the popular complaint: heavy qualifying makes weak, unpleasant reading (10:07).
  - He explains the habit rather than defending excess. Academic writing is necessarily argumentative, and calibration shows you have thought about how strong your claim really is.
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each major claim, decide how certain the evidence makes you, roughly as a percentage.
  2. Choose one hedge or booster that matches that level.
  3. Do not stack hedges.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Over-hedging: "It seems as though X could possibly…", the parody he gives at 10:07.
  - Over-claiming: universal statements with no stated limits.
- **When not to use it:** he frames it within academic writing, which he calls "necessarily argumentative."
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Over-hedged:* "It could perhaps be suggested that the policy may have contributed."
  - *Over-strong:* "The policy caused the decline."
  - *Calibrated:* "The policy likely accounts for most of the decline in the two districts studied."
- **Timestamp:** V3 9:31 to 11:39.

#### 3.7 Use the writing center early
- **What it is:** practical advice. Book a writing-center consultation, ideally before your first paper, so a tutor can catch these issues before the professor does (1:07 to 1:47). This is how he himself learned what the professor wanted.
- **Timestamp:** V3 1:07 to 1:47.

#### 3.8 Writing is thinking [his phrase]
- **What it is:** in school we write about what we already know. At university we write to discover ideas, then share them with a community committed to finding the truth together (11:39 to 12:11).
- **How to apply:** treat drafting as the place where the claim is found, not merely recorded.
- **Timestamp:** V3 11:39.

### 4. Stances and warnings
- **Blames high school teaching** for rewarding surface qualities (spelling, organization, vocabulary) regardless of content (0:00 and 3:21).
- **Against summary** when analysis is wanted (3:43).
- **Against unsupported personal opinion** as the basis of a paper (the "Who are you?" story, 0:51).
- **Concedes** that academic over-hedging makes poor reading (10:07), while explaining its purpose.
- **Frames the demands as good:** writing becomes real intellectual work, not grammar and spelling (11:39).

## V4: Literature Review SECRETS from Hollywood Writers (2024-04-06)

### 1. Who it is for, and their problem
Graduate students and academics whose literature reviews "put everyone to sleep" and go uncited (0:00). Their problem: they list research like a phone book, or organize it by date, country or discipline in a spreadsheet matrix. That produces information with no narrative, and so no demonstrated value for their own study.

### 2. Core argument
He borrows a distinction from literary study, using *The Avengers* as the example:
- **Plot** is the events shown on screen.
- **Story** is the larger narrative behind them: backstories, motivations, what led up to the events (1:26 to 2:54).

A review that lists or sorts studies by criteria is only plot. It must tell a story in which your research looks like the missing piece of the puzzle (5:47). To find the story:
1. Cast the studies as major and minor characters, allies and adversaries, and cut those who contribute nothing.
2. Use a mind map rather than a matrix, so the grid does not straitjacket your thinking.

The purpose is persuasion: convincing readers your research is a meaningful contribution (8:45). Persuasion also depends on word choice. Using "I" to bring a real, flesh-and-blood author into the text creates a second, story layer. A reader who connects with that person is more persuadable (10:27 to 14:02).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Plot vs story [his terms]
- **What it is:** plot is the sequence of what appears. Story includes the backstory, motivations and larger narrative the plot belongs to (1:26 to 2:54). Organization is not story: sorting studies under headings is still "just the plot" (2:54).
- **Why it works:** people are drawn in by story, and a story makes them open and receptive to information. He points out that he opened with the Avengers for exactly this reason (3:40). A review that says "first this study, then that one, and now mine" cannot demonstrate value. It is just information (7:03 to 7:58).
- **How to apply:**
  1. State the story in two or three sentences: what the field believed or tried, what complicated it, and what is still missing that your study supplies.
  2. Make every paragraph advance that arc.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Headings by year, country or discipline.
  - Paragraphs each devoted to one study.
  - A "then… then… then" sequence.
  - The reader feels they are reading a phone book (2:06).
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Plot:* "In 2010, Ahn found A. In 2014, Baptiste found B. In 2019, Costa found C."
  - *Story:* "For a decade the field assumed A. Baptiste's 2014 anomaly sat unexplained until Costa showed it depends on temperature. That leaves open how the effect behaves in the cold, which is where this study begins."
- **Timestamp:** V4 1:26 to 3:40.

#### 3.2 Cast your literature review [his framing: "cast," major/minor characters, allies/adversaries]
- **What it is:** assign roles to studies as a screenwriter assigns roles to characters (3:40 to 5:47).
  - **Primary studies (main characters):** the most relevant and influential sources. They drive the review, so bring out their key findings. They get prominent treatment.
  - **Minor studies (supporting characters):** they rarely get paragraphs of their own and usually take non-integral (parenthetical) citations. They appear beside a main study to bring out its meaning through comparison and contrast.
  - **Allies:** studies that share a main study's methods or findings.
  - **Adversaries:** studies that contradict or complicate it.
  - **Cut:** any study that does not contribute to the one story. A good film has no pointless characters.
- **Why it works:** it forces the value judgments that V1 calls "set your priorities." The contrasts it sets up generate meaning, rather than leaving the reader to connect separate summaries.
- **How to apply:**
  1. List all candidate sources.
  2. Mark two to five as main characters.
  3. For each remaining source, write "ally of X," "adversary of X," or "cut."
  4. Build one paragraph or section around each main character, with allies and adversaries inside it.
  5. Use integral citations ("Kim (2021) shows…") for main characters. Use parenthetical citations for supporting ones.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Every study gets its own paragraph of similar length.
  - Every citation has the author as grammatical subject.
  - Sources appear that no later sentence uses.
- **When not to use it:** not stated.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Before:* three equal paragraphs on three studies.
  - *After:* "Kim (2021) remains the strongest evidence that the drug slows decline, a result two smaller trials repeat (Lopez, 2022; Nair, 2023). The one large trial that found no effect (Ortega, 2022) used a dose half as high, which is the discrepancy this study tests."
- **Timestamp:** V4 3:40 to 5:47.

#### 3.3 See the forest, not the trees; your research as the missing puzzle piece
- **What it is:** the trees are the individual studies, which he equates with the plot. The forest is the pattern across them. The goal is to reveal a pattern that makes your research look like the missing piece (5:47).
- **How to apply:** after casting, write one sentence naming the pattern and the gap-shaped hole your study fills.
- **Timestamp:** V4 5:47.

#### 3.4 Mind map before matrix
- **What it is:** writers usually organize sources in a grid, in a spreadsheet or a reference manager such as Zotero, with columns for year, country, field and findings. He likes having a filing system but says it is a bad *first* step (6:36 to 7:58). Instead:
  1. Write the research topic in the center.
  2. Branch out to the big topics, the "hooks," that come to *your* mind about it.
  3. Place each study where it fits.

  The map reveals the major and minor characters and shows where your own study sits (7:58 to 8:16).
- **Why it works:** a grid "imposes a grid on our thinking," and our minds are not organized that way. He cites the phrase "linear straitjacket" as something this has been called, without naming its source (7:03). The grid is why writers get lost in the trees.
- **How to apply:** keep the matrix for filing. Draft the review's structure from the map.
- **Symptoms:** section order that mirrors spreadsheet columns, for example by year or by country.
- **When not to use it:** he does not reject the matrix as a filing tool, only as the first step in thinking.
- **Example (mine):** the center reads "why hospital alarms get ignored." Branches: alarm frequency, nurse workload, alarm design, unit culture. Placing twelve studies on this map shows that eight cluster on frequency (main characters). It also shows that no study links design to culture, which is the missing piece.
- **Timestamp:** V4 6:36 to 8:16.

#### 3.5 Two kinds of "I": the author-function I vs the flesh-and-blood I
- **What it is:** he contrasts two reviews of *The Avengers*, from the *Wall Street Journal* and the *New Yorker* (9:17 to 11:22).
  - An "I" that points *inside* the text: "I'll explain later," "I argue." It refers to what Eric Hayot calls the **author function**, a persona existing only in the text (10:27).
  - An "I" that points *outside* the text, to a real person who sat in the cinema and could not follow the dialogue. That brings a new character into the text's world and creates the story layer (11:22).
  - Personality adds to this layer: pop-culture references and humorous asides (11:22 to 12:15).
- **Why it works:** the reader now watches a story with a character they can identify with. They are more willing to accept that character's judgments, and to follow them through the details to their conclusions (12:15).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Where you want the reader's goodwill, most likely the review's framing and your own motivation, let a real researcher appear: what you noticed, what surprised you, what you tried.
  2. Use the author-function "I" freely for signposting and claims.
- **Symptoms:** a voice so impersonal that nobody seems to have done or thought anything. Every claim floats free of any person.
- **When not to use it:** he lists cases where avoiding "I" makes sense (13:03 to 13:24).
  - When writing about *your own* research and you don't want to seem arrogant or self-congratulating.
  - When a topic is controversial and you don't want to name people. There you can present beliefs as though they exist on their own.

  Avoiding "I" tells a different story: a small researcher in the background with the spotlight on the research. That is legitimate, just less compelling.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Author-function:* "I argue in Section 3 that the sensor error is systematic."
  - *Flesh-and-blood:* "The first time I ran the model on field data, I assumed the sensor was broken. It wasn't, and that is what this paper is about."
- **Timestamp:** V4 8:45 to 13:24.

### 4. Stances and warnings
- **Against the phone-book review,** and against reviews organized by criteria without a story (2:06 to 2:54).
- **Against making a matrix the first step** (6:36).
- **Against the "don't use I" advice** as a blanket rule. He calls it common academic advice that tells a less compelling story (12:15 to 13:03).
- **Calls the idea that academic writing must be formal and impersonal a "widespread misconception."** He claims research shows informal, personal articles get more citations and appear in higher-impact journals. He promises the evidence in the next video, V5 (14:02).
- No change of mind is expressed. V5, however, qualifies his personal-voice advice by section, and it reverses one of his exceptions (see "Across these five videos").

### 5. Sources named
- His undergraduate English literature training, the source of plot vs story (0:34).
- *The Avengers* (the film).
- Zotero (6:36).
- "Linear straitjacket," credited only as something this has been called (7:03).
- Reviews of *The Avengers* in the *Wall Street Journal* and the *New Yorker*. The reviewers are not named (9:17).
- Eric Hayot's "author function" (caption "Eric Hayatt," 10:27).
- Unnamed research linking informal and personal tone to citations (14:02).

## V5: You're NOT a Bad Researcher! How to Easily Boost Citations (2024-05-12)

### 1. Who it is for, and their problem
Academic researchers whose work is not getting published in high-impact journals or cited. Their problem, as he frames it: they blindly follow academic style "rulebooks," which tell them to write complex, formal, impersonal, past-tense prose. That style keeps their ideas from reaching readers, so the research never has the impact it could (0:04 and 0:55 to 2:05).

### 2. Core argument
Ideas spread only if they leave the page and lodge in a reader's mind. An unread discovery is like the tree that falls in an empty forest (0:55). His tips come from an analysis of more than 30,000 high-impact articles, meaning those in top journals or those heavily cited (0:55). Three rulebook rules backfire:
1. **Complexity.** More complex wording of the same ideas is rated *worse* (Oppenheimer). Introductions with fewer articles and prepositions draw more citations (2:05 to 5:53).
2. **Impersonality.** Personal language in the abstract and introduction draws more citations. In the methods and results it draws fewer, so tone should follow each section's purpose (5:53 to 9:01).
3. **Past tense.** The present tense makes your own research seem current and alive, and tense has nothing to do with time (9:53 to 10:43).

The aim is to make the reader feel smart, not to make yourself look smart (3:55).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Write so the reader feels smart (simplicity over complexity)
- **What it is:** he opens with a deliberately inflated sentence that says "be clear" in pompous words, as a joke that demonstrates the point (2:05). He then cites Oppenheimer. Original essays were rewritten with the same ideas in more complex language, and the more complex versions were rated worse, across genres from admissions essays to research reports (2:05 to 3:55).
- **Why it works:** readers will do only so much work to unpack your message. Complex language keeps the idea on the page. Clear language lets it move into the reader's mind, and a reader who grasps an idea easily is more likely to pass it on through citation (2:58 to 3:55).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find words chosen to sound learned and replace each with the plain word.
  2. Keep the idea intact. He insists this is *not* dumbing down or talking to readers like a fifth grader. It is packaging ideas so they are easier to process (3:55).
- **Symptoms:**
  - Words that display the writer rather than inform the reader.
  - Sentences you would have to read twice.
  - Showing off vocabulary in introductions.
- **When not to use it:** not simplifying the *ideas* themselves.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Before:* "The implementation of the intervention resulted in a substantial diminution in infection incidence."
  - *After:* "The intervention cut infections by a third."
- **Timestamp:** V5 2:05 to 4:43.

#### 3.2 Syntax as the DNA of an idea: fewer function words [his metaphor]
- **What it is:** syntax is the code that copies an idea from writer to reader. Complex code causes "mutations," and the idea fails to arrive (4:43).
  - Words come in two kinds: **content words** (nouns, verbs, adjectives) and **function words** (prepositions, determiners such as articles).
  - Two versions with the same content words can differ greatly in function words. The one with more function words is heavier and branches more.
  - Introductions with fewer articles and prepositions get more citations (4:43 to 5:53).
- **Why it works:** fewer function words make a simpler sentence. A simpler sentence is easier for the reader to take in and more likely to be passed on.
- **How to apply:**
  1. In the introduction especially, look for chains of *of the / in the / for the / to the*.
  2. Collapse them. Turn "the X of the Y" into "Y's X" or a direct verb, and cut articles that add nothing.
  3. Check that every content word survives.

  *(My caution, not his: collapsing too far produces noun stacks such as "farm yield fertilizer effect analysis." Stop when the sentence reads naturally.)*
- **Symptoms:**
  - Long noun phrases held together by repeated "of."
  - Several prepositional phrases in a row.
  - Sentences where most words are small grammatical ones.
- **When not to use it:** he ties the citation finding to the introduction specifically. He does not say how it applies elsewhere.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Before:* "an examination of the effects of the use of fertilizer on the yields of farms in the region" (seven function words among six content words).
  - *After:* "how fertilizer use affects regional farm yields."
- **Timestamp:** V5 4:43 to 5:53.

#### 3.3 Personal front matter, impersonal middle
- **What it is:** match the degree of personal language to each section's job (5:53 to 9:01).
  - **Front matter (abstract, introduction):** its job is to motivate readers and get them interested. Use personal language, such as "we" and "I" with active verbs. It draws more citations.
  - **Middle, or empirical section (methods, results):** readers come here to examine methods and findings and do not care about the story. Passive, impersonal sentences keep the author out of view and put the work forward. Personal language here is linked to *fewer* citations (6:47 to 8:01).
- **Why it works:**
  - People relate to people, and readers are people. He contrasts a textbook description of a Klein bottle with a passionate TED-talk version. The information is the same, but the version with a person in it is more memorable and makes the audience share the interest (6:47).
  - Owning your ideas also makes you seem more authoritative. Readers attach the research's value to you (9:01).
  - Impersonal front matter makes ideas seem to float "in the ether," lifeless and detached.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Put "we show," "we found," "I argue" in the abstract and introduction, with the researchers as subjects of active verbs.
  2. Let the methods and results foreground procedures and data. Passive is fine there.
  3. If the work is good, say it is yours. "Don't be shy" (9:01).
- **Symptoms:**
  - An abstract full of "it was found that…" and "this study was conducted…".
  - Or the reverse: methods sections that narrate the researcher's feelings or journey.
- **When not to use it:** personal language in methods and results.
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Abstract before:* "It was found that sleep restriction was associated with reduced recall."
  - *Abstract after:* "We show that losing two hours of sleep cuts next-day recall by a fifth."
  - *Methods, keep impersonal:* "Participants were assigned to one of three sleep schedules."
- **Timestamp:** V5 5:53 to 9:53.

#### 3.4 Present tense for your own research
- **What it is:** describe your own research and findings in the present tense (9:53 to 10:43). Of two restaurant reviews, the present-tense one sounds more convincing.
- **Why it works:**
  - The present tense makes research seem current, relevant and applicable. The past tense makes it sound like old news: stale and forgettable.
  - People think the past tense is required because the research happened in the past. He says that is a mistake: "tense has nothing to do with time." He skips the deeper grammar (9:53).
  - Authors who describe their research in the present tense get more citations (10:43).
- **How to apply:** in sentences that state what your study shows, finds or means, switch to the present ("our data show," "this model predicts").
- **Symptoms:** "our results showed," "this study demonstrated," "we found that X was," used for findings that still hold.
- **When not to use it:** he limits the claim to your own research. He does not address other researchers' work, or narrating procedures step by step. *(My inference: procedures, such as "we collected samples in May," naturally stay in the past tense. The advice targets findings and claims.)*
- **Example (mine):**
  - *Before:* "Our analysis revealed that the policy reduced turnover."
  - *After:* "Our analysis shows that the policy reduces turnover."
- **Timestamp:** V5 9:53 to 10:43.

#### 3.5 Style as the vehicle of impact (framing, not a technique)
- He calls Watson and Crick good writers, and says their style is one reason their DNA discovery had such impact (0:04).
- "If a tree falls in the forest…" means a discovery nobody reads makes no impact (0:55).
- Use this framing to persuade expert writers that style work is not vanity. It is how the research reaches the people it could help.
- **Timestamp:** V5 0:04 to 2:05.

### 4. Stances and warnings
- **Against "blindly following" academic writing rulebooks.** He calls it "the biggest mistake going" (0:55 to 2:05) and "the lousy rule books" (9:01).
- **Against complexity used to look smart** (3:55).
- **Against uniform impersonality,** but explicitly **for** impersonality in methods and results (8:01).
- **Against the belief that research must be reported in the past tense** (9:53).
- **Warning:** do not dumb down. Simplifying the language is not simplifying the ideas (3:55).
- **Reassurance:** if your research is overlooked, the old rulebooks are to blame, not you (10:43).
- **Teaser:** a different kind of article, other than a standard IMRaD report, gets cited more often and for longer (10:43). The next video in the series presumably covers it.

- Watson and Crick (0:04).
- An analysis of more than 30,000 high-impact articles. The authors are not named (0:55).
- Oppenheimer's study on complex wording. *(My note: this is almost certainly Daniel Oppenheimer's 2006 paper on needlessly long words, whose title his opening joke echoes. He gives only the surname.)*
- A popular TED talk on the Klein bottle. The speaker is not named (6:47).

**1. One diagnosis at every scale: listing instead of thinking.**

**2. The reader's mind is the judge, and rules are tools.**
- V2 states the principle outright: good and bad by effect on the reader, not right and wrong (0:21). It also models the method in both directions. He discards a false rule (double negatives), then keeps another false rule for a reader-based reason (final prepositions).

These are one picture seen from different angles. Writing is how an idea enters and moves through a community of readers, and every technique is judged by whether it helps that. V1's "set priorities" and V3's "debatable claim" both require the writer to commit to a judgment others could contest.

A reconciliation a skill can use *(my inference; he does not connect the three videos himself)*: V3's problem was never the word "I." It was opinion standing in for reasons. "I" works when it introduces or takes ownership of a claim that is backed by evidence, or when it adds a human presence to the front matter. Keep it out of empirical sections. Consider leaving it out where naming people would inflame a controversy (V4 13:24).

**6. Hedging vs clarity: a latent tension.**
- V3 defends hedges and boosters as calibration and admits that over-hedging makes weak reading (10:07 to 10:43).

They fit together if calibration means *one* well-chosen hedge that matches the evidence, not stacked qualifiers. The earlier summary's "certainty calibration" (citing V3 and V50) develops this. A skill should treat hedging as a matter of accuracy, not of a formal register.

If the value of writing lies in the thinking it does, a tool that skips the thinking produces text without value.
