# Applicability: where the Writer Science method applies, and how

This file answers three questions for anyone building a writing skill from William Fitzpatrick's channel:

1. For each kind of writing, which of his techniques matter, which to turn down, and what changes?
2. For each writer situation (blocked, messy draft, flat draft, sceptical readers, several audiences), which moves apply, and in what order?
3. What does the method leave out, or cover only thinly?

**Conventions**
- Citations are `V# m:ss`, using the V-numbers in `writer-science-system.md`. A range is written "to".
- Everything is paraphrase. His coined labels appear as names (for example *interference*, *index*, *uneven U*), not as quotations.
- All examples are my own illustrations, written in his spirit. None are copied from his videos.
- **[inferred]** marks my extrapolation from his principles to a case he doesn't discuss.
- Fit ratings: **Core** (he built the teaching for it), **Strong** (his techniques transfer with small adjustments), **Partial** (some parts transfer, others don't), **Thin** (he says little; the skill must lean on inference).

Sources: the ten deep-read reports (`read_01.md` to `read_10.md`), checked against the transcripts in `readable/` for the genre-specific remarks.

## 1. Four gates that decide whether a technique applies

Before matching techniques to genres, the skill needs four facts about the job. Almost every misapplication of his method comes from skipping one of them.

### 1.1 Is it pointed writing?
His method is built for writing that answers a question or solves a problem for a particular reader.
- He says outright that his revision system is not for fiction, memoir, poetry, or any writing that makes no point (V50 0:48).
- His definition of an essay is writing that helps readers understand something better that they want to understand (V25 0:45).
- Clear and organised writing that solves nothing the reader cares about is still useless (V12 35:29; V19 8:34 to 9:44).
- In mid-2025 he narrowed his audience from "writers" to experts turning knowledge into value, and turned away fiction writers and memoirists (V16 8:58 to 10:24).

**For the skill:** the further a text is from "answer a question or solve a problem for someone", the less of the core pipeline applies. The sentence-level and practice tools still apply (see Fiction, §3.9).

### 1.2 Which phase is the draft in?
He separates three modes. Each has techniques that are harmful in the other two.

| Mode | What it's for | Where he teaches it |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Goal-free freewriting | Venting raw material. It is not even a first draft. | V41 22:17 to 28:56 |
| (b) Thinking draft (writer's draft, artist) | Working out what you think, often with a question at the top of the page. | V20 6:12; V35 6:40; V38 3:03; V42 16:31 to 18:34; V47 20:14 |
| (c) Reader draft (architect, adaptation) | Rebuilding the material around where readers look and what they need. | V19 21:46 to 29:54; V31 7:55 to 10:11; V47 9:18 to 18:15; V50 |

- Reader-facing checks must stay out of modes (a) and (b). That includes subjects, index positions, concision and code words (V33 14:11; V47 21:09; V20 5:22; V23 34:19).
- Bringing readers in too early causes block (V20 5:22) and bends your thinking toward the audience (V38 18:28).
- **The separation is one of orientation, not strict timing.** He calls separating writing from revision "completely backwards" if revision means later clean-up of a static text (V23 36:18). What he protects is the difference between writer patterns and reader patterns (V31 18:37; V47 0:00).

**For the skill:** always ask which mode the text is in before offering any fix.

### 1.3 How high are the stakes?
The full method is heavy: two documents, problem construction, the uneven U, early readers. He himself scales it down for routine writing.
- Nobody fears writing emails, because they only aim at information transfer (V23 30:53).
- Forcing functions probably work for low-value writing like emails and reports (V41 13:46).
- Transcribing fully formed ideas is how memos and emails get written, and it's the boring kind of writing (V17 3:34).
- The two-document process is framed for writing that makes new knowledge, not for summarising others (V47 16:21).
- In plain factual genres (memos, manuals, reports), aiming "beyond the facts" would be out of place (V21 1:08 to 2:32).

**For the skill:** scale the method to the stakes. For a routine email, point-first plus the reader's questions is enough. For a grant, an essay for cold readers or a book, run the whole pipeline.

### 1.4 Who is the reader, and what does the genre expect?
- There are no universal rules, because there are no universal readers (V12 7:30).
- He won't look at a client's text until the reader questions are answered (V24 3:22).
- Coherence lives in the relationship between writer, reader and text, not in the text alone (V49 8:21 to 10:14).
- Genre expectations are a legitimate *contextual structure* that readers bring with them, for example a scientific report's standard sections (V49 12:09 to 13:01).
- A genre is a sequence of functions that serve a goal shared by writer and reader (V20 17:08 to 21:38). Each genre has its own goal: essays aim at understanding, white papers at persuading people to act (V25 2:24 to 3:18).

**For the skill:** name the reader and the genre's function before judging any passage.

## 2. The dials that change by genre

His rules are mostly defaults with conditions. This table collects the settings that move from one genre to another, so the skill can adjust rather than apply one house style.

| Dial | His default | Where it shifts | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Where the main point goes** | End of the introduction, and at the top of each section and paragraph. | Point-last is an advanced option, mainly in creative nonfiction at section scale, and it needs an early preview of the key terms. Routine memos and emails [inferred]: first line. | V24 4:18 to 7:28; V50 8:23 to 11:36; V21 34:19; V46 8:20 |
| **Certainty** | Calibrated: direct and backed by evidence. No stacked hedges, no stacked absolutes. | Peer-reviewed academic writing expects more hedging. Books of ideas and "gift" books take the boldest case with no "it depends". | V50 30:18 to 35:11; V19 1:02:00 to 1:05:09; V3 10:07; V17 12:13, 35:19 |
| **Personal presence ("I", "we")** | Present where it builds the relationship and ownership. | Research papers: personal in the abstract and introduction, impersonal in methods and results. Personal essays: aim the passion outward, not at the self. Avoid habitual "you" as the grammatical subject. Consider dropping "I" where naming people inflames a controversy. | V5 5:53 to 9:53; V4 8:45 to 13:24; V19 1:00:28; V43 3:08; V18 27:28 to 32:35 |
| **Passive voice** | Chosen by what the reader should see first: topic continuity, focus, unknown or irrelevant agent, the stress position, agency. | Natural for procedures. Flag it wherever it hides responsibility the reader needs to know about. | V14 3:03 to 7:04; V15 13:05 to 17:20; V42 4:27 to 9:28; V14 5:51 |
| **Jargon and register** | Use the reading community's own code words, deliberately. | Unpack field shorthand for outsiders such as funders, investors and the lay public. He rejects "write at a fifth-grade level" everywhere. | V12 3:46 to 7:54; V35 13:27 to 18:54; V42 9:28 to 14:20; V47 12:59 to 16:21 |
| **Stance toward opponents** | Common ground first, concession, then the turn. | Picking a fight (the *villain*) suits positioning a book for sympathetic readers. With peers whose acceptance you need, tip your hat before you disagree. | V28 4:20 to 8:44; V22 8:14; V16 12:21 to 15:14; V19 35:19 to 40:04 |
| **Signposting (metadiscourse)** | Minimal in prose. Replace it with questions and the language of looking together. | Fine in speech. Plain enumerators ("first, second") for heavy, complex lists. Time markers in narrative. "For example" to mark a zoom into an illustration. Transitions at section joins. | V9 13:32 to 16:45; V37 11:59; V39 11:29 to 13:36; V32 17:01; V50 15:50 |
| **The genre's function** | Nonfiction moves the reader from where they are to somewhere new. | Essay: understanding first, action second. White paper: persuasion to act. Report or briefing: questions of fact. He also calls nonfiction persuasion (V20, V19); V25 is his sharpest statement. | V25 1:33 to 3:18; V20 21:38; V19 14:33; V22 22:52 |
| **Length and compression** | A sentence is as long as its load. Compress late, never while drafting. | Lead-getter books are short (finishable on a short flight). Semicolons and dense clause stacks are for revision passes. | V42 0:00 to 4:27; V23 34:19; V29; V17 20:35 |
| **Titles** | For cold readers: a curiosity trigger, often followed by three key-term nouns. Never an inside joke. | [inferred] For committed readers (an assigned report, a known boss) a descriptive title is fine, though still no inside joke. | V6 2:43 to 5:27; V46 1:04 to 2:55; V18 2:38 to 7:12 |

## 3. Genre by genre

### 3.0 Map at a glance

| Genre | Fit | Most important techniques | Turn down or adjust |
|---|---|---|---|
| Academic papers | Core (his origin) | Literature review as story; debatable claim with evidence and a bridge; problem introduction that corrects the field's model rather than claiming a gap; voice that varies by section; topic strings; calibrated hedging | Villain rhetoric; the no-hedging boldness of idea books |
| Grant proposals and applications | Strong | Rhetorical planning wheel; "what must they also believe?"; the model-error frame; unpacking compression for committees; point first; cost and benefit for this funder | Point-last; whimsy; anything that treats novelty as value |
| Business reports, memos, white papers | Strong (decision documents); Partial (routine reports) | V24's seven-step order; four reader questions; genre as a sequence of functions; reader-interest subjects; compression; agency | Creative-nonfiction moves; a full two-document process for routine reports |
| Emails | Thin (principles transfer) | Point first; the decision the reader faces; the reader-question test; compression; first-date test | Everything heavy: two documents, uneven U, problem construction (except cold or persuasive emails) |
| Online essays and newsletters | Core (2025 to 2026 focus) | Reader's definition of an essay; question not topic; title triggers; common ground, then problem, then solution; index and skim test; key terms; push-off conclusion; code words; early readers | "Write about whatever interests you"; one sentence per line; hooks |
| Personal essays | Strong (through a reframe) | Dream test; the impersonal turn; park bench; six arrows once the position is known; aim beyond the facts; plant and call back; lived experience | Problem-solution scaffolding in its strict form; point-first as a rule |
| Creative and narrative nonfiction | Strong | One driving question; foreground what you saw; teach inside scenes; time markers; setup and payoff; bridge from what readers already care about; web of signification; ellipsis | Metadiscourse; exposition breaks; point-first at section scale |
| Nonfiction books | Core (expert books) | Book type by goal; purpose chain; Vow, Victim, Villain; chapter titles as promises; fractal coherence; persona that partners rather than preaches; long-project process | Mass-market breadth unless the goal is reach; outline before thinking |
| Fiction and dialogue | Partial, outside his declared scope | No author-splaining; lines open to several readings; ellipsis; free indirect discourse; literary figures; sentence practice; copy work | Value and problem framing; point placement; index and discussion; code words |
| Social posts | Thin | Curiosity triggers; compression; memorable-line figures; one idea; code words | Platform craft (threads, formats, algorithms) is absent; he rejects value-free hooks |

### 3.1 Academic papers (research articles, literature reviews, theses, abstracts)

**Fit: Core.** He taught academic and professional writing for a decade (V35 0:50; V46 0:17). His first five videos are academic (V1 to V5).

**What to use, roughly in order**
1. **Literature review as an organising mind, not a list.**
   - Choose the review type and scope on purpose first (V1 5:23 to 8:56).
   - Tell a story, not a plot. Cast the studies as major and minor characters, allies and adversaries, and cut the ones that serve nothing (V4 1:26 to 5:47).
   - Map the ideas before building a grid of sources (V4 6:36 to 8:16).
   - Synthesise, set priorities and reach across disciplines, the fixes for the standard peer-review complaints (V1 9:44 to 11:33).
2. **Claims that can be argued.**
   - Significant, non-obvious, debatable (V3 8:00).
   - Answer the critics (V3 8:57).
   - Point, because reason, with evidence and an explicit bridge (V50 17:24 to 21:04).
   - For value questions, the inquiry method: question, evidence, reasoning, claim (V38 6:40 to 9:31).
3. **Introduction as a problem.** Status quo, concession, destabilising condition, cost, solution (V27 25:12 to 31:36; V28 4:20 to 14:52). Replace "nobody has studied X" with what the field's current model gets wrong and what that costs (V31 14:34 to 18:37).
4. **Voice that varies by section.** Personal and active, present tense for your own findings, in the abstract and introduction. Impersonal in methods and results (V5 5:53 to 10:43).
5. **Sentence and paragraph clarity.**
   - Characters as subjects and actions as verbs, with nominalisation triage. Field terms familiar to these readers may stay as nouns (V27 0:41 to 11:43, especially 9:21).
   - A short runway before the verb (V27 21:11 to 25:12).
   - Topic strings and old-before-new (V32; V37; V15).
   - The passive when it keeps the topic or the agent is beside the point (V14 4:37 to 7:04; V15 13:05). His Korean science students overused it; the cure was choosing by agency, not banning it (V42 5:01 to 8:28).
6. **Register.** Disciplinary code words are legitimate among peers (V42 12:42 to 14:20). Compression is efficient for peers (V47 14:32) and has to be unpacked for interdisciplinary readers or funders (V47 15:29).
7. **Certainty.** Peer review expects more caution (V19 1:02:55). Still aim for one calibrated hedge, with the scope narrowed in the subject noun, not stacked modals (V50 32:08 to 35:11; V3 10:07 to 11:39).
8. **Structure.** Standard sections such as IMRaD are contextual structure the reader brings, so meet them (V49 12:09). Inside each section, use index and discussion (V27 37:51 to 43:21).

**Adjustments and cautions**
- Journals expect a move that positions the paper in the literature. **[inferred]** Keep that slot, but fill it with the model-error frame, not the gap claim he criticises (V31 15:00; V46 4:50).
- The uneven U reflects his training in literary criticism, and he says to adapt it to your field (V50 28:54).
- His attack on "nuance" and "objectivity" (V16 0:22 to 3:01) concerns books meant to be shared, not papers. He explicitly keeps restraint for academic writing (V17 12:13).
- Academic signposting ("Section 3 discusses...") is the metadiscourse he wants cut from prose (V9 13:32 to 16:45).

*Illustration.* Gap version: "Few studies have examined how rural pharmacists handle drug shortages." Model-error version: "Shortage policy assumes pharmacists substitute drugs on the day of the shortage. In rural counties they stockpile weeks ahead, so national shortage data understates rural gaps by the time policy reacts."

### 3.2 Grant proposals and applications (including personal statements)

**Fit: Strong.** Funding is one of his most frequent client cases. He cites research funded (V28 0:00), startup grant funding (V29 0:51), grants and fellowships (V30 0:00), a large grant (V50 0:48), and a failed archive grant built on "nobody has read these yet" (V31 15:00).

**What to use**
1. **Rhetorical planning wheel before a word is written** (V30 4:46 to 11:13):
   - Purpose, written as a belief you need the panel to hold.
   - "What must they also believe?" Break that belief into qualities, and find real evidence for each.
   - Your role: junior or senior, and how confident you are about each claim.
   - The audience's knowledge and where they look.
   - The triggering event (the call) and the outcome (funded or rejected).
   - His Stanford personal-statement story shows that guide-compliant, error-free content can signal the wrong trait (V30 5:36 to 7:08).
2. **The model-error frame, not novelty.** "Nobody has done this" persuades enthusiasts, not funders (V31 15:00 to 18:37).
3. **Cost and benefit in the funder's terms.** Condition plus consequence, with the "so what?" test run against this panel (V28 8:44 to 12:20; V19 54:13 to 57:08).
4. **Unpack expert compression.** Committees sit outside your discourse community (V47 15:29 to 16:21).
5. **Point first and a working skim.** Skimmers get the point too (V24 5:17). Put each section's point in its index (V50 14:49 to 15:06). The indexes alone should tell the story (V46 12:12).
6. **Confident without arrogance, backed by evidence** (V19 1:02:00 to 1:05:09; V50 30:18).
7. **Stakes and revision.** Write for readers whose judgement you care about, and revise several times (V36 17:19 to 18:08).

**Cautions**
- He is against templates (V20 16:36; V31 2:16), but funders mandate them. **[inferred]** Treat the mandated headings as contextual structure the reader expects (V49 12:09), and apply index and discussion inside each one.
- Budgets, compliance, aims pages and scoring rubrics are not covered (see §5).

*Illustration.* Before: "This project will be the first to digitise the county's 19th-century court ledgers." After: "Historians of rural credit rely on bank records, which leave out most small farmers. The county court ledgers record thousands of small-debt suits those banks never saw. Digitising them lets researchers test whether rural credit collapsed in the 1890s or simply moved outside the banks."

### 3.3 Business reports, memos, white papers, proposals

**Fit: Strong for decision documents and white papers. Partial for routine status reports.**

**What to use**
1. **V24's seven-step editing order works as a memo method.**
   - Know the reader through four questions, including the decision they are trying to make (V24 3:01).
   - Lead with the one sentence the reader must accept, act on or use (V24 4:18 to 7:28).
   - Frame it as the answer to their problem, identifying the reader as uninformed, indifferent or resistant (V24 8:15 to 12:20).
   - Keep key terms stable, put the characters readers care about in the subject slot, state logical links explicitly, and fix mechanics last (V24 12:20 to 22:56).
2. **The memo as a document under stakes.** Amazon's memo culture: state the issue, trace the reasoning, ground the proposal in facts, and expect close reading (V36 17:19 to 18:08).
3. **Genre as a sequence of functions** (the Starbucks test). List the functions the genre needs and cut any paragraph that has none, however polished (V20 17:08 to 21:38).
4. **Know which genre you're in.** A report or briefing settles questions of fact (V25 1:33). A white paper's job is to persuade stakeholders to act (V25 2:24 to 3:18).
5. **Reader-interest subjects.** Put the client's business, not your feature, in the subject position, and use the passive if that's what it takes (V33 4:54 to 8:08).
6. **Interference.** A memo that narrates the investigation before the recommendation is a writer's draft (V31 18:37 to 21:37; V47 2:51 to 3:55).
7. **Compression.** Fillers, doublets, redundancy, phrase-to-word swaps, and the affirmative for rules and instructions (V29 2:36 to 20:02).
8. **Agency.** Name the actor where responsibility matters. The agentless passive can dodge blame (V14 5:51; V42 7:12).
9. **Structure signals.**
   - Headings built from the section's key concepts, not riddles (V27 34:45 to 35:23).
   - A theme preview for "three reasons" structures, with no synonyms for the previewed terms (V48 18:16 to 20:31).
   - Plain enumerators for heavy lists (V37 11:59).

**Adjustments**
- **Routine reports** are information transfer. Point-first plus the reader questions is enough. Skip problem construction and the two-document process **[inferred, from V23 30:53; V41 13:46; V47 16:21]**.
- Creative-nonfiction moves are out of place (V21 1:30 to 2:32). So are point-last and whimsy.

*Illustration.* Before: "Over Q2 the team reviewed three courier contracts, compared delivery times, and surveyed store managers, and on that basis we recommend switching to Courier B." After: "Switch to Courier B by August. It is on time 96% of the time against our current 81%, it costs 4% more, and store managers rank it first."

### 3.4 Emails

**Fit: Thin.** He has no technique specific to email. What he does say places email at the low-stakes end:
- Nobody fears emails, because they aim only at information transfer (V23 30:53).
- Forcing functions are fine for them (V41 13:46).
- They are typically the "transcribe what you already know" kind of writing (V17 3:34).

He does name "an email to a professor" as a genre worth running through the planning wheel (V30 7:46).

**What transfers [inferred, grounded in general principles]**
- **Point first, and the decision the reader faces** (V24 3:01, 7:28).
- **The reader-question test.** What would they ask? Make that question's subject your sentence's subject (V33 9:55 to 12:31).
- **The first-date test.** Who exactly is this for, and do you want to communicate with them (V45 1:08 to 3:33)?
- **Compression and the affirmative.** "Attach receipts for claims over $50" is better than a triple negative (V29 16:20 to 20:02).
- **For cold or persuasive emails,** the planning wheel and a compressed common ground, problem and ask can fit in three sentences (V30 7:46; V28 4:20 to 14:52).

**Skip:** two documents, the uneven U, index and discussion beyond "point first".

*Illustration.* Cold email to a professor. Before: "My name is X and I am very interested in your research on soil microbes, which I have followed for some time..." After: "Your 2023 paper found drought-stressed soils recover microbial diversity within a season. I'm testing whether that holds for irrigated farmland, and I'd value 15 minutes on whether your sampling method would carry over."

### 3.5 Online essays and newsletters

**Fit: Core.** This is the channel's main target from 2025 onward (V25, V28, V31, V33, V38, V43, V44, V46).

**What to use**
1. **The reader's definition of an essay:** help specific readers understand something better that they want to understand (V25 0:45 to 11:00).
2. **Question, not topic.** A question filters evidence and admits apparent digressions (V38 2:14 to 5:42; V39 0:56 to 3:20).
3. **Title.** One curiosity trigger plus three key-term nouns. No inside jokes (V46 1:04 to 2:55; V6 3:54 to 5:27).
4. **Introduction.**
   - Common ground, then problem (condition plus consequence), then a solution preview (V28; V46 3:52 to 8:20).
   - The two-vendor test: does the opening talk about you, or about the reader's problem (V28 1:15 to 4:20)?
5. **Body.**
   - Index and discussion at every scale (V46 8:20 to 12:43).
   - The skim test (V46 12:12).
   - A constellation of key terms (V46 13:26 to 15:51).
   - Value words at the top of paragraphs, and the "so what?" test (V19 49:20 to 1:08:47).
6. **Conclusion.** Return to the problem, show it solved, and push off to a larger question. Don't summarise (V46 16:18 to 19:48).
7. **Code words** of the target community (V35 13:27 to 18:54). **Reader-interest subjects** (V33). **Expectations** that pay off (V26 11:54 to 13:21).
8. **Process.** Two phases (V38 16:38 to 21:34). Early readers and the letter of response (V44 15:30 to 21:35).

**Adjustments and cautions**
- **Function.** Essays aim at understanding, with action second (V25 2:24 to 3:18). In V22 he praised an HBR article's persuasive, side-by-side tone (V22 22:52 to 23:32). **[inferred]** Default to understanding, and use the side-by-side tone because it produces understanding without defensiveness.
- **Against:** "write about whatever interests you" (V25 0:00; V31 7:00; V27 31:36); a new line for every sentence (V22 20:27 to 21:00); value-free hooks (V28 0:00).

*Illustration.* Topic title: "Thoughts on Pricing." Trigger plus key terms: "Why Our Cheapest Plan Lost Money: Support Load, Churn and Anchoring."

### 3.6 Personal essays

**Fit: Strong, through a reframe.** V43 is the main source, with V21 and V36.

**What to use**
1. **The dream test.** Are the images vivid only because of your private associations (V43 0:21 to 1:11)?
2. **The impersonal turn.** Ask what your subject reveals about the world, not what it means to you. Aim the passion outward rather than removing it (V43 2:43 to 4:05, 9:46 to 10:09).
3. **Be in relation, not "relate".** The writer and reader sit side by side looking at something together (V43 4:05 to 4:56, 8:50 to 9:46).
4. **The value question:** will the reader use this to understand the writer or the world (V43 10:09)?
5. **The six arrows, once you know your position.**
   - Title in the centre; write the first and last lines first.
   - Then: promise, development, devil's advocate, wind down, reprise, conclusion (V43 4:56 to 8:50).
   - **[inferred]** Use it in the architect phase or as a revision check, not as an opening outline. V42 rejects imposing structure before you know what you think (V42 19:27).
6. **Aim beyond the facts** (V21 1:08 to 3:09). The opening image as a seed, planting and calling back, and truths delivered through a concrete moment (V21 4:20, 8:40, 27:01).
7. **Voice from lived experience** (V36 1:10 to 5:55). **Discovery while drafting:** you start with idea X and end on a better idea Y (V11 5:30 to 8:33). **A vision first:** something you have actually seen to be true (V45 5:09 to 7:30).

**Adjustments**
- The point may emerge late, as long as its key concepts are previewed (V50 9:37 to 11:36; V21 34:19).
- A flesh-and-blood "I" is welcome (V4 11:22), but not an "I" as the subject of most sentences (V43 symptoms).
- Value is gentler here: helping readers see the world differently (V21 35:33), not a formal problem-solution architecture.

**Limit:** confessional writing that exists to be about the writer falls outside his model. He says essay readers aren't mainly interested in you (V43 3:08), and V50 excludes memoir (V50 0:48).

*Illustration.* Before: "Moving back into my childhood bedroom at 34 felt like failure." After: "The rent-to-income ratio in my city passed 45% the year I moved back home. My childhood bedroom is how a whole cohort is paying for that number."

### 3.7 Creative and narrative nonfiction (features, science narratives, case studies)

**Fit: Strong**, for the parts he covers (V21, V39, V13, parts of V12).

**What to use**
1. **One driving question** that lets apparent digressions in (V39 0:32 to 3:20). **Make the judgement calls,** borrowing an expert's credibility if you lack your own (V39 3:20 to 4:58).
2. **Foreground what you saw; background the research.** Draw on research, interviews and your own reporting. Research is now cheap; what you noticed in the room is not (V39 8:57 to 11:29).
3. **Teach inside the scene,** attaching each definition to an object or gesture (V39 5:39 to 8:57).
4. **Orient the reader in time with plain markers** (V39 11:29 to 13:36). **Set up, then pay off** (V39 13:36 to 16:37; V26 11:54).
5. **Assume the reader doesn't care** and build a bridge from what they do care about (V39 16:37 to 19:04). **Characters, not labels** (V39 20:04 to 22:57).
6. From V21:
   - Selection of detail carries meaning (5:47).
   - Research brought in inside the narrative (9:57 to 19:47).
   - A web of related words (11:55 to 15:32).
   - Artistic license only to a purpose (12:38 to 13:58).
   - Two time scales (22:33).
   - A change of setting to mark the culmination (32:03).
7. **Literary tools** (V12): ironic juxtaposition (7:54), synaesthesia (18:45), piling up (25:40), the engine of the story (33:24), understating the serious (38:43). **Silent dialogue (ellipsis)** for shame and vulnerability (V13 10:52 to 19:06). **Word order as a camera shot** (V26 1:50 to 3:37). **Embodied metaphor** (V36 5:55 to 9:58).

**Adjustments**
- Point-last is acceptable at section scale (V50 9:37). Time markers replace metadiscourse.

**Thin spots**
- The ethics of license: he grants it because creative nonfiction "isn't journalism" but doesn't say where the line falls (V21 13:12).
- Fact-checking and sourcing ethics.
- Reporting method beyond two interview questions (V39 21:58 to 22:57).

*Illustration.* Exposition break: "A heat pump is a device that moves heat rather than generating it." Inside the scene: "Marta pressed my hand to the outdoor unit. It was cold. 'It's pulling heat out of February,' she said, 'and pushing it into the living room.'"

### 3.8 Nonfiction books

**Fit: Core for expert books** (V16, V17, V18), supported by the process videos for long projects (V23, V40, V41, V42).

**What to use**

### 3.9 Fiction and dialogue

**Fit: Partial, and outside his declared scope.**
- He excludes fiction from his revision system (V50 0:48) and turned away fiction writers when he repositioned (V16 8:58).
- He treats fiction as the home of "write for yourself", which he calls a bad export to nonfiction (V38 16:38; V42 20:50).
- Fiction may run on personal taste (V18 14:32). Entertainment fiction sits outside his value-exchange model (V31 3:35).

**What transfers**
1. **Dialogue restraint** (V13 1:21 to 19:06):

2. **Point of view.** Free indirect discourse, where the narration takes on a character's diction without "she thought" (V7 8:18 to 9:51).
3. **Literary figures** (V12): enallage (2:45), polyptoton (12:09), antithesis (12:59), diacope (21:48), epistrophe (22:50), the forward-pointing pronoun opening (24:22), collecting names (40:37). **Deliberate rule-breaking** once you know the rule (V36 9:58 to 13:48).

**Order and cautions for the skill**
- **Tension: discipline versus letting go.**
  - V23 (Sep 2025) recommends about two months of deliberate discipline, morning sessions and small daily targets, and insists they must be kind and forgiving (V23 6:00 to 19:52).

### 4.2 Messy first draft

This is the situation his late videos address most directly (V19, V31, V35, V47, V49, V50, V24). A workable sequence:

0. **Is the thinking finished?** He wants the feeling that you have all the pieces (V47 12:03), a vision you could keep elaborating if the reader talked back (V45 9:06 to 11:41), and a point you can state. If not, go back to mode (b).
1. **Don't trust rereading, and don't line-edit.**
   - Rereading is remembering (V49 1:49 to 3:40). You are always the briefed reader (V50 2:00 to 4:24).
   - Line edits can't fix a draft built for the writer's thinking (V31 8:47; V35 12:09 to 13:02; V47 17:30).
   - Read vague feedback such as "dense" or "unclear" as friction flags, not causes (V47 4:44 to 8:19; V32 3:24 to 3:50).
2. **Reverse outline.** Label what each part actually does. Unlabelable parts are tangles or fluff (V35 8:11 to 9:41; V23 22:22 to 24:09; V47 19:13 to 22:07).
3. **Reader brief.**
   - Four questions about the reader (V24 3:01; V42 23:44).
   - Where the reader starts and where they must end up (V35 10:31; V25 7:59).
   - The planning wheel for high-stakes documents (V30 7:46 to 10:12).
4. **Frame.** Compare the point sentences in the introduction and conclusion, synthesise the sharper one, and put it at the end of the introduction (V50 4:24 to 9:37).
5. **Introduction:** common ground, problem, solution (V28; V46 7:04).
6. **Sections.**
   - Find each section's point and move it to the section's index (V50 12:16 to 15:06).
   - Order the sections by the questions the reader will ask, not the order you discovered things (V50 13:57).
   - Add transitions that round off and push on (V50 15:50).
7. **Anatomy of each point:** point because reason, evidence, bridge (V50 17:24 to 21:04). Answer the three standard objections (V19 57:59).
8. **Develop** each unit with the uneven U, and no hit-and-run quotations (V50 21:38 to 30:18; V18 24:03).
9. **Paragraphs.**
   - A topic-progression pattern chosen by purpose (V48).
   - The underline test on the first seven or eight words of each sentence (V27 18:19; V32 4:29).
   - Old before new (V15; V37).
10. **Sentences:** characters and actions, runway, nominalisation triage, handoffs (V27; V37; V26).
11. **Compression,** only now (V29; V23 34:19). **Calibration** (V50 30:18). **Mechanics** last (V24 21:04).
12. **Early readers** using the letter of response and the silent-author rule (V44 15:30 to 27:14).

**Tension to handle: rewrite versus salvage.**
- V19 and V47 say to write a new document without copy and paste (V19 27:22 to 29:54; V47 9:18 to 18:15).
- V50 extracts the points from the messy draft and relocates them, preserving the writer's meaning and voice (V50 0:00, 15:06).
- Both are structural rebuilds. **[inferred]** Reuse sentences only where they serve a turn on the reader's route.

### 4.4 Sceptical readers

**First, classify the reader.**
- Uninformed, indifferent, or resistant (V24 10:50 to 12:20).
- Three levels of awareness (V27 27:00).
- His planning questions: do they have reservations, already accept the premise, cherish the idea, or want a model critique (V20 23:28 to 24:25)?
- Readers actively look for reasons to doubt you, especially when an idea asks them to change (V19 57:59).

**Moves, in the order a piece meets them**
1. **Common ground before the challenge.** No opening hot take (V28 4:20 to 8:44). **Concede** what the old view got right (V22 8:14; V27 29:22; V19 35:19 to 40:04).
2. **Aim the "but" at the belief that blocks them,** not at a side fact (V24 8:15 to 10:27). **Frame it as a model error,** not a gap (V31 14:34 to 18:37).
3. **Cost or benefit in their terms** (V19 54:13 to 57:08; V28 8:44 to 11:53).
4. **Answer the three standard objections** (other causes, counterexamples, definitions) where they would arise (V19 57:59 to 1:00:28; V19 15:13 to 16:59). **Plan it as a decision tree** of their likely replies (V20 22:49 to 24:03).
5. **Evidence this reader accepts, plus a stated warrant or bridge** (V25 8:27 to 11:00; V50 17:24 to 21:04). Make the claim explicit enough that a critic can say whether they reject the evidence or the reasoning (V38 8:29 to 9:12).
6. **An invitation, not an attack.** Questions, "let's look", "notice", the shared mistake as "we" (V22 20:49 to 23:32). **Intuition pumps** let them reach the conclusion themselves (V22 28:15 to 30:25).
7. **Test the argument in the open.** The devil's-advocate arrow (V43 6:49 to 7:08). Mitigations set off in em dashes (V34 7:45). Pre-empt "this isn't new" (V22 26:27 to 27:16).
8. **Calibrate.** No "obviously", no stacked absolutes, no stacked hedges (V50 30:18 to 35:11; V18 32:35 to 33:25).
9. **For a divided audience,** an agentless passive can keep both sides reading (V42 6:37 to 8:28, the Darwin example).
10. **For readers who don't care yet,** start from what they do care about and build a bridge (V39 16:37 to 19:04).

**Cautions**
- The villain and New Authority boldness (V16 12:21; V17 11:39 to 14:08) are for positioning a book toward readers who are already partly sympathetic. With peers whose acceptance you need, concede first (V19 38:19 to 38:53).
- Don't let "confident" slide into "strident" without evidence (V19 1:03:38; V50 30:57).

### 4.5 Several audiences

**What he says directly is limited.**
- Writing for two or more audiences is fine, but it is like playing several chess games at once. You still have to think about each one (V25 2:24).

**Moves he supports**
1. **Pick the primary reader for each document, or each section.** Different groups ask different questions of the same sentence. Choose the group you're writing for, and let its question pick your subject (V33 9:55 to 12:31).
2. **Scale the consequence to each group.** One problem, different costs: careers for individuals, organisational drag for executives (V28 9:34 to 10:19). An academic presents the same argument to colleagues in conceptual terms and to the public library in terms of why it matters to them (V19 55:31 to 56:18).
3. **Separate documents for separate communities.** Skilled writers address specialists, then write a popular version (V42 14:20).
4. **Unpack compression for whichever group sits outside your field:** funders, investors, the lay public (V47 15:29 to 16:21).
5. **Sections can target different readers.** A paper's front matter draws readers in while its methods section serves examiners, and he varies the voice accordingly (V5 5:53 to 9:01). **[inferred]** The same logic lets an executive summary and a technical section use different registers.
6. **Code words belong to one community at a time.** "But", "however" and "although" are value signals across communities (V35 16:49).
7. **Breadth follows the goal.** Narrow the reader unless the goal is reach (V16 7:47 to 8:58; V17 25:25).
8. **Profile each audience separately** on the planning wheel (V30 7:46 to 10:12).

**Thin spots:** layered documents read at different depths by different people, documents that pass through gatekeepers to end users, and translating across cultures (touched on only through code-switching, V31 10:11 to 13:10; V42 11:29). **[inferred]** Index-first structure already serves this: skimmers get the point from the indexes (V24 5:17), and specialists read the discussion.

## 5. What the method doesn't cover, or covers thinly

### 5.1 Genres
- **Fiction craft beyond dialogue and sentences.** Plot, structure, character arc, pacing, POV systems, genre conventions. He excludes fiction from his system (V50 0:48) and gives only dialogue restraint (V13), free indirect discourse (V7 8:18) and literary figures (V12). *Skill:* use those; say that the rest is outside his method.
- **Poetry.** Excluded (V50 0:48). He only appreciates Merwin's punctuation choices (V7 15:56 to 19:31).
- **Memoir.** Excluded (V50 0:48). The personal essay is reframed away from the self (V43 3:08). The Legacy book is the nearest case (V17 28:04 to 32:47).
- **Social media craft.** Threads, formats, character limits, platform norms and analytics are all absent (§3.10).
- **Talks, scripts, slides.** Only remarks in passing (§3.11).
- **Technical documentation, manuals, UX writing, legal writing.** Touched only as "plain information transfer" (V21 1:30), affirmative rules (V29 16:20) and doublets (V29 5:39). No guidance on procedures, warnings, specifications or contracts.
- **Journalism and news.** Named only as a contrast: the journalism lede (V31 3:15), and "this isn't journalism" (V21 13:12). Nothing on news judgement, sourcing ethics or verification.
- **Academic subgenres beyond the introduction and literature review.** Discussion sections, responses to reviewers, abstracts beyond voice and tense (V5), theses as long documents, and peer reviewing are thin.

### 5.2 Craft layers
- **Visual formatting and document design.** Headings are covered (V27 34:45 to 35:23), as is skimmable formatting for lead-getter books (V17 20:35). Bullets, tables, figures, typography and layout are not. Mechanics and presentation come last in V24 (V24 21:04 to 22:56).
- **Presenting numbers and data.** Only the point that numbers need context (V15 5:16 to 7:03) and the uneven U's evidence level (V50 22:13). Nothing on charts, tables or statistical reporting.
- **Citation and referencing.** Integral versus parenthetical citations (V4 3:40), and research as creating tension (V19 40:04 to 42:56). Nothing on styles or formats.
- **Grammar and punctuation.** Deliberately de-emphasised: comma versus semicolon doesn't matter (V10 0:00; V31 10:11). Covered: V2's small set of real rules, the semicolon in antithesis (V12 13:51) and the em dash (V34). A skill needs general grammar knowledge for proofreading.
- **Rhythm and sound.** He dismisses rhythm as a proxy for music (V42 2:12) but admires DeLillo's ear (V10 0:00). There's no method for prose rhythm.
- **Humour.** Only asides (V4 11:22) and overstating the light (V12 38:43).
- **Delicate messages** (bad news, apologies, conflict). Only the point that a sentence can't be judged without its reader, shown by the pet-death example (V24 1:40 to 3:01).
- **Inclusive language, accessibility, plain-language standards.** Absent. He opposes reading-level targets (V12 3:46; V47 13:39).

### 5.3 Process and context
- **Collaboration and co-authoring.** Absent, apart from Voss's ghostwriter (V17 22:31) and the four-day Arthur and McCarthy edit (V22 1:34).
- **Professional editing of someone else's text.** His editor stance is to help the writer name the goal and work backwards from the reader, not to impose pet preferences (V18 14:32 to 15:26; V19 13:47). There's no protocol beyond V24's order and V44's peer letter.
- **Speed and deadlines.** The method assumes time for two drafts. For fast turnaround there's only the forcing-function remark (V41 13:46).
- **Very long documents.** Fractal coherence and a table of ideas per chapter (V18 21:12 to 25:41) are the only tools for continuity across chapters.
- **Writers in a second language.** He teaches EFL students and draws on them (V11 1:05; V23 19:52; V42 5:01) but offers no systematic ESL guidance.
- **Measuring outcomes.** Test with real readers (V19 1:11:07) and listen for recurring themes (V17 34:36). No metrics or testing.

Several claims rest on sources he doesn't name:

- his own analysis of 100 introductions (V28 0:00);
- research on intrigue behind the four curiosity triggers (V46 2:16);

### 5.5 A constraint the method places on an AI skill
The method limits what an AI helper should do:
- No AI in communicative writing at any stage, including "cleaning up" notes or voice memos (V45 7:52 to 8:47, 17:18).
- AI works on your thinking; it doesn't do your thinking (V40 15:54 to 23:23).
- Hiding AI tells is the wrong goal (V36 5:02).
- Coherence can't be automated, because it depends on the reader (V49 7:34 to 9:16).
- He does allow AI or a thesaurus to find the one precise verb (V50 33:26 to 34:15), and AI for logistics (V44 11:19 to 13:07).

**[inferred] What this means for a Claude writing skill built on his method:**
- Act as his editor and coach: ask the reader questions, locate problems by position, name the cause, and offer options with the reason for each (V27 4:54: writing is not paint-by-numbers).
- Don't invent the writer's ideas, and don't turn their notes into finished prose.
- When showing a fix, mark it as an illustration and give the writer the principle so they can do the rewrite themselves.

This tension (a writing skill built from a method that rejects AI writing) should be stated openly in the skill.

---

## 6. Routing summary for the skill

Before any advice, settle five things:

1. **Genre and function.** Is it pointed nonfiction? If not, which parts transfer (§3.9, §3.10)? Which genre function applies: understanding, persuading to act, or reporting facts (V25 1:33 to 3:18)?
2. **Reader.** Who exactly? Uninformed, indifferent or resistant? One audience or several (V24 3:01 to 12:20; V25 2:24)?
3. **Phase.** Goal-free venting, a thinking draft, or a reader draft (§1.2)? Offer only that phase's tools.
4. **Stakes.** Routine information transfer (light touch) or new thinking for cold readers (full pipeline) (§1.3)?
5. **Situation.** Blocked, messy, flat, facing scepticism, or several audiences (§4)? Diagnose the cause before prescribing.

Then set the genre dials (§2), apply the genre's priority techniques (§3), and say plainly where the method is silent (§5).
