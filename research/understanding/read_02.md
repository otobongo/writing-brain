## V6: What ANY WRITER can learn from Naval Ravikant (2024-08-26)

### 1. Who it is for and the problem it addresses
The video is for any writer who is not yet near the top of the craft. He pitches it at anyone below about 9 out of 10, and especially at online and business writers who want to persuade. He sees three problems. Writers get attached to the extra words in their early drafts. Titles give everything away, so they fail to pull the reader in. Sentences come out "flabby" and are hard to picture. (V6 0:18, 1:48, 4:12, 6:03)

### 2. Core argument
Naval Ravikant has said a short Scott Adams blog post holds the secret of his writing. William says he keeps the post open whenever he writes something important, and he draws three lessons from it. First, simple writing persuades, and "simple" means something concrete at the sentence level: cut every word that doesn't change the meaning. Second, the title is the real first line. It should open a curiosity loop rather than summarize the piece. Third, write the way brains organize ideas: put the doer in the subject and the action in the verb. That is why the active voice should be the default. He ends by limiting his own advice: this is a default, not a law. The passive survives in English because it does a job, and perhaps one sentence in twenty needs it. All three lessons rest on one idea: good writing is care, meaning careful attention to the reader and how the reader's mind works. (V6 0:00, 1:08, 2:43, 4:12, 5:27, 10:41, 11:47)

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 Prune extra words ("kill your darlings", which is his label for Adams's point)
- **What it is:** Cut any word you can remove without changing the meaning, especially intensifiers like "very". He says openly that "kill your darlings" is his phrase, not Adams's (V6 1:48). In his usage the "darlings" are not favourite passages. They are the extra words of an early draft that the writer has grown attached to.
- **Why it works:** Adams says simple writing is persuasive. A writer who believes "very" adds meaning is usually wrong, and the bare word is stronger. Pruning lets the important parts come through, the way a gardener's pruning lets the strongest branches flower [his metaphor]. Compression also makes an idea stick and spread. A short, memorable phrase travels, which he offers as the reason the post fits in about 250 words and why Naval does well on X. (V6 1:48, 2:43)
- **How to apply:**
  1. Reread the draft for words you would defend with "but it adds something".
  2. Delete each one on trial and reread the sentence.
  3. If the meaning survives, keep the cut. If the meaning really changes, restore the word.
  4. Treat your own attachment as the warning sign: the harder you want to keep a word, the harder you should test it.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** intensifiers (*very, really, quite, extremely*); qualifier stacks; phrases that restate; the writer resisting cuts to early phrasing.
- **When it does not apply:** He admits the long and short versions do not mean exactly the same thing (V6 1:48), but he sides with Adams that the extra word rarely earns its place. He gives no explicit exceptions. (inference) Keep a qualifier when removing it would change what the claim asserts, for example "rarely" versus "never", or a hedge that the evidence requires.
- **Example:** Before: "The new intake process is really quite a lot faster, and it is actually very easy to learn." After: "The new intake process is faster and easy to learn."
- **Timestamp:** V6 1:08 to 2:43.

#### 3.2 The headline is the first line (his framing)
- **What it is:** Readers meet the title before the opening sentence, so the title has to do the opening's job: make them want to read. Don't treat it as a **parking lot** [his metaphor] to fill with keywords that tell the reader exactly what the piece contains.
- **Why it works:** A title that gives everything away opens no curiosity loop, so the reader has no reason to continue. (V6 4:12)
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write the obvious descriptive title first ("How to X", "Three tips for Y").
  2. Ask what question you want the reader to have when they see the title.
  3. Rewrite it using one of the four curiosity triggers (3.3).
  4. Check: does the title leave a question that the piece actually answers?
- **Symptoms in a draft:** the title is a topic label or a keyword string; it states the conclusion, so the reader could skip the piece; it defaults to "How to..." or "N tips for...".
- **When it does not apply:** He grants that the keyword approach is fairly logical. His only objection is that it fails to spark curiosity. He names no exceptions. (inference) Reference material, where readers search for a specific answer, may still need a descriptive title. That caveat is mine, not his.
- **Example:** Before: "Tips for Writing Better Meeting Notes". After: "The Meeting Everyone Remembered Differently".
- **Timestamp:** V6 2:43 to 4:12.

#### 3.3 Curiosity loops and the four triggers
- **What it is:** A **curiosity loop** (a term he presents as already established) opens when the reader notices something they don't understand. He gives four ways to open one (V6 5:03):
  1. Pose a question or an incomplete puzzle.
  2. Signal that someone else has knowledge the reader lacks.
  3. Violate the reader's expectations, so they go looking for an explanation.
  4. Set up a sequence of events whose resolution the reader expects but doesn't yet know.

  He asks viewers to work out which trigger Adams's title uses, then answers: the third. The title surprises people who already want to write better and implies they have missed something. (V6 5:27)
- **Why it works:** It is the same pull that makes people binge TV series and detective novels. Once the reader sees a gap, they want to close it. (V6 4:12)
- **How to apply:**
  1. Work out what your reader already expects on this topic.
  2. Pick a trigger: a question, knowledge someone else has, a broken expectation, or a sequence with an open ending.
  3. Draft one title variant for each trigger.
  4. Keep the variant that raises a question the piece really answers.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** titles and opening lines that only state a topic; nothing is left open.
- **When it does not apply:** He doesn't say. (inference) The piece must close the loop it opens. A trigger the content doesn't pay off is bait, and it spends the trust that V9 and later videos depend on.
- **Example** (my titles for one piece about grant writing, one per trigger):
  1. Question: "Why Do Strong Proposals Get Rejected Twice?"
  2. Someone else knows: "What Reviewers Check Before They Read Your Aims"
  3. Broken expectation: "The Rejection That Funded My Lab"
  4. Open ending: "Three Drafts, Two Reviewers, One Deadline"
- **Timestamp:** V6 3:54 to 5:27.
- **My note:** The four triggers closely match the situations that provoke curiosity in George Loewenstein's 1994 information-gap paper. He doesn't cite a source.

#### 3.4 Agent in the subject, action in the verb (active voice as the default)
- **What it is:** He separates two layers of a sentence. **Syntax** is the fixed English order of subject, verb, then object or complement. **Semantics**, the meaning, has its own roles, and he names them **agent** (who acts), **process** (the action) and **goal** (what receives the action). In an ordinary active sentence the two layers line up: agent as subject, process as verb, goal as object. The passive voice scrambles them. The goal moves into the subject, the agent moves into a "by" phrase at the end, and the action leaves the main verb to sit in a past participle after a form of "be". (V6 6:50 to 9:42)
- **Why it works:** He names two problems with the passive.
  1. We read left to right and expect the doer in the subject position. The passive makes us wait until the end of the sentence to learn who acts. Actions don't happen on their own, so until the agent arrives the reader can't picture the scene. (V6 8:49)
  2. The passive shifts the weight onto "be", a verb for describing and defining that has no energy, and away from the action. (V6 9:42)

  He then widens the point beyond the passive. A sentence with an abstract pronoun as subject and "be" as its verb has the same flaw, and he shows it by rewriting a sentence from Adams's own post both ways (V6 10:41). Readers want characters in subjects and actions in verbs because that is easier to picture, and it engages the visual part of the brain (V6 11:07).
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each sentence, ask who or what performs the action.
  2. Check whether that doer is in the subject position. If not, move it there.
  3. Check whether the action is in the main verb, or whether the verb is "is/was/be" with the action hidden in a participle or a noun. Move the action into the verb.
  4. Watch for openings like "That is the way...", "This is why...", "It is the case that...", which put an abstract pronoun with "be" at the front.
  5. Keep a passive only when you have a reason for it (see below).
- **Symptoms in a draft:** "was/were/is + past participle + by..."; the doer turns up only at the end or not at all; "That/This/It is" followed by an abstract noun; sentences you can't picture.
- **When it does not apply:** He is explicit here. Don't turn this into a hard rule against the passive. He cites David Foster Wallace calling "never use the passive" an idiotic thing to teach. The passive is sometimes exactly what you want, though probably in no more than about 5% of sentences. Languages behave like organisms and don't keep a form that has no function (V6 11:07 to 12:04). He doesn't list those functions here. V8 and V9 show two of them: the agent is unknown, and keeping the passage's topic and flow.
- **Example:**
  - Passive: "The contract was reviewed by legal before the terms were changed by the client." Active: "Legal reviewed the contract before the client changed the terms."
  - Abstract pronoun plus "be": "That is the reason most pilot projects stall." Rewritten: "Most pilot projects stall for that reason."
- **Timestamp:** V6 6:03 to 12:04.
- **My note:** "Agent, process, goal" matches the transitivity terms of Halliday's systemic functional grammar (actor, process, goal). He doesn't name Halliday.

### 4. His stances
- **Argues against:**
  - writers' attachment to the extra words of early drafts, and the defence that "very" adds meaning (V6 1:48);
  - titles used as keyword parking lots that give the game away (V6 4:12);
  - "flabby, lifeless" sentences (V6 6:03);
  - hard rules such as "never use the passive". He sets "useless writing rules" against making active decisions about your own writing (V6 11:07, 12:04).
- **Core value:** the heart of good writing is care, meaning careful attention to the reader and their psychology (V6 5:27).
- **Warning:** he expects his own passive-voice advice to be over-learned as an absolute ban, and says that is not the point (V6 11:07).
- **He concedes** that "keep it simple" is old advice found in almost every style guide. Adams's contribution is making it specific at the sentence level (V6 2:43).
- **Changed his mind:** he states no change of mind in this video.

### 5. People and books named as sources
- Naval Ravikant, in his interview with Tim Ferriss, as the one who credited the post (V6 0:00).
- Scott Adams, blog post "The Day You Became a Better Writer": the source of all three lessons. He traces the persuasion lesson to Adams's training as a hypnotist (V6 1:08).
- David Foster Wallace, on teaching the passive voice (V6 11:07).
- He names no source for "curiosity loops", the four triggers, or the syntax and semantics roles (see my notes above).

## V7: Incredible Books that Were NOT Recommended to Me (2024-09-19)

### 1. Who it is for and the problem it addresses
This is not a lesson video. It is an unscripted tour of his bookshelf for viewers who follow his reading, prompted by David Perell's question about which book you keep recommending that nobody recommended to you (V7 0:49). He says he prepared no notes (V7 0:00). The problem behind it is implied rather than stated: much online writing advice comes from hustle culture, and the books that really shape a writer are often found by chance, not through recommendations (V7 1:29). For a skill, the video matters mainly for his values (writing as a gift rather than a commodity; read what has lasted) and for a few craft practices he mentions in passing.

### 2. Core argument
Walter Benjamin wrote about the connections he noticed while his books lay unsorted during a move. William, also moving, copies the method and follows associations from book to book. The thread that matters for writing:
- Writing is not a commodity to be optimized. Lewis Hyde's *The Gift* is his "manifesto" on this.
- A writer learns by absorbing great work closely. He once typed out a whole McCarthy novel, and Pound told Merwin to translate.
- Read what has lasted: Pound's "seeds, not twigs", which he links to the Lindy effect via Taleb.

Along the way he admires stylistic choices that carry meaning (Joyce's free indirect discourse, Merwin's missing punctuation and pointed capitalization) and praises Harari's clarity as the product of clear thinking. The second half moves into relationships, public institutions, climate and deep ecology. That material is his personal opinion, not writing instruction.

### 3. Techniques
This is not an instructional video, so there are fewer explicit steps, symptoms and exceptions. Where I supply them, they are marked (inference).

#### 3.1 Copy out a whole admired work
- **What it is:** Type out an entire book you love, word for word. He used a typewriter, not a computer. He did this with Cormac McCarthy's *All the Pretty Horses* over two or three days, after hearing that Hunter S. Thompson had done the same with *The Great Gatsby*. (V7 7:00)
- **Why it works:** He did it to get a feel for the book and to work out what made it tick, what it was that he loved (V7 7:00). (inference) Copying slows reading down to the speed of writing, so you notice every choice.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Choose a work whose effect you want to understand.
  2. Copy all of it by hand or by typing. Don't paste.
  3. (inference) Note the sentence choices that surprise you as you go.
- **Symptoms it fixes:** (inference) you admire a style but can't say what produces it.
- **When it does not apply:** He doesn't say. He presents it as a personal project. (inference) It is a large commitment. V10's sentence bird-watching and Mad Libs are the lighter, daily versions of the same idea.
- **Example:** A policy writer who admires a columnist types out ten of her columns. She notices that almost every paragraph ends on a concrete noun.
- **Timestamp:** V7 7:00 to 7:46.

#### 3.2 Translation as apprenticeship
- **What it is:** Ezra Pound advised the young W. S. Merwin to translate older Italian and Spanish poets into English. (V7 13:45)
- **Why it works:** Like copying, it brings other writers' voices into your own. Unlike copying, it also adds your own interpretation. (V7 13:45)
- **How to apply:**
  1. Pick a passage in another language.
  2. Render it in your own language.
  3. (inference) For writers who only have one language, translate between registers instead, such as a dense technical paragraph into plain speech, keeping every claim.
- **Symptoms it fixes / when it does not apply:** He doesn't say.
- **Example:** (inference) Rewrite a paragraph of a data-protection regulation as an explanation for a new employee, keeping every obligation.
- **Timestamp:** V7 13:45.

#### 3.3 Read seeds, not twigs
- **What it is:** Pound also told Merwin what to read: go back to the classics, the works that have survived, and skip the contemporary writers of his day. William ties this to the **Lindy effect**, which he learned from Taleb's *Skin in the Game*: what has lasted a long time is likely to keep lasting. He tells the origin story of the name (a New York restaurant where out-of-work actors noticed that shows which lasted tended to keep lasting). (V7 14:36 to 15:56)
- **Why it works:** Surviving the test of time is evidence of robustness. (V7 15:30)
- **How to apply:** When you choose models to study or copy, prefer works that have lasted.
- **When it does not apply:** He doesn't say. (observation) He recommends several recent books in the same video.
- **Timestamp:** V7 13:45 to 15:56.

#### 3.4 Free indirect discourse (a fiction concept he explains)
- **What it is:** A way of narrating in which the third-person narrator never says "he thought", yet the choice of words is coloured by a character's inner life. He contrasts it with first-person narration and with a camera-like third person, and places McCarthy in the camera-like camp because he rarely goes inside his characters. His example is the opening of Joyce's *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man*. Its baby-talk words and run-on rhythm tell you it is a small child's view, although no narrator says so. (V7 8:18 to 9:51)
- **Why it works:** The language itself tells you whose perspective you are in. (V7 9:04)
- **How to apply:** (inference, for nonfiction narrative such as case studies and scene-setting) Let the vocabulary and rhythm of a passage reflect the person it is about, without "she felt" or "he thought".
- **Symptoms:** (inference) narration that keeps reporting inner states ("she felt that...", "he realized that...") instead of letting the diction carry them.
- **When it does not apply:** He only discusses fiction. (inference) Avoid it in argument, where the reader must always know whose claim is whose.
- **Example:** Before: "The junior analyst felt overwhelmed when she saw how many comments her manager had left." After: "Forty-one comments. Forty-one, on a six-page memo, and the first one was about a comma."
- **Timestamp:** V7 8:18 to 9:51.

#### 3.5 Mechanics can carry meaning
- **What it is:** Merwin writes without punctuation, which makes his poems more fluid and visual, closer to images than sentences (V7 15:56). In one poem he capitalizes "the End" but never capitalizes "god". William reads this as a deliberate belittling that points at human arrogance (V7 18:45 to 19:31). McCarthy sits between the two: he uses periods and some commas.
- **Why it works:** Type and punctuation set tone and direct attention. A deliberate break from convention gets noticed.
- **How to apply:** (inference) If you break a mechanical convention, make it a choice that serves meaning. Otherwise follow the convention.
- **When it does not apply:** (inference) Ordinary expository nonfiction. He shows it in poetry.
- **Example:** (inference) A report that always writes "the Plan" with a capital P makes a policy read as an unquestionable authority.
- **Timestamp:** V7 15:56 to 19:31.

#### 3.6 Clear writing is the output of clear thinking
- **What it is:** Defending Yuval Noah Harari, he says Harari is an exceptionally clear writer because he is a very clear thinker with a clear view of the world. (V7 28:11)
- **Why it works:** Clarity on the page follows from clarity of thought.
- **How to apply:** (inference) When a passage won't come clear through sentence edits, stop editing and restate the idea to yourself first.
- **Symptoms:** (inference) repeated line edits that don't reduce the confusion.
- **Timestamp:** V7 28:11 to 28:51.

#### 3.7 Dramatize ideas
- **What it is:** Richard Powers's *The Overstory* dramatizes the ideas in *The Hidden Life of Trees*. He admires that Powers found a way to write a drama about trees at all. (V7 38:32)
- **Why it works:** (inference) Story makes ideas felt and memorable. This connects to V9's memory hack.
- **Timestamp:** V7 36:47 to 38:32.

#### 3.8 Unpacking the library (connections from disorder)
- **What it is:** Taking books out of their ordered shelves and seeing them in a heap revealed connections to Benjamin that the order had hidden. William follows associations from one book to the next without a plan. (V7 0:00; he repeats the idea at 39:21)
- **How to apply:** (inference, for generating ideas) Take notes or sources out of their folders, lay them side by side at random, and follow the links you notice.
- **Timestamp:** V7 0:00 to 0:49 and 39:21.

### 4. His stances

## V8: 3 Simple Concepts that'll Make Your Writing Clear (2024-09-29)

### 1. Who it is for and the problem it addresses
The video is for expert writers who share knowledge (email newsletters, YouTube scripts, papers) and worry that their writing is unclear. The stakes, in his framing: an idea's value doesn't matter if it never gets off the page and into the reader's mind. (V8 0:00)

### 2. Core argument
Clarity comes from how sentences are built and linked, and every fix is judged by the reader's experience. Three concepts do most of the work:
1. Keep the grammatical subject short so the reader reaches the verb quickly.
2. Put the action in the verb, not in "zombie" nouns.
3. Link sentences old-to-new so the passage flows.

The first two concern single sentences and the third concerns how sentences connect. Flowing prose lets the reader lose themselves, which he calls the hallmark of good writing. The key is an obsessive focus on the reader, not on grammar rules. (V8 0:00, 8:46, 11:53)

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 Short subject, early verb ("the tunnel", his metaphor)
- **What it is:** Keep the grammatical subject short so the verb comes near the start of the sentence. (V8 2:27)
- **Why it works:** He compares it to children holding their breath through a car tunnel. The verb tells the reader what is happening and how the other parts of the sentence relate, so readers "hold their breath" until it arrives. A verb near the start is a short tunnel. A long, front-loaded subject is a long tunnel that leaves the reader impatient and struggling. (V8 0:42 to 2:27) His demonstration pits a long noun-phrase subject ("a detailed explanation of why...") with its verb at the end against a version where a short subject is followed at once by an active verb.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find the main verb of each sentence.
  2. Count the words before it. If the subject is long, find the doer.
  3. Make the doer the subject and put its verb right after it.
  4. Move qualifying material ("of why...", "which...", "that...") after the verb.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** long noun phrases before the main verb; the verb lands in the last third of the sentence; subjects stacked with "of" phrases and relative clauses; sentence-final "is given / was carried out / has been shown".
- **When it does not apply:** He names no exceptions in V8. (inference) When a longer subject is the old information that links back to the previous sentence, V9 shows that keeping it first can be right. Weigh that against this rule.
- **Example:** Before: "The committee's decision to postpone the vote on the revised parking policy until the spring semester was announced yesterday." After: "Yesterday the committee announced that it will postpone the parking vote until spring."
- **Timestamp:** V8 0:42 to 2:27.

#### 3.2 Zombie nouns and the three-step fix ("zombie nouns" is a term he presents as existing; "three-step formula" is his)
- **What it is:** A **zombie noun** is a verb turned into a noun (*transformation*, *detection*, *destruction*). The action, which he calls the soul of the verb, has left its body and lurks inside a lifeless noun phrase. (V8 2:52 to 4:39)
- **Why it works:** He calls the verb the beating heart of the sentence. Readers look to the verb to learn what is going on. When the action sits in a noun, the sentence reads as dead. (V8 3:48)
- **Spotting tip** (his "pro tip"): zombie nouns often end in *-tion*, *-ment* or *-ence*. That doesn't catch all of them, but it is a good sign. (V8 4:39)
- **How to apply** (his three steps, V8 4:39 to 8:06):
  1. **Find each action and put it back in a verb.** Hunt the nominalizations and turn each into its verb (*transformation* → *transform*, *mutation* → *mutate*, *detection* → *detect*). Swap vague linking verbs for precise ones (for example, "was the result of" → *cause*). Drop verbs that only mean "be" (he treats "signal the presence of" as just "be there"). Make non-finite verbs finite.
  2. **Decide which character performs each action, and make that character the subject.** Ask "who or what did this?" for each verb. If you can't know the agent, use the passive; he does this for a quarantine zone that "was destroyed" by an unknown agent (V8 5:50 to 6:33).
  3. **Decide how the clauses relate, and join them.** Is it cause and effect? Time? Pick the main clause and connect the others with words that show the logic: an *-ing* participle for a consequence ("..., causing..."), *as* for simultaneous events, *since* for a cause, and a modal such as *could* where the original said "allowed X to". (V8 6:33 to 8:06)
- **Symptoms in a draft:** *-tion, -ment, -ence* nouns as subjects or objects; "of" chains; weak main verbs (*was, resulted in, allowed, signaled*); nobody visibly doing anything.
- **When it does not apply:** He shows two limits by example rather than rule. Keep the passive when the agent is unknown (V8 6:33). Some suffix matches are not zombies, since the tip "doesn't account for all of them" (V8 4:39). V8 doesn't list legitimate nominalizations. The earlier summary has such a list, sourced to other videos, so a later reader should confirm where it comes from.
- **Example:** Before: "The implementation of the new rota resulted in a reduction of overtime costs." Step 1: *implement*, *reduce*. Step 2: managers implemented, overtime costs fell. Step 3: cause and effect. After: "When managers introduced the new rota, overtime costs fell." Or: "Managers introduced the new rota, cutting overtime costs."
- **Timestamp:** V8 2:52 to 8:06.
- **My note:** Helen Sword coined "zombie nouns", and the character/action approach is associated with Joseph M. Williams's *Style*. He names neither in V8.

#### 3.3 Old-to-new flow ("old to new", a term he presents as existing)
- **What it is:** Begin each sentence with information the reader already has, usually something from the end of the previous sentence. End it with new information. (V8 10:03)
- **Why it works:** Readers don't read sentences one at a time. Like the pieces of a 3D wooden cube puzzle [his metaphor], each sentence has to show how it fits the whole (V8 8:59). Sentences that flow push off one another the way a swimmer pushes off the pool wall [his metaphor]. When the end of one sentence sets up the start of the next, the reader moves easily from idea to idea (V8 10:03). Flowing sentences let the reader experience **flow**, the feeling of losing yourself that he calls the hallmark of good writing (V8 11:53).
- **How to apply:**
  1. In a paragraph, underline the start and the end of each sentence.
  2. Find the sentence whose opening brings in a brand-new topic. That sentence breaks the flow.
  3. Rearrange it so it opens with the old item (often as a pronoun or a repeated word) and ends with the new one.
  4. Check that its new ending is what the next sentence picks up.
  5. Bonus: echoing a word (he links "use" at the end of one sentence with "used" in the next) strengthens the link.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** a sentence that opens with a noun the reader has never met ("Numerous products..."), right after a sentence that ended on something else; a paragraph that feels choppy even though each sentence is clear.
- **When it does not apply:** He gives no exceptions. (observation) His own fix uses the passive ("it is used...") to get the old item first (V8 11:15). That quietly shows the passive serving flow, which V9 then makes explicit.
- **Example:** Before: "Deep sleep helps consolidate memories. During this stage, the hippocampus replays the day's events. The cortex stores long-term memories in distributed networks." After: "Deep sleep helps consolidate memories. During this stage, the hippocampus replays the day's events. These replays are then passed to the cortex, which stores them as long-term memories."
- **Timestamp:** V8 8:46 to 11:53.

### 4. His stances
- **Against:** a focus on grammar rules. The key to good writing is an obsessive focus on the reader (V8 11:53).
- **Warning:** valuable ideas are wasted if the writing is unclear, because they never reach the reader's mind (V8 0:00).
- **He positions** the first two concepts as sentence structure and the third as connection, and says short subjects and action-filled verbs are not enough without flow (V8 8:06 to 8:46).
- He states no change of mind.

### 5. People and books named
None in the body of the video. The outro points viewers to V6 (Naval Ravikant). "Zombie nouns" and "old to new" are introduced as established terms without a named source (see my note above).

## V9: 4 Writing Hacks that Readers Love (2025-01-13)

### 1. Who it is for and the problem it addresses
The video is for writers whose readers can't focus or don't remember what they read. He especially has in mind the scientists and engineers he works with. The problem as he frames it: readers blame their distraction on short-video scrolling, but if readers can't focus, the fault lies with the writer. Good writing is designed, not accidental. (V9 0:00 to 0:32)

### 2. Core argument
Writing is like coding [his metaphor]. You are transferring an idea into a "machine", the reader's mind. Amateur programmers rely on memorized scripts or AI no-code tools. Great programmers understand how the machine processes information. So writers should drop surface tricks and study four features of how readers process text, then shape the writing to fit (V9 0:32 to 1:55):
1. Readers track two levels at once, what the sentence is about and what the passage is about, so topics must be consistent.
2. Flow comes from linking old to new, which can require the passive voice that rule-based teachers forbid.
3. Memory favours images, human action and the senses over bare information, so write concretely.
4. Signposting about the text itself (metadiscourse) is a leftover from oral rhetoric. Replace it with conversational moves in which writer and reader move and look together.

Throughout he contrasts writing **rules** with writing **tools** (V9 6:01).

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 Two skis: sentence topic and passage topic ("two skis", his metaphor)
- **What it is:** Readers manage two levels of reading at once, like two skis that have to stay parallel. Ski one: what is this sentence about? That is its **topic**, which readers take to be whatever they meet first, the "star of the show". Ski two: what is this passage about? When sentence topics jump around, the skis split and the reader falls. (V9 1:55 to 4:24)
- **Why it works:** We read left to right, so the first thing in a sentence is taken as its topic (V9 1:55). Writing is read at different levels of resolution, like an impressionist painting [his metaphor]. The larger unit (paragraph, section, chapter, book) sets the context for interpreting each sentence, so readers must see how every sentence fits that context. That is why advice about sentences alone isn't enough. (V9 4:08 to 4:24) His demonstration takes a passage adapted from Pinker whose sentences start with language, biology, children, "you", every individual and general abilities. Each sentence is clear alone, but the passage is hard to follow.
- **How to apply** (his three steps, V9 4:24 to 5:35):
  1. Decide what the passage is about, choose each sentence's topic on purpose, and try to make it the grammatical subject.
  2. Make topics short, concrete and familiar. Readers can't picture an abstract topic such as "the more general abilities to process information". Clear writing is visual and engages the mind's eye.
  3. Don't vary topics just for variety. Repeating the topic shows the reader what the passage is about as a whole.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** listing the first few words of each sentence yields a scatter of unrelated nouns; you can't say in one word what the paragraph is about; long, abstract subjects; synonyms or new subjects brought in only to avoid repeating a word.
- **When it does not apply:** He doesn't say. (inference) Later videos (V48 in the index) name other valid topic patterns, such as linking and whole-to-parts. V9 only teaches the constant-topic version. His own rewrite repeats "language" four times in a row, which is the constant-topic pattern.
- **Example:** Before: "Remote work saves employees time. Commuting hours disappear. Managers worry about oversight. Productivity measurements vary widely across studies. Isolation affects some staff." After: "Remote workers gain back the hours they once spent commuting. They are harder for managers to oversee, and studies disagree about whether they produce more. Some of them also report feeling isolated."
- **Timestamp:** V9 1:55 to 6:01.

#### 3.2 Use the passive when it keeps the flow (the relay race, his metaphor)
- **What it is:** He calls this hack a "trigger warning" because rule-based teachers forbid it. Choose active or passive by what the passage needs at the start and end of the sentence, not by rule. (V9 6:01 to 9:17)
- **Why it works:** His demonstration uses a passage by Csikszentmihalyi in which every sentence has "happiness" as its topic, followed by a sentence about "people who..." He offers two versions of the missing middle sentence. The active version starts with "each person". The passive version starts with happiness and ends with "by each person". The passive fits better for two reasons: it continues the passage's topic, and its last words introduce the character the next sentence starts with. The active version reverses both links. (V9 6:41 to 9:17) Sentences should behave like runners in a relay race [his metaphor], each picking up where the last one left off. Old information goes first, and new, unexpected information goes last (V9 9:17).
- **How to apply:**
  1. When a sentence sits between two others, ask what the previous sentence ended on (or what the running topic is) and what the next sentence begins with.
  2. Put the old or topic item at the front, even if that makes the sentence passive.
  3. Put the item the next sentence needs at the end, even if it lands in a "by" phrase.
  4. Test by reading all three sentences aloud and comparing both versions.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** an active sentence that breaks a run of shared topics; the character that the next sentence depends on is buried mid-sentence; the writer has turned every passive active on principle and the paragraph now zigzags.
- **When it does not apply:** Read with V6: the default remains active, and the passive stays a minority tool (V6 11:47). He doesn't restate that limit in V9. (inference) The passive is justified here by its links. A passive that links to nothing is still the "flabby" passive of V6.
- **Example:** Surrounding sentences: "The checklist is short. It fits on a single card. It asks five questions. [?] Night nurses who use it report fewer handoff errors." Active: "The night nurses designed it in 2019." Passive: "It was designed in 2019 by the night nurses." The passive keeps "it" (the checklist) as topic and ends on "night nurses", which the next sentence picks up.
- **Timestamp:** V9 6:01 to 9:17.

#### 3.3 Write for how memory works: concrete, sensory, human
- **What it is:** Give readers more than information. Give them images, human actions and sensory detail. (V9 12:44)
- **Why it works:** He shows it rather than tells it. He asks viewers to recall a series of things: a capital city, a song, a painting, their childhood home, the definition of an abstract word, a fruit. Each recall feels different: quick fact recall, a melody replaying, a picture, a walk through rooms with smells and sounds, effortful reasoning, sensory memory followed by a gear change into words. Memory is a range of mental activities, which is why faces are easier to remember than names. We remember images, human actions and sensations, not words. The visual part of the brain is far older than the part that evolved for language. (V9 9:17 to 12:44)
- **How to apply** (his three steps, V9 12:44 to 13:32):
  1. Choose concrete language and examples. Don't just name a place. Give the sights, sounds, smells and feelings a reader can attach to it.
  2. Write about flesh-and-blood characters.
  3. If you must write about an abstraction, turn it into a visual character by making it the subject of verbs that tell a story.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** generic nouns (*factors, issues, impacts, stakeholders*); statements of place or event with no sensory detail; no people anywhere in a passage about human affairs; abstractions as the objects of "is" rather than the subjects of action verbs.
- **When it does not apply:** He doesn't say. (inference) Concreteness serves memory and attention. It doesn't license padding every point with description.
- **Example:**
  - Before: "The factory closure had significant negative effects on the local community." After: "When the mill shut, the diner across the road cut its breakfast shift, and the Little League lost its sponsor."
  - Abstraction as a character. Before: "There was a decline in trust in the institution." After: "Trust drained out of the institution one broken promise at a time."
- **Timestamp:** V9 9:17 to 13:32.

#### 3.4 Replace metadiscourse with conversational moves
- **What it is:** **Metadiscourse** is writing about the writing: statements that give the reader "GPS coordinates" within the text, also called **signposting**. Examples are "Chapter 2 discusses...", "The preceding paragraph demonstrates...", "This section raises the question...", "The final topic to be discussed is...". He opens this section by performing the fault: a paragraph of agentless future passives describing what the section "will" do. Then he asks whether he lost you. (V9 13:32 to 14:50)
- **Why it works:** Metadiscourse is a leftover of the old rhetorical rule "tell them what you're going to tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them". The rule made sense when speeches dominated and speakers had to stop listeners' minds from wandering, and people still signpost in conversation. In writing it is unnecessary, and readers find it boring. A clearly laid-out path doesn't need the driver to pull over every 15 minutes [his metaphor]. (V9 14:50 to 15:37)
- **How to apply** (his fixes, V9 15:37 to 16:45):
  1. **Signpost sparingly, as in conversation.** Introduce a topic with the question it answers instead of announcing it.
  2. **Use visual language.** Treat the content as something happening in the world, not on the page ("As we have seen..."). Seeing implies someone who sees, so the characters become the writer and reader watching together, not the units of the text.
  3. **Use the language of moving together.** Writer and reader can travel and cooperate: "we arrive at...", "let's finish with...", "now that we have seen...".
  4. **Then reorganize what is left.** Deleting metadiscourse isn't enough; the remaining text still needs organizing. He points to the next video for that. (V9 16:45)
- **Symptoms in a draft:** document units as grammatical subjects ("This chapter examines", "The previous section showed", "The following table illustrates"); agentless passives about the text's own moves ("will be discussed", "will be provided"); frequent previews and recaps; "firstly... secondly..." scaffolding.
- **When it does not apply:** He says signpost sparingly, not never. He allows it in conversation and speech, where its origin made sense (V9 14:50, 15:37). (observation) His own video scripts open with spoken signposting ("in today's video I'm going to..."), which fits his exception for oral media.
- **Example:**
  - Before: "Section 3 examines the causes of staff turnover. The previous section demonstrated that pay is not the main factor. The final issue to be addressed is retention policy."
  - After: "Why do staff leave? As we saw, pay is not the main reason. ... Let's finish with what managers can change."
- **Timestamp:** V9 13:32 to 16:45.
- **My note:** "Seeing implies seers", with writer and reader as joint observers, is the core of "classic style" (Thomas and Turner, popularized by Pinker's *The Sense of Style*). He doesn't name it here. The earlier summary links it to later videos (V22, V30, V44).

### 4. His stances
- **Against:**
  - blaming readers or technology for poor attention; the writer is responsible (V9 0:00);
  - surface-level tricks, memorized scripts and AI no-code tools, as opposed to understanding the reader's "architecture" (V9 1:08);
  - "rule-based writing teachers", including Strunk and White, on the passive (V9 6:01, 7:29, 8:34);
  - the teacher's rule to avoid repetition, which he says only looks at the sentence level (V9 5:35);
  - metadiscourse and the old "tell them..." formula in writing (V9 14:50).
- **Frame:** writing tools over writing rules (V9 6:01). The payoff is prose that "flows like water".
- **Warnings:** sentence-level grammar advice isn't enough (V9 4:24); deleting metadiscourse isn't enough (V9 16:45).
- **Changed his mind:** only a small aside. He used to think he was just bad with names; the way memory works explains it (V9 9:17). For the larger shift on the passive voice across V6 to V9, see "Across".

### 5. People and books named
- Steven Pinker, *The Language Instinct*: the source of the adapted passage (V9 1:55).
- Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, the leading researcher on flow: the source of the two versions of the happiness sentence and the surrounding passage (V9 6:41). He doesn't name the book; the passage appears to be from *Flow*.
- Strunk and White, as the rule-based foil (V9 7:29).
- The "tell them..." formula, attributed to old rhetoric in use since the Roman Empire, with no author (V9 13:32, 14:50).
- The memory demonstration is his own and cites no study.

## V10: How to Write a Great Sentence | Writing Tips from an English Professor (2025-01-23)

### 1. Who it is for and the problem it addresses
The video is for writers in any style (academic, creative, social, professional) who either worry about rules like comma versus semicolon, or put off writing until they feel inspired. The problem: fixing on grammar holds you back from what counts, sentences that reach the reader's mind and heart. And a writer who waits for inspiration has no daily practice. (V10 0:00, 7:19)

### 2. Core argument
The writer's raw material is the sentence, not the word, because meaning lives in the relationships between words. Grammar and vocabulary are necessary ingredients, but no recipe produces a great sentence. A great sentence is a successful one: it achieves its intended effect in context and meets the reader's expectations. Readers read to understand the world, and a great sentence opens a window onto it. So when writing, ask what the sentence is for and what the reader should see. Writers do have "scales" to practise, however: collecting striking sentences, Great Sentences Mad Libs (practising form without content), and DeLillo's habit of giving each paragraph its own page. Practice is not performance, though. Once your skills are in place, you move on to meaningful content. (V10 1:57, 4:40, 5:18, 6:04, 6:56, 10:46)

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 The sentence-purpose question
- **What it is:** Instead of worrying about grammar while writing, ask what you are trying to do with this sentence and what you want the reader to see. (V10 6:04)
- **Why it works:** Readers don't read to admire grammar. They read to understand the world better. Rule-based advice is like a detailed set of flat-pack assembly instructions for building the window frame [his metaphor], when the point is the view. (V10 6:04)
- **How to apply:**
  1. For a sentence that feels wrong, stop looking at its grammar.
  2. Say in one line what the sentence should do (define? show evidence? turn the argument?) and what the reader should picture.
  3. Rewrite toward that.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** sentences that are correct but empty; "It is important to note that..."; the writer fussing over punctuation choices in a sentence whose purpose is unclear.
- **When it does not apply:** He doesn't say. (inference) Mechanics still matter at the final proofread. His claim is about what makes a sentence great, not a licence for errors.
- **Example:** Before: "It is important to note that there are a number of considerations regarding the timeline that need to be taken into account." Question: what should the reader see? Two specific risks. After: "Two things could delay us: the permit and the steel supplier."
- **Timestamp:** V10 5:18 to 6:56.

#### 3.2 Judge a sentence by its effect in context ("great sentences are successful sentences")
- **What it is:** A sentence is great when it achieves its intended effect and meets the reader's expectations, not when it obeys rules. Even a "bad" sentence can be great in the right context. (V10 4:40 to 5:18)
- **Why it works:** His first analogy: ask someone about their best meal and they describe the time, place, company and occasion, not the ingredients or cooking technique. He tells a story about a dinner in Bergen. Ingredients are necessary, but they aren't what made the meal exceptional (V10 2:27 to 4:40). His second: a deliberately terrible cover of a famous Beatles song, played on a radio show about annoying music, breaks every rule of composition yet works, because its clash with what listeners expect is the joke. The effect comes from how it plays against the context (V10 4:40 to 5:18). He had to cut the clip because of a copyright strike.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Name the effect the sentence should have on this reader at this point.
  2. Judge the sentence by whether it has that effect, not by a checklist.
  3. If breaking a convention produces the effect, the break is justified.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** revisions made to satisfy a rule that make the passage worse; feedback such as "never start with 'and'" applied without asking what the sentence was for.
- **When it does not apply:** (inference) Readers' expectations include genre conventions. His definition includes "match the reader's expectations", so a rule-break that confuses the intended reader fails his own test.
- **Example:** A three-word fragment after a long, careful paragraph ("It never came.") breaks a school rule and lands exactly as intended.
- **Timestamp:** V10 2:27 to 6:04.

#### 3.3 Sentence bird-watching (his metaphor)
- **What it is:** Keep a running list of sentences that stand out in your reading, especially ones with new or startling patterns, and get curious about them. Keep sentences, not words. (V10 2:27)
- **Why it works:** Words stacked together make no sense on their own. He shows this by scrambling a sentence into word salad, then restating it. Meaning comes from the relationships between words (V10 1:57). If you love words, read the dictionary. To write, get obsessed with sentences (V10 2:27). The painter who said he just likes the smell of paint teaches the same lesson: people become artists by loving the raw material, and for writers that is the sentence (V10 1:26 to 1:57).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Keep a sentence notebook while you read.
  2. Copy in any sentence whose shape surprises you.
  3. (inference) Beside it, note what the pattern is: how it is built, where the weight falls.
  4. Use entries as models for Mad Libs (3.4).
- **Symptoms it addresses:** word lists or vocabulary notebooks with no sense of structure; a narrow range of sentence shapes.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Example entry** (my sentence): "The town had two bars, one church, and no secrets." Pattern: a list of concrete counts that turns on an abstract noun at the end.
- **Timestamp:** V10 1:57 to 2:27.

#### 3.4 Great Sentences Mad Libs (his name)
- **What it is:** Take a model sentence, keep its structure, and swap words slot by slot, like the children's game Mad Libs. It practises form, not content. (V10 8:09 to 10:08)
- **Why it works:** Writers do have scales to practise (V10 6:56 to 7:19). Chomsky's famous sentence about sleeping green ideas is grammatical nonsense, a "vase without flowers" [his metaphor]. The same words in reverse order are "flowers with no vase": each word means something, but nothing holds them together, and you learn nothing from writing them. Practising the vase, the structure, is what builds skill (V10 7:19 to 8:33). You don't need to know the grammar rules, because you know them intuitively: some swaps obviously fit a slot and others obviously don't (V10 9:17 to 10:08). And unlike musical scales, the exercise produces new sentences (V10 10:08).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Pick a model sentence. He demonstrates with the opening of Lewis Carroll's "Jabberwocky" and offers ten models in a practice sheet.
  2. Go slot by slot and list replacements that fit (for an adjective and noun, many options) and notice what doesn't fit.
  3. Write several complete new sentences in the same shape.
  4. Do it regularly, like practising scales.
- **Symptoms it addresses:** writer's block from waiting for inspiration; a limited stock of sentence shapes; knowing a sentence is good without being able to reproduce its effect.
- **When it does not apply:** It is practice, not performance. Nobody buys tickets to hear scales, so once your skills are in place, move on to meaningful content (V10 10:46). He also explicitly separates it from content: it is form practice.
- **Example** (my model sentence): "The river kept its own calendar, flooding whenever the town forgot it."
  - Slots: [The N] [kept its own N], [V-ing] whenever [the N] [V-ed] it.
  - New sentences: "The market kept its own clock, crashing whenever the analysts ignored it." "The garden kept its own ledger, failing whenever the owner neglected it."
  - A swap that breaks the slot: "The market kept its own quickly."
- **Timestamp:** V10 6:56 to 10:08.

#### 3.5 One paragraph per page (DeLillo's method)
- **What it is:** Don DeLillo told *The Paris Review* that after finishing any paragraph, even three lines, he started a fresh page for the next one. No crowded pages. (V10 10:08 to 10:46)
- **Why it works:** He could see each set of sentences more clearly. DeLillo calls himself someone who constructs sentences, driven by a rhythm he hears, with typed words that feel sculptural (V10 0:00 to 0:44). William presents the method as the natural result of working at the sentence level (V10 10:08).
- **How to apply:**
  1. While drafting or revising, give each paragraph its own page or screen, with whitespace around it.
  2. Read that paragraph's sentences in isolation and adjust their rhythm and shape.
  3. (inference) Reassemble for the final layout.
- **Symptoms it addresses:** (inference) dense pages where individual sentences blur; revision that skims.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated. (inference) It is a drafting and revision method, not a layout for the final document.
- **Example:** (inference) An academic revising a discussion section pastes each paragraph onto its own page and finds that three paragraphs in a row open with "However".
- **Timestamp:** V10 0:00 to 0:44 and 10:08 to 10:46.

### 4. His stances
- **Against:**
  - worrying about comma versus semicolon; he says it doesn't matter (V10 0:00);
  - focusing on grammar, which he says wrecks sentences and is "completely beside the point" for greatness (V10 0:00, 2:27, 6:04);
  - collecting words instead of sentences (V10 2:27);
  - rule-based writing advice, his flat-pack window-frame image (V10 6:04);

- **Reassurance:** it isn't a sneaky way of getting you interested in grammar (V10 2:27). You already know the rules intuitively (V10 10:08).
- **Changed his mind:** he used to think musicians had it easier because they can practise scales while writers face a blank page. He now thinks writers have scales too (V10 6:56 to 7:19).

### 5. People and books named

## Across these five videos

### One through-line: the reader's mind, not the rulebook
Each lesson video justifies its advice by how readers process text, and sets itself against rule-following:
- V6: good writing is care, meaning careful attention to the reader's psychology (V6 5:27). Stop following useless rules and make active decisions (V6 12:04).
- V8: focus obsessively on the reader, not on grammar rules (V8 11:53).
- V9: study the reader's "architecture"; tools, not rules (V9 1:08, 6:01).
- V10: grammar is beside the point; ask what the reader should see (V10 6:04).

The anti-rule stance grows stronger over the period. In V6 it is a caveat at the end. In V9 and V10 it is the frame for the whole video. The coding analogy (V9) and the ingredients analogy (V10) are two versions of one claim: surface correctness is necessary but it isn't what makes writing work.

### A small model of how readers read, assembled across V6, V8 and V9
1. **Readers read left to right and build expectations from position.** V6 says readers expect the doer in the subject and must wait for it in a passive. V8 says readers hold their breath until the verb. V9 says whatever comes first is taken as the topic. These are one claim, that position creates expectation, applied to three slots.
2. **Readers picture things.** V6 says doer plus action engages the visual brain. V9 says clear writing is visual, topics should be concrete, and memory runs on images, actions and senses. V10 asks what the reader should see and calls great sentences a window. Even the metadiscourse fix in V9 works through sight: seeing implies seers.
3. **Readers read at two resolutions.** V8 introduces passage-level flow (old-to-new). V9 turns it into the "two skis": sentence topic and passage topic. Sentence-level fixes must be checked against the passage.

The three sentence principles, **characters as subjects and actions as verbs** (V6, V8 steps 1 and 2, V9 hack 3), **short subject and early verb** (V8), and **old-to-new** (V8, V9), are all ways of serving points 1 to 3.

### The passive voice: a shift in emphasis from V6 to V9
- **V6 (Aug 2024):** make active the default, because the passive delays the agent and hides the action behind "be". The passive has a function, but perhaps 5% of sentences need it. The judgement is made at the level of the single sentence: can the reader picture it?
- **V8 (Sep 2024):** his own fixes quietly use the passive twice: once when the agent is unknown (V8 6:33), and once to put the old information first for flow (V8 11:15). He makes no comment on it.
- **V9 (Jan 2025):** he openly champions the passive against Strunk and White when it keeps a passage's topic and hands the right character to the next sentence (V9 7:29 to 9:17). The judgement is now made at the level of the passage. His "two skis" hack explains why: sentence-level advice isn't enough.

This is not a reversal. V6 already rejected a ban. But the reason for using the passive moves from "a rare exception" to "a main tool for controlling what comes first and last". For a skill: default to active, but never convert a passive without checking its neighbours. If the passive keeps the topic string or sets up the next sentence, it stays.

### Curiosity and conversation connect the title advice to the signposting advice
V6's first curiosity trigger (pose a question) returns in V9's first metadiscourse fix: introduce a topic with the question it answers. Both treat the reader as someone who wants a gap closed, not someone who needs a map. V6 works at the scale of the title, V9 at the scale of a section.

### Two scales of simplicity
V6's simplicity is subtraction: prune words. V9 adds that subtraction isn't enough. After you delete metadiscourse you must reorganize what remains (V9 16:45). V10 isn't about cutting at all. It is about command of form. (inference) A skill should treat pruning as one pass among several, not as "clarity" itself. The earlier summary puts compression late in the process ("concision applied earlier is harmful", attributed to V23 and V29). V6 doesn't discuss timing, so the two don't conflict. But V6 alone would suggest pruning is the main event, and the later videos don't support that.

### Learning by absorbing models (V7 and V10)
V7 describes heavy-duty imitation: typing out a whole novel, translating, reading classics ("seeds, not twigs"). V10 turns it into light daily drills: collect sentences, rework their structure with Mad Libs, isolate paragraphs on their own pages. Both rest on one belief: skill comes from absorbing forms from strong models, and the unit to study is the sentence and its structure, not the word. V10's painter anecdote (love the raw material) echoes V7's *The Gift* (writing as something other than a commodity).

### Tensions worth flagging for a skill
1. **Hacks versus the anti-commodity stance.** V7 criticizes productivity-driven, hustle-culture writing advice. V9 is packaged as "hacks" and "brain-optimized writing". The substance of V9 is reader psychology, not tricks, and V9 itself rejects "surface-level tricks", so the conflict is mostly one of packaging.
2. **Clarity versus unsummarizability.** V7 approvingly reports Taleb's view that a book which can be summarized won't last.
