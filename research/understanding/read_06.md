## V26: How to write so well that readers stop scrolling (2025-10-26)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it addresses
It is for nonfiction writers who want readers to move through an essay easily and remember it. He names three problems (0:00 to 0:38):
- Transitions that lose the reader.
- Visual writing that swamps the reader with detail.
- Prose that needs "boring signposts" to keep moving.

He puts all three down to one cause: the writer does not know what the reader's brain needs at each point in the reading.

### 2. Core argument
He opens with the old aphorism that easy writing makes hard reading. The writer has to do the work so the reader doesn't have to.

Reading happens in a line, one word at a time, left to right. The reader decodes symbols and builds meaning as they go, and they have to hold that meaning while they decode the next words (1:32, 3:37). Short-term memory holds only about three or four items, not the seven or so once believed (3:37).

To cope, the reader **chunks**. After each sentence they pack only the essentials into a **"mental briefcase" (his term)** and carry it into the next sentence (4:16). Four things go into the briefcase:
1. The last words of the sentence.
2. The sentence's topic.
3. Sensory impressions.
4. Expectations.

If you control those four, reading feels effortless. He sums this up as four "hacks" (13:21):
- Put emphasis-worthy material at the end of the sentence (the stress position).
- Put main characters at the start (the topic position).
- Ground abstract ideas in concrete images.
- Manage expectations.

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.0 The reading model: linear reading, three to four items, the mental briefcase (his term). This is a lens, not a rule.
- **What it is:** A way of looking at a draft through the reader's working memory. At every word, ask what the reader is holding and what they are waiting for.
- **Why it works:** Readers can't see a whole sentence at once. Meaning stays unresolved until the pieces click, and unresolved pieces take up memory slots. Readers hold three or four chunks, then compress and move on (3:37 to 4:55).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Read a hard sentence slowly, left to right.
  2. At each word, count the open items the reader must keep in mind: unattached details, a subject still waiting for its verb, an undefined term.
  3. Wherever the count goes above three or four before the sentence resolves, restructure. Resolve earlier, split the sentence, or move material after the verb.
  4. At the end of each sentence, ask what goes into the briefcase. It should be the end words, the topic, a concrete image, and any open expectation.
- **Symptoms:** A reader has to reread to understand. Details float without a place to attach. The main verb comes late.
- **When not to use it:** He gives no limit. (Inferred) It is a diagnostic lens, not a style to impose. He shows that "costly" orders can be used on purpose for effect (3.1).
- **Example:** A sentence opens with a department, then a date range, then a policy name, then a caveat clause, and only then reaches the verb. By the verb, the reader is holding five things. Rewrite so the subject and verb come in the first six words and the qualifications follow.
- **Timestamp:** V26 1:32 to 4:55.

#### 3.1 The sentence as a camera shot (his cinematographer analogy)
- **What it is:** The order in which you give particulars and context decides what the reader "sees" first and where they end up. There are two orders:
  - **Particulars first, context last.** The scene assembles out of floating pieces, and the reader learns where they stand only at the end. He likens this to a tilt-down, zoom-out shot.
  - **Context first, then the focus.** The reader moves from a wide shot to a close-up.
- **Why it works:** The image forms left to right. Until context arrives, the image is vague and static (1:50 to 2:51). Both effects exist only because reading is linear.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Decide what the reader should see first and what they should be left looking at.
  2. For orientation, which is most expository prose, lead with context and end on the focus.
  3. For a reveal or surprise, lead with the striking particular and let the context land last.
  4. Read the sentence slowly and notice the word at which the picture snaps into place. Is that the moment you wanted?
- **Symptoms:** You can't picture the scene until the last clause. Details are "disembodied" (his image for crumbs hanging in the air like the Cheshire Cat's smile). Or the focus is buried mid-sentence when it should land at the end.
- **When not to use it:** He says plainly that neither order is better. The point is that you can choose (2:51). (Inferred) The reveal order costs working memory, so use it sparingly in dense exposition.
- **Example:**
  - Reveal: "Two unsigned consent forms turned up in the box of files the auditor carried up from the basement."
  - Establishing: "In the box of files the auditor carried up from the basement, she found two unsigned consent forms."
- **Timestamp:** V26 1:50 to 3:37.

#### 3.2 The stress-position handoff (his terms: "relay race effect", sentences "shaking hands"; he names the slot the "stress position" at 13:21)
- **What it is:** The last words of a sentence are still in the reader's mind when the next sentence starts. Begin the next sentence on the same note, so the baton passes from the end of one sentence to the start of the next.
- **Why it works:** The end of a sentence lingers. He likens it to the ripple left after you draw your hand through water (4:55). It is the freshest thing in the briefcase, so new information attached to it needs no search.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Read the last three to five words of sentence A.
  2. Open sentence B with those words, a pronoun for them, a synonym, or a summary phrase. His example turns two named people into "the two women".
  3. Put in the stress position whatever the next sentence needs to pick up, plus whatever deserves emphasis (13:21).
- **Symptoms:** Each sentence opens on something unconnected. The reader has to reach back two sentences to find the link. Transitions feel "jarring".
- **When not to use it:** Nothing stated here. In V27 (15:42 to 17:46) he shows that handoffs alone can produce a smooth but pointless chain, so this is necessary but not sufficient.
- **Example:**
  - Before: "In March the clinic began texting patients the day before appointments. The manager says missed visits had been costing thousands a month."
  - After: "In March the clinic began texting patients a reminder the day before each appointment. Those reminders cut missed visits by a third within two months."
- **Timestamp:** V26 4:55 to 5:48, 13:21.

#### 3.3 A consistent topic (his terms: "topic", "topic position")
- **What it is:** The topic is who or what the sentence tells a story about. Readers assume it is whatever comes first. Keep the same topic across a run of sentences, and change it only for a reason.
- **Why it works:** The reader's mind works like a camera following a main character (6:30). Readers chunk information more easily when a paragraph or section stays on one topic (8:40).

  Changing topics without reason is like a guest who wanders into a conversation about a friend's new business and remarks on the weather. The remark is grammatical, but it doesn't follow the conversation (6:30 to 7:29). The opening of a sentence sets up an expectation that the same star will appear in the next one (7:55).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Name the main character of the paragraph or passage.
  2. Start most sentences with that character, or a pronoun or short synonym for it (8:40).
  3. Whenever a sentence starts with something else, ask whether you meant to change the subject.
  4. Never vary sentence openings just for variety (9:19).
- **Symptoms:** Sentence openings in a paragraph are all different nouns. One sentence follows a person and the next opens on an unrelated object. The writer has been following advice to "vary your sentences".
- **When not to use it:** Repetition does not mean the same word; pronouns and synonyms count (8:40). He objects only to change "without real reason", which implies that a deliberate switch is fine. See V27 for how deliberate topic changes still need a unifying set.
- **Example:**
  - Before: "The new hire logged in at nine. The onboarding portal crashed twice. HR's help line was busy all morning."
  - After: "The new hire logged in at nine. She watched the onboarding portal crash twice, then spent the morning on hold with HR."
- **Timestamp:** V26 5:48 to 9:19.

#### 3.4 Concrete sensory language (his term: "sensory impressions", the third briefcase item)
- **What it is:** Write with words for things a reader can perceive and for concrete actions. Avoid abstract nouns that stand in for actions.
- **Why it works:**
  - Sensory memory packs a whole experience into a single "mental file". His example is a grandmother's kitchen: smell, warmth and a creaking floorboard all come back together (9:19 to 10:02). Concrete words are therefore cheap to carry.
  - Concrete words also make the reader supply the surrounding context for free. A delivered package brings a hallway, a building and the courier's hands with it. An abstract noun such as "delivery" brings none of that (10:24 to 11:32).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find nouns that name actions ("delivery", "occurrence", "implementation") and the empty verbs that carry them ("occurred", "took place").
  2. Rebuild the sentence around the physical thing and what happens to it.
  3. Picture the sentence. If no image forms, it is still abstract.
- **Symptoms:**
  - "X of Y occurred" or "took place".
  - Sentences you can't picture.
  - An abstract noun as the subject.
- **When not to use it:** No limit is stated. Note that his concrete version uses the passive voice. The fix is concreteness, not active voice.
- **Example:**
  - Before: "Reduction of waiting-room congestion was achieved through staggered scheduling."
  - After: "Once appointments were staggered, patients no longer stood along the waiting-room walls."
- **Timestamp:** V26 9:19 to 11:54.

#### 3.5 Expectations and payoffs (his term: "expectations", chosen deliberately over "cliffhangers")
- **What it is:** A word or claim that promises something still to come. The reader carries the open question forward and reads on to find the payoff. It works at sentence scale, not only between episodes of a series.
- **Why it works:** The reading brain is always in pursuit. It uses what it has read to anticipate what comes next. All good writing runs on this setup-and-payoff engine (12:53).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Plant a promise that raises one specific question: an unexplained result, a surprising finding, a named but unrevealed item.
  2. Pay it off at a deliberate point, usually soon.
  3. In revision, map where each expectation is set and where it is paid off. Look for promises that are never paid and payoffs that were never set up.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Flat prose in which nothing is pending.
  - Promises left unpaid.
  - (Inferred) Heavy signposting ("next, I discuss...") doing the work an open question should do.
- **When not to use it:** He calls his own trigger word ("shocking") a little clichéd. The device is the specific open question, not hype words. V28 later rejects hooks that promise without offering value.
- **Example:** "The pilot program did save money, just not in the budget line anyone was watching." The next sentence pays it off: "The savings showed up in overtime, which fell by a third."
- **Timestamp:** V26 11:54 to 13:21.

### 4. Stances and warnings
- **Against "vary your sentences / don't repeat yourself".** He says this advice, common on writing YouTube and forums, could not be further from the truth. Repetition, especially in the topic position, helps readers track what is being developed (5:48 to 6:30, 9:19).
- **The science changed.** Working memory is now thought to hold three to four items, not seven or eight. He presents this as a change in the field, not in his own view (3:37).
- **Flow should come from memory management, not signposts.** He promises flow "without littering your text" with signposts (0:38).
- **Word-order effects are choices, not rules** (2:51).
- **Memory is only one part of reader psychology.** He promises more hacks in a follow-up video (13:21).

### 5. People and books named as sources
- He names no source for these ideas.
- The Cheshire Cat is only an image.
- (My inference, not stated) "Topic position" and "stress position" are the terms of Gopen and Swan's *The Science of Scientific Writing*, and the same ideas appear in Joseph M. Williams's *Style*. The three-to-four-item capacity reflects working-memory research that revised the older "seven plus or minus two" figure.

## V27: 9 Techniques for Clearer Writing (I Wish I Knew Sooner) (2026-01-04)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it addresses
It is for academic and professional writers who want clearer prose. He frames the problem around AI: people finish a draft, ask AI to clean it up, and get back text that doesn't sound like them. Readers notice, and they lose trust (0:00).

His answer is to learn the principles and clarify your own text. He gives nine techniques in three groups: sentence level, paragraph level and idea level (0:00 to 0:41).

### 2. Core argument
Clarity is something readers judge, and it has a structure at every scale.
- **Sentences.** Every sentence, even in academic writing, is a small story of characters doing actions. Clear sentences put the characters in the subjects and the actions in the verbs. They start simply and save complexity and new information for after the verb.
- **Paragraphs.** Clarity has two parts:
  - Cohesion: each sentence hands off to the next.
  - Coherence: the sentences add up to one picture. Consistent topics at sentence openings signal it.
- **Ideas.** Clear sentences are worth nothing if readers don't see why the text deserves their attention. The introduction must set up a problem they care about, and the reader needs a conceptual map before the detail.
- **One principle ties it together.** Every unit, from sentence to whole document, opens with a short **index** that states the point and key terms, then a longer **discussion**.

### 3. Techniques
The numbering below is mine; he groups the nine by level.

#### 3.1 Characters in the subject position (his framing: "storytelling at the level of the sentence"; the subject position is "center stage")
- **What it is:** Identify the story's characters: people, organizations, or things that act. Make them the grammatical subjects.
- **Why it works:** Every story is someone doing something (2:04). The subject position is where readers put their attention, like center stage in a musical or the middle of a camera frame. If characters are buried in phrases like "on the part of X" or "of X", the spotlight falls on abstractions instead (2:04 to 3:08).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Ask who this sentence is about.
  2. Find where that character sits grammatically.
  3. Move it to the subject.
  4. Check that the subject can actually perform the verb.
- **Symptoms:**
  - The character appears in "on the part of X", "by X", or "X's [noun]".
  - Abstract nouns sit in most subject slots.
- **When not to use it:** He separates this from big-picture storytelling (hero's journey, story circle, "but/therefore"). For clarity work, set those patterns aside and look at the sentence (0:41 to 1:35). Abstract concepts can serve as characters when readers know them well (see 3.3, use 4).
- **Example:**
  - Before: "An assessment of the pilot was conducted by the regional office, with expansion recommended as the conclusion."
  - After: "The regional office assessed the pilot and recommended expanding it."
- **Timestamp:** V27 0:41 to 3:08.

#### 3.2 Actions in verbs (his framing: "construal", how you construe an action)
- **What it is:** Express the story's key actions as verbs, not nouns.
- **Why it works:**
  - The same scene can be painted realistically or broken into abstract planes, and how you construe an action changes what the reader experiences (3:08 to 3:48).
  - When actions become nouns, you need vague, flabby verbs to carry them ("undertaken", "took place", "resulted in"), and those verbs carry no action (4:16).
  - Readers call a sentence clear when they find the someone in the subject and the something being done in the verb (4:54).
- **How to apply:**
  1. List the sentence's key actions.
  2. For each one, check whether it is a verb or a noun.
  3. Turn noun-actions back into verbs and attach them to the character subject.
  4. Delete the empty verbs that were carrying the nouns.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Empty verbs: occur, take place, undertake, conduct, result in, perform.
  - Clusters of -tion/-ment/-ance nouns.
  - Constructions like "there is a need for".
- **When not to use it:** See 3.3. He adds that writing is not paint-by-numbers; it takes decisions about when to use a tool and when to avoid it (4:54).
- **Example:**
  - Before: "Implementation of the policy took place in June, and a decline in complaints was the result."
  - After: "The city implemented the policy in June, and complaints declined."
- **Timestamp:** V27 3:08 to 5:34.

#### 3.3 Nominalizations: which to cut, and four good uses (his term: "nominalization", also called "zombie nouns")
- **What it is:** A nominalization turns a verb or adjective into a noun: investigate becomes investigation, brave becomes bravery. He calls them the main cause of unclear writing, but not always wrong.
- **Why it works:** They make prose abstract and indirect. But languages are organisms that keep only organs that serve a purpose, so nominalizations must be useful for something (6:11).

  **His four legitimate uses:**
  1. **As a subject that links back.** A nominalization can sum up the whole previous clause and carry it forward, which builds cohesion (6:33).
  2. **To replace "the fact that" or "the idea that".** Those phrases are dead weight in writing. He adds that rewriting with a character subject and an action verb often works just as well (7:05 to 7:44).
  3. **As an object.** A nominalization can turn a clause into a solid object for a verb, as in "approved his proposal". The key rule: **nominalizations work as objects, not as subjects.** In the subject slot they pretend to be characters doing things they can't do. Proposals don't arrive; people do (8:31 to 9:21).
  4. **For concepts so familiar to these readers that they act as characters.** This depends on the field (9:21).
- **How to apply (his triage):**
  1. Circle each nominalization.
  2. Does it hide an important action? Make it a verb. Then ask who does it, and what they do it to.
  3. Is it so familiar to these readers that it works as a character? Keep it.
  4. Does it link back, replace "the fact that", or serve as an object? Keep it.
  5. Is it a subject pretending to act? Rewrite with a real character.

  His worked case is a sentence with three nominalizations. Two hide actions and become verbs; the third is a familiar field term and stays (10:34 to 11:03).
- **Symptoms:**
  - "There is a need for an X of the Y."
  - A nominalization as subject with a verb it can't perform.
  - Chains of "of" phrases.
- **When not to use it:** Leave a nominalization alone when it is one of the four uses above. The skill is telling hidden actions apart from familiar concepts (11:03).
- **Examples:**
  - Use 1: "The board voted to sell the warehouse. That sale paid for the new lab."
  - Use 2: "The fact that the survey closed early skewed the results" becomes "Because we closed the survey early, the results skewed."
  - Use 3: "The committee accepted what she recommended" becomes "The committee accepted her recommendation." But "Her recommendation reached the committee after the vote" becomes "She sent her recommendation after the committee had voted."
  - Use 4: for software engineers, "Deployment failed at the last step" is fine as it stands.
  - Triage: "There was a requirement for a review of the vendor contracts" becomes "We had to review the vendor contracts". "Contracts" stays.
- **Timestamp:** V27 5:34 to 11:43.

#### 3.4 The Busan taxi driver effect and the end-to-start handoff (his term: "the Busan taxi driver effect")
- **What it is:** The symptom is prose where every sentence is fine alone, but together they lurch start-stop-start. He compares it to Busan taxi drivers pumping the accelerator: the driver doesn't notice, but the passenger in the back feels sick.

  The fix is to end each sentence with information the next one can pick up and continue. Choose active or passive voice to make that happen.
- **Why it works:** When the start of the next sentence repeats what the reader just heard, the two sentences join. When it opens on something new, the information seems to "come out of nowhere" (13:02 to 13:56). The passive voice lets you put the thing acted upon first. That lines the end of one sentence up with the start of the next, and also lets the new sentence end on something the following one can use (13:56 to 14:45).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Read sentences in pairs.
  2. Check whether the start of B picks up the end of A.
  3. If not, reorder B. Often switching to passive, or the reverse, brings the shared element to the front.
  4. Check that B's end sets up C.
- **Symptoms:**
  - The prose feels choppy, disorganized, doesn't flow.
  - Sentences open with brand-new information.
  - Each sentence reads well alone.
- **When not to use it:** He rejects blanket "avoid the passive" advice here (13:56). Handoffs alone don't create coherence (see 3.5).
- **Example:**
  - A: "Our survey found that most complaints came from night-shift nurses."
  - B, choppy: "Hospital administrators rarely schedule meetings after 6 p.m."
  - B, handoff (passive): "Those nurses are rarely reached by the daytime meetings where schedules get set." A following sentence C can then open on "Those meetings...".
- **Timestamp:** V27 11:43 to 15:07.

#### 3.5 Cohesion versus coherence, and the underline diagnostic (his terms: "cohesion" and "coherence"; jigsaw-puzzle analogy)
- **What it is:**
  - **Cohesion** is the pieces fitting together.
  - **Coherence** is the pieces forming the picture on the box.

  A passage can hand off perfectly from sentence to sentence and still be incoherent. His example drifts from a city, to coffee, to machines, to a typewriter, to a professor (15:42 to 16:34). He names three causes of incoherence:
  1. The sentence subjects are unrelated. From the subjects alone, you couldn't predict what the passage is about.
  2. The sentences share no theme, meaning no web of related words from one "ecosystem".
  3. No thesis unifies the passage (16:34).
- **Why it works:** You can't judge your own flow by rereading, because you know the text too well and fill in what's missing. You need a mechanical diagnostic (17:46 to 18:19). Consistent topics at sentence openings tell readers what the passage is about as a whole. Random topic shifts lose context and feel disorganized (20:30 to 21:11).
- **How to apply (his three steps):**
  1. Underline the first seven or eight words of every sentence, stopping at the verb.
  2. Ask whether the underlined words form a small set of related topics. Would your *readers* see how they're related, not just you? Do they name the most important characters, real or abstract?
  3. If not, rewrite. In most sentences, make the subject name the topic and put it near the start (20:05).

  Also run the theme check: do the key content words belong to one family?
- **Symptoms:**
  - Subjects hop from one unrelated noun to the next.
  - You can't guess the topic from the subjects.
  - The vocabulary comes from different "ecosystems".
  - There is no stated point.
- **When not to use it:** He says "in most of your sentences", not all (20:05).
- **Example:**
  - Before: "A rise in remote work has reshaped downtown economies. Lunch spots near office towers report falling sales. The average commute has shrunk. Productivity research remains divided." The underlined subjects are four unrelated things.
  - After: "Office workers who now stay home have reshaped downtown economies. They no longer buy lunch near the towers, they no longer commute, and whether they get more done at home is still disputed."
- **Timestamp:** V27 15:07 to 21:11.

#### 3.6 Complexity at the end: the runway (his plane-on-a-runway analogy)
- **What it is:** Open each sentence simply and put the complexity after the verb.
- **Why it works:** Readers build momentum in the first nine or ten words. With momentum, they can handle complexity later in the sentence. Put obstacles on the runway, meaning a long subject full of technical terms, and the sentence never gets off the ground (21:35 to 22:16). His good version reaches subject and verb in five words, and technical words come only after that.

  He insists this is how readers read, not his taste (24:06).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Use this default shape: a short introductory phrase or clause, then a short concrete subject, then a verb naming a specific action, then everything long and new.
  2. Move two kinds of complexity to the end: long phrases and clauses, and new information, especially unfamiliar technical terms (24:06).
  3. Order information from simple to complex, and from old and familiar to new (24:45).
- **Symptoms:**
  - A subject of fifteen or more words.
  - A verb that arrives late.
  - Technical terms in the opening words.
  - Endings like "...is the focus of this research."
- **When not to use it:** Length after the verb is fine. The sentence can run for several lines if it is well built (24:45). Length isn't the problem; where the complexity sits is.
- **Example:**
  - Before: "Comparative analysis of emergency-room wait times across urban and rural hospitals during the pandemic's first two years is presented here."
  - After: "Here we compare how long patients waited in emergency rooms at urban and rural hospitals during the pandemic's first two years."
- **Timestamp:** V27 21:11 to 25:12.

#### 3.7 Value comes first: the problem-first introduction (his "framework for setting up problems"; market-vendor analogy)
- **What it is:** A sequence of moves for the introduction that turns your idea into the solution to a problem the reader cares about:
  1. Status quo.
  2. Concession.
  3. Destabilizing condition.
  4. Cost or benefit.
  5. Solution, meaning your idea "X".
- **Why it works:**
  - Nonfiction isn't read for entertainment; it takes work, so readers need to see value in exchange for their attention (25:31).
  - A vendor who says only that his gadget is new and took him a lot of work sells nothing. Value is a solution to a problem (26:10 to 26:33).
  - In nonfiction, the problem matters more than the idea. If readers can't perceive a problem, they can't perceive value (27:00 to 27:24).
  - Motivated readers read more attentively and even judge the prose clearer (27:52). So clarity depends partly on motivation.
  - Readers also differ in awareness (27:00):
    - some know their problem and want a solution;
    - some know the solution and want someone who can deliver it;
    - some don't know there is a problem.

    Adapt the message to where the reader stands.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write your idea X in one line.
  2. **Status quo:** state the opposite of X as how things are, or what people now think (27:52 to 29:04).
  3. **Concession:** show you understand and respect that view. Say what it got right, or why people hold it. Don't come off the top ropes swinging (29:22).
  4. **Destabilizing condition:** use a strong turn word, *but* or *however*, to show something is wrong. This is where the reader becomes alert (30:09).
  5. **Cost or benefit:** say what bad thing happens if the problem stays, or what good comes from solving it. Pitch it to this reader: what cost they fear, what benefit they want (30:36).
  6. **Solution:** now state X. The reader receives it as a fix, not a topic.
- **Symptoms:**
  - The introduction announces a topic.
  - It sells the idea on novelty or effort.
  - The writer is "writing about" something rather than posing a problem.
  - It is written for oneself.
- **When not to use it:** The moves are a skeleton; write them with more grace than his abstract demonstration (29:04). Adjust to the reader's level of awareness (27:00).
- **Example:** Thesis: small nonprofits should apply for fewer grants.
  1. "Most small nonprofits apply for every grant they qualify for."
  2. "It's understandable: more applications look like more chances."
  3. "But each proposal costs a two-person team about forty hours."
  4. "Those are hours taken from the programs funders want to see, so scattershot applicants lose on quality."
  5. "Groups that pick a few well-matched funders raise more with less work."
- **Timestamp:** V27 25:12 to 31:36.

#### 3.8 The map before the terrain, and the four signals of coherence (his terms: the "conceptual map before the detailed terrain" and the "four signals of coherence")
- **What it is:** Give an overview before the detail. He compares it to directions: first the general heading across town, then the individual turns (31:36 to 32:42). In a document, four signals make coherence visible:
  1. **Clear section boundaries**, marked by headings built from the section's key concepts.
  2. **A short introductory segment**: a sentence or short paragraph that introduces the central idea.
  3. **A point sentence at the end of that segment**, which works as the thesis for that unit.
  4. **Repeated key concepts.** The point sentence names them toward its end, and they recur through the unit (33:44 to 37:34).
- **Why it works:**
  - Coherence is an experience readers build in their heads; it can't be underlined on the page (33:17).
  - Because of the **curse of knowledge**, your own document feels coherent to you. You already know how it fits together. Readers depend only on the signals you give them (33:17 to 33:44).
  - Headings set the frame for interpreting what follows, so they must make sense right away, not only after reading (35:23).
  - Highly motivated readers will struggle through clotted sentences, which is why bad writers still get read. But incoherence eventually defeats everyone (32:42).
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each section, list the concepts that recur in it. Build the heading from them. No riddles, no inside jokes, and no concept unrelated to the section (34:45 to 35:23).
  2. Open with a short segment that introduces the central idea.
  3. End that segment with the point sentence.
  4. Put the key concepts into the point sentence, near its end, and reuse the same terms throughout.
  5. Do this for every major unit: sections and subsections (37:34).
- **Symptoms:**
  - Clever or cryptic headings.
  - A long run-up before any point.
  - Key terms that appear only late.
  - Readers "can't follow the line of thought".
- **When not to use it:** How much this matters depends on reader motivation, but no reader survives incoherence (32:42).
- **Example:**
  - The heading "Picking the Lock" becomes "Why Password Resets Drive Support Costs".
  - The opening paragraph ends: "...and most resets trace back to three design choices: forced expiry, hidden rules and silent lockouts." Those three terms then recur in the section.
- **Timestamp:** V27 31:36 to 37:51.

#### 3.9 Index and discussion at every scale (his terms: "index" and "discussion"; a "fractal" structure)
- **What it is:** Every unit has two parts:
  - **Index:** a short, easily grasped opening that states the point and introduces the key concepts.
  - **Discussion:** a longer, more complex development that delivers on it.

  The index works like a table of contents for the unit. It promises; the discussion delivers (37:51 to 38:15). His scale ladder (42:44 to 43:21):

  | Unit | Index | Discussion |
  |---|---|---|
  | Simple sentence | Short subject and verb | Longer predicate |
  | Complex sentence | Short main clause | Longer supporting clause |
  | Paragraph | Short opening sentence(s) | Longer development |
  | Section | Short opening paragraph(s) | Longer discussion |
  | Document | Short introduction | Longer body |

  Each part of a discussion has its own index and discussion. The discussion closes by returning to the point and leading into the next one (42:07).
- **Why it works:** Readers who get a short, graspable opening that ends on the point understand what follows more easily (36:17). A buried point makes them carry details without knowing what the details are for.
- **How to apply:** At each level:
  1. State the point in its shortest form first.
  2. Name the key concepts.
  3. Develop.
  4. Close by returning to the point and moving on.

  For sentences, check that the subject-plus-verb segment already states the claim. For paragraphs, check that the first one to three sentences state the point and name its themes. His paragraph example is a study report that walks through each phase before saying what it found. The rewrite states the outcome first (39:59 to 41:10).
- **Symptoms:**
  - The point is buried at the end of a sentence or paragraph.
  - A paragraph opens with method details or raw data.
  - A sentence's main claim sits in a long nominalized subject.
- **When not to use it:** The index grows with the unit. For a section it may be several paragraphs (41:10). He gives no exception.

  Compare V50 in the system file, which allows a point-last arrangement as an advanced option. It is not mentioned here.
- **Example:**
  - Before: "Forty volunteers wore step counters for eight weeks. In weeks one to four, daily steps rose by 2,000. In weeks five to eight, they drifted back to baseline..."
  - After: "Wearing a step counter raised volunteers' activity for about a month, and then the effect faded. Forty volunteers wore counters for eight weeks..."
- **Timestamp:** V27 37:51 to 43:21.

### 4. Stances and warnings
- **Against handing clarity to AI.** The result doesn't sound like you, and readers lose trust (0:00).
- **Against big-picture storytelling frameworks as the route to clarity.** Storytelling that helps clarity happens in the sentence (0:41).
- **Tools, not bans.** Nominalizations and the passive voice both have proper uses. Writing is not paint-by-numbers (4:54, 6:11, 13:56).
- **Against "avoid the passive voice" as a blanket rule** (13:56).
- **Rereading won't tell you how clear your own text is.** You know it too well; use a diagnostic (17:46).
- **These principles describe how readers read, not his personal taste** (24:06).
- **Against "just write for yourself".** He calls it honestly one of the worst pieces of writing advice. Write for readers, and see yourself as posing a problem they want solved, not as writing about a topic that interests you (31:36).
- **Novelty and effort are not value** (26:10 to 26:33).
- **Motivation can carry readers through bad sentences; nothing carries them through incoherence** (32:42).
- **Change of mind:** none stated. He does qualify his own rule: right after saying not to use nouns for actions, he lays out when to use them (4:54 to 5:34).

### 5. People and books named
- Joseph Campbell (hero's journey), Dan Harmon (story circle), and the South Park creators' "but / therefore" rule. He names these only as big-picture patterns to set aside for clarity work (0:41).
- Rembrandt and Picasso, as an analogy for construal (3:08).
- Watson and Crick, as an example sentence.
- The "curse of knowledge", named as a concept without a source (33:17).
- The follow-up video about a Pulitzer Prize-winning novelist and a Harvard Business Review essay; the novelist is not named here.
- (My inference, not stated; nothing in the corpus names these sources) Several of these ideas closely follow Joseph M. Williams's *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*:

  - old before new;
  - "issue and discussion", which Fitzpatrick calls "index";
  - the signals of coherence.

## V28: I Analysed 100 Opening Paragraphs. Here's What Makes Readers Obsessed (2026-01-11)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it addresses
It is for writers of any nonfiction opening: newsletters, white papers, research reports, sales pages, book proposals. Their problem is that readers don't care about their ideas, because the introduction opens with a hook, or with the author's own excitement, instead of value (0:00).

He says he analysed over 100 introductions from professional and academic genres. He credits the resulting pattern with helping clients get research funded and published, pitch startups, and turn readers into buyers.

### 2. Core argument
Value is not the author's belief that a topic matters, nor the effort spent on it. Value is a function of problems and their solutions (4:20).

His case study is George Mack's popular "high agency" essay. Its introduction, and the author's own announcement of it, lean on effort, conviction, a reading-time warning, a sense of secret discovery, and a famous quote used as a hook. It offers a topic but gives readers no reason to care (2:44 to 3:57).

The fix is a three-part introduction:
1. **Status quo:** common ground the reader already accepts.
2. **Problem:** a condition plus a consequence, which unsettles that ground.
3. **Solution:** a clear preview of the idea as the answer.

An optional prelude may come first.

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 The two-vendor test (his crowded-market analogy)
- **What it is:** Picture your reader walking through a busy market with full arms, trying to get home. One vendor talks about how long he spent perfecting his product and how important he thinks it is. The other names the shopper's problem and offers a fix. The shopper stops for the second (1:15 to 1:59).

  Test your opening: which vendor does it sound like?
- **Why it works:** Readers stop for their own problem, not for the writer's story. Most writers, even good ones, sound like the first vendor (1:59).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Read your first paragraph.
  2. Highlight every phrase about you: your effort, your beliefs, your excitement, how important you think it is.
  3. Highlight every phrase about the reader's problem.
  4. If the first colour dominates, rewrite around the reader's problem.
- **Symptoms:**
  - "After months of research..."
  - "I believe this may be the most important..."
  - "It felt like a secret..."
  - Promises that it will change how you see reality.
  - A quote or anecdote that only introduces the topic.
- **When not to use it:** No limit given. He notes that the Mack essay succeeded anyway; he chose it to punch up, not down (2:27).
- **Example:**
  - Before: "I spent two years studying sleep, and I'm convinced it's the most underrated productivity tool there is."
  - After: "If you've ever answered emails at 11 p.m. and reread them the next morning with dread, you already know what tired decisions cost."
- **Timestamp:** V28 1:15 to 4:20.

#### 3.2 Establish the status quo, or common ground (his term: "establishing the status quo")
- **What it is:** Before you challenge the reader, open with something they already accept, so they nod along.
- **Why it works:** An opening hot take, such as telling someone that everything they believe about success is wrong, makes people defensive. Finding common ground first changes how the challenge feels (4:20 to 5:18). Once readers are nodding and feel understood, you have lined up the dominoes and can knock them over (5:18 to 6:16).

  If the opening contradicts the direction of the essay, the reader senses there is something about the world they don't yet understand (6:35).
- **How to apply (his three steps):**
  1. **Invert your idea.** Take whatever you're building toward and think about its opposite (6:16 to 6:35).
  2. **Find what readers already believe:** something undisputed, obvious, or widely accepted. This is the shared context. He gives three sources (7:05 to 7:43):
     - historical background;
     - a recent event, such as a survey result;
     - a common belief.

     State it plainly and don't over-explain. The only goal is to get readers thinking they already know this (7:43).
  3. **Set up the contrast.** Treat the status quo as the setup for a *but* or *however*. Check that it deserves to be challenged (8:06).
- **Symptoms:**
  - The opening fires the thesis at the reader.
  - A famous quote is used as a hook.
  - A clichéd opener.
  - Nothing the reader would agree with before the turn.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use the three openers (history, recent event, common belief) as clichéd hooks. They exist to build common ground (7:43).

  In his high-agency rewrite, the status quo isn't a literal opposite of the thesis. It is a familiar, accepted observation that some people wait for orders, which the problem then shows to be costly (5:18, 9:06). So the status quo can be either the opposite belief or an accepted state whose cost nobody has counted.
- **Example:**
  - Hot take: "Open offices are a disaster."
  - Common ground: "Most managers assume open offices encourage collaboration, and for quick questions they often do."
  - The three sources:
    - History: "For forty years, hospitals have judged quality by readmission rates."
    - Recent event: "Last month the city announced a record number of new bike lanes."
    - Common belief: "Most people assume a bigger team finishes a project faster."
- **Timestamp:** V28 4:20 to 8:44.

#### 3.3 The problem statement: condition plus consequence (his terms: "condition" and "consequence")
- **What it is:** A problem has two parts:
  - **Condition:** what's actually wrong or unstable in the status quo.
  - **Consequence:** the cost of staying there, or the benefit of fixing it.

  A good problem statement shows that what seemed stable isn't, and shows the reader the cost of staying put (8:44).
- **Why it works:** Without a cost, the reader can shrug and say "so what?" (10:19). A problem is only a problem when the reader cares about it. They need either a cost they don't want to pay or a benefit they want (10:19).
- **How to apply (his three steps):**
  1. **State the condition** with a turn word, *but* or *however*. These words are overused elsewhere; reserve them for announcing the problem, at least in the opening, where they signal "here is the problem" (11:00).
  2. **Run the "so what?" test (his term).** Imagine your specific reader saying "so what?" to the condition. Your answer is the consequence or benefit. You can't do this for a general audience; you have to picture who is reading and what they care about (11:00 to 11:53).
  3. **Keep condition and consequence close together.** Readers need both before they care (11:53).

  You can scale the consequence to each reader group. In his example, it hurts individuals' careers, and for CEOs and founders it also slows whole organizations (9:34 to 10:19).
- **Symptoms:**
  - A "but" with no cost attached.
  - A cost pitched at "society" or "everyone".
  - The condition and consequence paragraphs apart.
  - A problem that describes something the reader doesn't mind.
- **When not to use it:** No exception given. (Inferred) The "so what?" answer changes with the reader, so there is no universal consequence.
- **Example:**
  - Condition: "But when firms removed office walls, face-to-face conversations fell rather than rose."
  - Consequence for managers: "Teams that lose those conversations take longer to solve anything more complex than a quick question."
  - Consequence for employees: "...and the people who most need mentoring get the least of it."
- **Timestamp:** V28 8:44 to 12:20.

#### 3.4 The solution preview (his term: "solution")
- **What it is:** Right after the problem, tell readers what you are offering. Give the promise and a general direction, not all the details.
- **Why it works:** A reader left with only the problem is frustrated. They were told their view is wrong and given nothing to do about it (12:20). A clear preview lets them decide whether to come along (13:54).
- **How to apply:**
  1. State that there is a solution and roughly what it is.
  2. Signal what kind it is, and why it's achievable.
  3. Say what the text will deliver.

  Decide which kind of problem you have (14:11 to 14:52):
  - **Practical problem** (actions and behaviours): the solution tells readers what to do.
  - **Conceptual problem** (what we don't know or understand): the solution gives understanding, so readers change what they think.
- **Symptoms (his two failure modes):**
  1. A long background lecture before the idea, like answering "what time is it?" with a lecture on building a watch (12:20).
  2. A vague solution, like "we need to think differently". Of course, but how (12:20 to 13:06)?
- **When not to use it:** Don't give all the details here; preview only (13:06). He calls his sample rewrite unpolished. Focus on the functions, not his wording (13:36).
- **Example:**
  - Vague: "We need to rethink the office."
  - Practical preview: "Offices work best when they give people a choice between quiet rooms and shared tables. This essay shows how three firms made that switch without new construction."
  - Conceptual preview: "What drives collaboration isn't proximity but being able to see each other's work, and that changes what we should measure."
- **Timestamp:** V28 12:20 to 14:52.

#### 3.5 The optional prelude (his term: "prelude")
- **What it is:** A statistic, anecdote, quote, or even a meme placed before the status quo (15:14).
- **Why it works:** It can add colour. He thinks a tasteful meme suits an internet essay.
- **How to apply:** Use it only if it earns its place. Keep it short, then move into status quo, problem and solution.
- **Symptoms of misuse:** The prelude replaces the problem, and the problem never comes.
- **When not to use it:** If the problem is good enough, you don't need a prelude. The problem should be interesting on its own (15:14).
- **Example:** Prelude: "Last year, one team at a mid-size firm held 212 meetings about a single product launch." Then the status quo about meetings.
- **Timestamp:** V28 15:14 to 15:55.

**The formula he states (14:52):**
1. Status quo: a brief statement of something widely accepted.
2. Problem: a condition plus a consequence.
3. Solution: only now is the reader ready to accept your idea.

### 4. Stances and warnings
- **Against hooks.** Readers don't care because introductions open with hooks instead of value (0:00). Openers such as history, recent event and common belief must build common ground, not act as hooks (7:43).
- **Against selling an idea through effort, conviction or novelty.** Months of work and the author's belief don't create value (1:59, 3:57).
- **Against leading with the hot take.** He says don't come off the top ropes; get agreement first (4:20).
- **Against long background before the idea, and against vague solutions** (12:20 to 13:06).
- **Reserve *but* and *however* for the problem turn,** since they are overused elsewhere (11:00).
- **Readers care about problems and solutions, not topics.** He says fiction writers know this by heart and nonfiction teaching neglects it (15:55).
- **On choosing examples to critique:** pick successful work, so the critique punches up, not down (2:27).
- **Change of mind:** none stated. Compared with V27, the concession move is folded into common ground, and the method is presented as three parts instead of five moves (see "Across").

### 5. People and books named
- George Mack, author of the "high agency" essay, used as the case study.
- Eric Weinstein, mentioned as someone who spoke about high agency earlier (3:37).
- Justice Potter Stewart, whose "I know it when I see it" line is quoted inside Mack's introduction. Fitzpatrick doesn't use it as a source.
- The economists' "principal-agent problem", used as common ground in his rewrite (5:18).
- His own analysis of 100+ introductions.
- The follow-up video about a Pulitzer Prize-winning writer and an HBR article.
- (My inference, not stated) The problem framing of common ground, destabilizing condition, cost and resolution matches the introduction model in Williams's *Style* and in *The Craft of Research*. It is not named anywhere in this corpus.

## V29: How to Articulate Your Thoughts More Clearly than 99% of Writers (2026-01-18)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it addresses
It is for experts whose writing sounds dull, vague or inarticulate. He mentions clients applying to an MIT PhD and for startup grant funding. Their problem is that they write the way they speak.

Speech is full of fillers because the mouth runs ahead of the mind (0:00, 3:28). Readers are not listeners, and they want writing that is simple, elegant and efficient, like good code. Bloated writing makes readers struggle however valuable the ideas are, and it costs the writer authority (0:00 to 0:51, 3:28).

### 2. Core argument
The cure is **compression (his term)**. Remove redundancy and bloat so the reader carries less cognitive load. The aim is not short sentences: length doesn't matter if sentences are well shaped (2:15 to 2:36).

He gives five techniques. Three prune:
1. Filler words.
2. Doublets.
3. Three kinds of redundancy.

Two "add back in":
4. Replace phrases with the exact word.
5. Write in the affirmative.

He presents compression as the start of revision, not the end (20:02).

### 3. Techniques

His opening demonstration is one bloated sentence with five flaws, reduced to a four-word version (0:51 to 2:15):
- a pointless "personal" before "opinion";
- the "in my opinion" frame itself;
- two phrases that both mean "consider";
- a doublet;
- a modifier implied by its noun.

#### 3.1 Cut filler words (his terms: "filler words", "verbal tics", "throat clearing")
- **What it is:** Delete words that feel natural in speech but add almost no meaning in writing. His list (4:44): actually, basically, certain, various, particularly, really, virtually, individual, generally, given, practically.
- **Why it works:** In speech, fillers keep you fluent while you search for the next word. In writing, they are "verbal static" that make the message bloated and imprecise. Cutting them makes you sound precise and authoritative (3:28 to 4:44). He calls them nervous habits that dilute the message before it begins.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Search the draft for the listed words.
  2. Delete each one, and see whether meaning is lost.
  3. Also cut whole vague phrases they prop up ("certain elements that involve").
  4. Be ruthless. The goal is clarity through minimalism, not short sentences (4:44).
- **Symptoms:**
  - Hedgy, soft prose.
  - Several of the listed words per paragraph.
  - Sentences that sound like transcribed talk.
- **When not to use it:** He grants that every word has a purpose, but says these are generally the culprits (4:44). (Inferred) Keep one where it carries real meaning, for example "certain" meaning sure, or "individual" as a contrast with "group".
- **Example:**
  - Before: "The new policy basically aims to address certain issues that generally affect various departments."
  - After: "The new policy addresses problems shared across departments."
- **Timestamp:** V29 2:36 to 5:39.

#### 3.2 Cut doublets (his term: "doublets")
- **What it is:** Paired near-synonyms such as each and every, first and foremost, full and complete, true and accurate, any and all, basic and fundamental (5:57). Keep the stronger word and move on (6:19).
- **Why it works:** Writers think doublets sound thorough and authoritative, but to modern readers they sound repetitive, like overkill. He explains the history: after the Norman Conquest, writers paired a French or Latin word with an English one to show they were learned. The habit survives, but its purpose is gone (5:39 to 6:19).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Scan for "X and Y" pairs of near-synonyms.
  2. Keep the more exact word.
  3. Delete the other.
- **Symptoms:** Legalistic or ceremonial pairs, and "and" joining two words that mean the same thing.
- **When not to use it:** No exception stated. (Inferred) Fixed legal terms may be needed in legal documents.
- **Example:**
  - Before: "We are fully and completely committed to providing true and accurate data."
  - After: "We are committed to accurate data."
- **Timestamp:** V29 5:39 to 7:18.

#### 3.3 Cut redundancy: modifiers, categories, general implications (his three types)
- **What it is:** Delete words already implied by other words (7:36). He calls catching these what separates competent writers from exceptional ones, and says readers use it to judge whether a writer is paying attention (6:19 to 7:36).

  **(a) Redundant modifiers.** The word already contains its modifier: upcoming after "anticipate", totally before "transform", previous before "experience", ultimate before "result", suddenly before "astonish" (7:36 to 8:48). Common culprits: core essentials, basic fundamentals, future plans, free gift, consensus of opinion, in actual reality (8:48). You have to train your ear.

  **(b) Redundant categories.** The word already implies its category: "phase of development", "intuitive in nature", "large in size", "round in shape", "at an early time" (8:48 to 10:06). Sometimes you must turn an adjective into an adverb, so "in a logical manner" becomes "logically" (10:06 to 10:32).

  **(c) General implications.** The redundancy is spread across several words: trying to learn, when "learn" already implies trying; conventions for writing a genre, when "conventions" implies the genre; the art of poetry, when poetry is an art. These are the hardest to catch. Approach each sentence intending to delete as much as possible (10:32 to 11:34).
- **Why it works:** Every redundant word is work for the reader with no return in meaning. Leaving them in signals inattention.
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each modifier, ask whether its noun or verb already implies it. If so, cut it.
  2. For each "X in nature / size / shape / manner" or "X of Y", ask whether X or Y already names its category. If so, cut it. Turn adjectives into adverbs where needed.
  3. For whole sentences, ask what the sentence says in the fewest words. Look for verbs that imply attempt, nouns that imply their field, and descriptions that restate a definition.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Pairs like past history, end result, future plans.
  - "...in nature", "...in size", "...in a ... manner".
  - Sentences that feel padded when you can't point to one bad word.
- **When not to use it:** He admits there is no firm rule, especially for categories (10:06). It is a matter of ear and editing effort.
- **Example:**
  - Before: "Participants were asked to try to recall events from their past history of early childhood years that were significant in importance."
  - After: "Participants recalled important childhood events."
- **Timestamp:** V29 6:19 to 11:34.

#### 3.4 Find the exact word: replace phrases with a word (his term: the French *le mot juste*, "the exact right word")
- **What it is:** After pruning, some sentences still use five words for one idea. Compress them by choosing a single precise word, a verb or noun that carries the whole phrase (11:34).
- **Why it works:** He compares it to a cross-country drive versus a direct flight. You can't make the drive much faster; you change the vehicle (12:41).

  A wide vocabulary is for compressing, not for showing off. Using an expensive word where a cheap one would do is an abuse of vocabulary (13:07).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Look through the words to the ideas. Count the ideas and count the words. If there are many words for few ideas, look for replacements (15:56).
  2. Replace each long phrase with one verb or noun. His walkthrough (13:34 to 15:21) turns:
     - a long description of reviewing a draft into "revise";
     - a clause used as the verb's object into "your writing". He usually avoids nominalizations, but a transitive verb needs an object and a clause there is awkward;
     - "the thing to do before anything else" into "first";
     - "use X instead of Y" into "replace";
     - wordy definitions into "examples" and "abstractions".
  3. Fix the word order the new verb requires.
  4. Swap stock spoken phrases (15:21):

     | Phrase | Replacement |
     |---|---|
     | the reason for | why |
     | despite the fact that | although / even though |
     | in the event that | if |
     | in a situation where | when |
     | concerning the matter of | about |
- **Symptoms:**
  - Long descriptive phrases that stand in for a common word.
  - "The thing to do is...", "The way in which...".
  - Clause-shaped objects.
- **When not to use it:** He says there is no formula, only vocabulary, alertness and the wish to trim (15:21). Don't reach for rare words to impress (13:07).
- **Example:**
  - Before: "Due to the fact that the meeting was moved to a later date, we are not in a position to give a response at this point in time."
  - After: "Because the meeting was postponed, we can't respond yet."
- **Timestamp:** V29 11:34 to 16:20.

#### 3.5 Write in the affirmative (his framing: write in the positive, not the negative)
- **What it is:** Express ideas positively. Replace "not + word" with a single word that means the positive opposite, and untangle stacked negatives.
- **Why it works:** A negative makes the reader process the sentence and then reverse it. That is mental arithmetic, and it can happen several times in one sentence (16:20 to 16:51). The worst case is negatives combined with passives and nominalizations (18:00).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Translate simple negatives (17:13):

     | Negative | Affirmative |
     |---|---|
     | not different | similar |
     | not the same | different |
     | not allow | prevent |
     | not notice | overlook |
     | not many | few |
     | not often | rarely |
     | not include | omit |

  2. Watch for words that are negative without saying "not" (18:59): preclude, prevent, lack, fail, doubt, reject, avoid, deny, refuse, exclude, contradict, prohibit, bar, without, against, unless, except. When these combine with explicit negatives (*not*, *no*), comprehension collapses (20:02).
  3. For a tangled sentence (18:20 to 19:02):
     1. Move the actions out of nouns and into verbs.
     2. Split it into clauses.
     3. Turn each negative into its positive.
     4. Drop clauses that become unnecessary.

     His example ends as a single conditional instruction.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Two or more negatives in one sentence.
  - "Unless... not...".
  - Readers re-reading a rule to work out what they must actually do.
- **When not to use it:** Keep the negative when you want to stress what *not* to do; sometimes the warning is the point (20:02).
- **Example:**
  - Before: "Claims will not be processed unless receipts are not missing for amounts that are not under $50."
  - After: "Attach receipts to every claim of $50 or more."
  - A negative to keep: "Do not mix bleach with ammonia."
- **Timestamp:** V29 16:20 to 20:02.

### 4. Stances and warnings
- **Against "write the way you speak",** which he calls one of the worst pieces of writing advice. It fills prose with verbal tics and makes experts sound vague and unsure (2:36 to 3:28). V30 revisits this advice and reinterprets it (see "Across").
- **Compression is not shortness.** The goal is cognitive load. Sentence length doesn't matter if sentences are shaped well (2:15 to 2:36, 15:56).
- **Readers judge competence by redundancy.** Catching implied redundancy is how they tell attentive writers from careless ones (6:19 to 7:36).
- **Vocabulary is for precision, not display** (13:07).
- **His own nominalization rule bends:** he normally avoids them, but uses one as an object when a transitive verb needs it (13:34). This matches V27's rule that nominalizations work as objects.
- **Compression is only the start;** afterwards you "spruce things up" (20:02).
- **Change of mind:** none stated.

### 5. People and books named

- The Norman Conquest and French and Latin borrowing are named as the historical origin of doublets.
- *Le mot juste* is credited only to "the French".
- The follow-up video covers "31 writing tools" from great writers.

## V30: Write 10X Clearer: Do These 3 Writing Exercises to Connect with Readers (2026-01-25)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it addresses
It is for professionals who write as part of their job: academics applying for funding, copywriters writing sales pages, entrepreneurs building a personal brand, scientists (0:00 to 0:58).

Their problem is prose that "sounds like writing". It is correct and carefully laboured, but formal and distant, and it shuts the reader out. The reader has to fill the gaps:
- Why was that said?
- How does it connect to what came before?
- Am I being talked to, or talked at? (1:38)

He argues that clarity matters more than correctness (0:00).

He labels this inward-turned style **"autistic writing" (his term)**. He means it through the Greek *autos*, "self", adds a disclaimer toward neurodivergent people, and includes himself among "we autistic writers" (0:58 to 1:38, 2:09). For a skill, a neutral label such as "self-enclosed writing" carries the concept without the clinical word.

### 2. Core argument
The usual cure, "write the way you speak", is misread as surface tricks: "use" for "utilize", "but" for "however" (1:38 to 2:09). What writing needs from conversation is not its words but its **scene**.

That scene is **joint attention** (a cognitive-science term): two people in one place attending to one visible thing, each aware that the other is attending. Looking together, they share a mental context, and that shared context anchors clear writing (2:44 to 3:20).

Professional writing is far from that scene. The audience is complex, the purpose abstract, and there is a time gap between writing and reading. So:
1. Map the real **situational network** with the **rhetorical planning wheel**.
2. **Blend**: anchor that network in the classic scene of joint attention.

Clear writing keeps both the anchor and the network in mind. Doing it on purpose gives you a style you can control. Drifting in and out of the scene without knowing it confuses readers (15:01 to 15:41).

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 Exercise 1: the joint-attention drill (his terms: "joint attention", "shared attention", "the classic scene")
- **What it is:** A spoken warm-up with a partner. Take turns presenting observable details of your surroundings to each other for about ten minutes (3:20 to 4:46).
- **Why it works:** It trains the felt experience of sharing one mental space with another person, which is what later has to be built into the prose (4:46).

  Offering details "like a gift" makes you point precisely, not vaguely (3:54). Keeping to what's present and verifiable means both people are looking at the same thing.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Sit with a partner: a classmate, a friend, a spouse over coffee.
  2. Take turns offering one precise, observable detail at a time: a shape, a count, a sound pattern, a comparison.
  3. Use only what is present and checkable. No imagination, no inference (3:54).
  4. Go back and forth for about ten minutes.
  5. Alone: record yourself, or describe a picture as if to someone sitting beside you (4:46).
- **Symptoms it addresses:**
  - Prose that talks at the reader.
  - Details only the writer can see.
  - Gaps the reader has to fill.
- **When not to use it:** He calls it "a bit of a conceit" (4:46). It's a training exercise, and professional writing is more complex. It leads into exercises 2 and 3.
- **Example:**
  - Vague: "Look at that building."
  - As a gift: "The office block across the road has four rows of windows, and only the top row still has its blinds half-down, all at the same angle."
- **Timestamp:** V30 2:44 to 5:36.

#### 3.2 Exercise 2: map the situational network with the rhetorical planning wheel (his terms: "rhetorical planning wheel", "situational network"; his rule that the text must match the context)
- **What it is:** A planning tool for any professional text.
  - **At the center:** genre and actions. The genre is the text type, such as an email to a professor, a research paper or a white paper, and each genre has its own patterns and functions.
  - **Around the rim:** the parts of the situational network. He focuses on four (7:46 to 10:12):
    - **Purpose and actions.** What are you trying to achieve, and what will the text do to achieve it?
    - **Writer's role.** Your relation to the topic: are you the expert, a junior asking for funding, someone bringing your own authority or relying on secondary sources? Your relation to the reader: the right social attitude, such as confidence without arrogance. Your relation to the information: is it yours, and how sure are you?
    - **Audience.** Who reads this? What do they already know? What do they want to know? What will they use the text for, what are they looking for, and where do they expect to find it?
    - **Context.** The text as something acting in the world. What event caused it to be written? What happened before it, and what should happen after, such as an acceptance or a rejection?
- **Why it works:** The context is a network too large to hold in your head, so the wheel breaks it into parts you can analyse (7:08). Generic writing advice is useless for professional purposes; the text must fit its context (10:12).

  His client story (5:36 to 7:08): a graduate student applying to Stanford wrote a personal statement with the anecdote most guides recommend, a love of tech videos. It was error-free and guide-compliant, and completely wrong. To the panel it signalled passive consumption, while the panel screens for "intellectual vitality". The fix was not a better anecdote. It was mapping what the readers needed to believe.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Name the genre and its usual functions.
  2. Write the purpose as a belief or action you want from the reader.
  3. Apply his "what must they also believe?" question. To get the reader to believe X, what else must they believe? Break X into specific qualities, then find a real story or piece of evidence for each (7:08).
  4. Sketch your role toward the topic, the reader and the information, and set the tone to match.
  5. Profile the audience: prior knowledge, wants, uses, where they'll look for things.
  6. Write down the triggering event and the outcome the text should produce.
  7. Do all this before writing a word (10:12).
  8. Note how far this network is from the park-bench scene. The farther it is, the harder clarity gets, and the more you need exercise 3 (10:12 to 11:13).
- **Symptoms:**
  - Advice-compliant but off-target content.
  - Anecdotes that show the wrong trait.
  - A tone that is either grovelling or arrogant.
  - Information placed where readers won't look for it.
  - The writer can't say what should happen after the text is read.
- **When not to use it:** He doesn't say. (Inferred) For casual or low-stakes texts, the network is already close to the classic scene and needs little mapping.
- **Example (grant proposal):**
  - Purpose: panel believes the project is fundable.
  - "What must they also believe?" The question matters, the method works, and this team can deliver on time.
  - Evidence for each: a pilot result, a validated method, a previous project delivered on schedule.
  - Role: early-career researcher, confident about the pilot data and modest about scale.
  - Audience: three reviewers skimming forty proposals, looking for feasibility in the first page.
  - Context: triggered by an open call; outcome is funded or rejected.
- **Timestamp:** V30 4:46 to 11:13.

#### 3.3 Exercise 3: blending, or anchoring the network in the classic scene (his terms: "blending", "anchor")
- **What it is:** Keep the real, complex situation, but write in a style that treats it as the simple scene: you and one reader side by side, looking at the same thing. His mappings (13:05 to 14:03):

  | Real situation | Written as |
  |---|---|
  | Distant or large audience | One person sitting beside you |
  | Abstract topic | Something visible you can point at |
  | Past event | Present tense, as if unfolding in front of you |
  | Complex purpose (persuasion, analysis) | Showing: show the problem, then show the solution |

  He sums up the last row as: don't argue for the policy; show the problem, then the solution.
- **Why it works:** His model is a Jane Austen letter to her sister. Austen writes alone, far away, to be read days later, yet says she has been "talking" to her sister throughout. She anchors distance, delay and solitude in the scene of two people talking (11:13 to 13:05).

  The blend lets the reader feel present even when the real situation is complex. Doing it deliberately avoids an uncontrolled style that slips in and out of the scene (15:01).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Take the network you mapped with the wheel.
  2. Write one paragraph of your real material anchored in the classic scene: one reader beside you, present tense where it fits, something to point at, show rather than argue (14:03).
  3. Read it aloud (14:03).
  4. Expect it to feel artificial at first, and repeat until it is automatic (14:03).
  5. In revision, check where the text leaves the scene: a sudden lecture, an unseen abstraction, a shift into talking at the reader.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Past-tense reports of abstractions.
  - Arguing with no picture of the problem.
  - Distance and formality.
  - Readers unsure whether they are being addressed.
- **When not to use it:** He gives no exception. (Inferred) Genres with strict conventions, such as a methods section, may limit tense and address. Keep the showing and the shared object even when you can't use "you" or the present tense.
- **Example:**
  - Before: "Analysis of the 2023 inventory records indicated that stock discrepancies were attributable to manual entry procedures."
  - After: "Look at the March inventory sheet. The warehouse counts 212 boxes; the system says 180. Every one of those gaps traces back to a number someone typed by hand."
- **Timestamp:** V30 11:13 to 15:41.

### 4. Stances and warnings
- **Clarity over correctness.** Error-free writing can still fail (0:00, 5:36).
- **"Write the way you speak" is misunderstood.** Swapping formal words for plain ones misses the point; what matters is the scene of conversation (2:09).
- **Generic writing advice and genre guides fail in professional contexts.** The Stanford anecdote followed the guides and was wrong (5:36, 10:12).
- **Map the context before writing a single word** (10:12).
- **Clarity gets harder the further you are from the classic scene:** a more complex audience, a more abstract purpose, a larger time gap (10:12 to 11:13).
- **Consistency matters.** Unconscious drifting in and out of the scene leaves readers unable to follow (15:01).
- **Clarity is only half of expert writing;** you also need something valuable to say (15:41).
- **On his own label:** he uses "autistic" deliberately and etymologically, with a disclaimer, and applies it to himself.

### 5. People and books named
- Jane Austen's letters to her sister Cassandra (11:13).
- "Cognitive scientists", for joint attention, with no individual named (2:44).
- Stanford's admissions criterion of "intellectual vitality".
- His client Yun.
- (My inference, not stated) "The classic scene", joint attention as the model for clear prose, and "blending" come from Francis-Noël Thomas and Mark Turner's *Clear and Simple as the Truth* (classic style), and from Turner's work with Gilles Fauconnier on conceptual blending. The joint-attention concept comes from developmental psychology (Michael Tomasello). None of these names appears in the corpus. I have not identified a source for the "rhetorical planning wheel".

## Across these five videos

### One reader model runs underneath all five
The reader:
- reads in a line;
- holds only three or four chunks;
- carries a briefcase from sentence to sentence (V26 3:37 to 4:55);
- has only what is on the page, since they don't share the writer's background.

Three videos build on this:
- **V27** names the writer's side of the problem: the curse of knowledge makes your own text feel coherent (V27 17:46, 33:17).
- **V30** names the reader's side of it: the reader is forced to fill gaps the writer left (V30 1:38).
- **V29** puts a cost on every wasted word: cognitive load (V29 2:36).

Together these explain why self-assessment fails and why he keeps giving mechanical diagnostics: the underline test (V27 18:19), the "so what?" test (V28 11:00), and looking through the words to the ideas (V29 15:56).

### V26's four briefcase items grow into V27's system
| V26 briefcase item | Where it goes in V27 |
|---|---|
| Topic | Characters in the subject position and the underline diagnostic (V27 2:04, 18:19) |
| End of the sentence | The Busan-taxi handoff, and "complexity at the end" (V27 15:07, 24:06) |
| Sensory or concrete | Actions in verbs and the case against nominalizations (V27 3:08 to 11:43) |
| Expectations | The index as a promise the discussion keeps (V27 38:15), and at larger scale the introduction's problem as the expectation the whole text pays off |

V26 teaches from memory psychology. V27 restates the same ideas as grammar: subject, verb, nominalization, active and passive.

His examples show these are separate levers. V26's "concrete" rewrite is in the passive voice (V26 10:24), and V27 defends the passive as the tool for handoffs (V27 13:56). A skill should treat concreteness, topic consistency and voice separately, and never apply "use active voice" as a reflex.

### Cohesion is necessary but not sufficient
V26 praises the relay-race handoff. V27 then shows a passage that hands off perfectly and still goes nowhere, and adds coherence: consistent topics, a theme vocabulary and a point (V27 15:42 to 17:46). The progression matters for a skill. Check handoffs first, then check that the subjects form one small cast and that a point sentence ties it together.

### The introduction method sharpens from V27 to V28
**V27 (27:52 to 31:36)** gives five moves:
1. status quo, as the inverted thesis;
2. concession;
3. destabilizing *but*;
4. cost or benefit;
5. solution.

**V28** repackages this as three parts plus an optional prelude, and adds:
- the two-vendor test;
- three sources of common ground (history, recent event, common belief);
- the "so what?" test aimed at a *specific* reader;
- consequences scaled to different reader groups;
- keeping condition and consequence close together;
- reserving *but* and *however* for the problem turn;
- two failure modes after the problem: a background lecture, or a vague solution;
- the practical-versus-conceptual solution split.

In V28 the separate concession move is absorbed into common ground: the status quo is framed with sympathy (he grants the common belief holds for a lot of people). His V28 example also shows that the status quo need not be the literal opposite of the thesis. It can be an accepted state whose cost nobody has counted.

The market-vendor analogy appears in both. In V27 it is one vendor boasting of novelty and effort (V27 25:31); in V28 it becomes two vendors, one of whom names the shopper's problem (V28 1:15).

V27's three levels of reader awareness do not reappear in V28. They would be a useful input for choosing how much of the problem a given reader needs spelled out.

### "Worst advice" is a running theme, and V29 and V30 fit together
- V27 attacks "just write for yourself" (V27 31:36).
- V29 attacks "write the way you speak": speech brings fillers, doublets and clumsy phrases (V29 2:36 to 3:28).
- V30 returns to the same advice and saves the useful part: keep the *scene* of conversation, a shared object and one reader beside you, and drop the idea that it means casual words (V30 2:09).

These don't contradict each other. They split speech into what to throw away (its noise) and what to keep (its relationship).

Read together, V29 and V30 also guard each other against overshoot:
- V29 pushes toward lean, code-like prose.
- V30 warns that careful, formal, distant prose shuts the reader out.

A skill should compress (V29) without going cold, and keep the reader in the scene (V30).

### Rules are tools with conditions, not bans
This is consistent across all five:
- Neither word order is "better" (V26 2:51).
- Nominalizations have four proper uses, and he uses one himself in V29 (V27 6:11; V29 13:34).
- The passive voice is often the right tool (V27 13:56).
- Negatives stay when the warning is the point (V29 20:02).
- A prelude is optional (V28 15:14).
- Writing is "not paint-by-numbers" (V27 4:54).
- Topic changes are fine with a reason (V26 9:19).

A skill built from these should diagnose, then offer a choice with its reason, not rewrite by rule.

### Concreteness recurs at every level
- Sensory words (V26).
- Concrete characters and verbs (V27).
- "Replace abstractions with examples", in his own worked sentence (V29 15:21).
- An abstract topic presented as something to point at, and "show, don't argue" (V30 13:05 to 14:03).

### Signals versus signposts
V26 promises flow without "boring signposts". V27 insists on headings, point sentences and repeated key terms.

These fit together. The signals V27 asks for are made of *content*: key concepts in headings, a point that names the terms to come. Empty metadiscourse is different. Expectations (V26) and index-then-discussion (V27) are how the text moves readers forward without announcing itself.

### Hooks versus expectations
V26 praises micro-expectations and even says "hooked" (V26 11:54). V28 opens by condemning hooks (V28 0:00).

The difference: an expectation is a specific open question that the text pays off. The hook V28 rejects is an attention-grabber that offers no value or problem, such as the author's excitement, a reading-time warning, or a famous quote. A skill should keep setup-and-payoff and cut value-free openers.

### Clarity depends on value and context, not only on sentences
- V27 turns midway from sentences to ideas: clear prose is worthless if the reader doesn't see the value, and motivated readers even find prose clearer (V27 25:12 to 27:52).
- V30 ends the same way: clarity is half of expert writing; the other half is having something valuable to say (V30 15:41).
- V30's rhetorical planning wheel adds that "correct" and "guide-compliant" can still be wrong for the context.

So clarity is not a property of text alone. It is:
- structure (V26, V27);
- plus the reader's motivation (V27, V28);
- plus fit to the situation (V30);
- plus economy (V29).

### Possible order of operations for a revising skill
He doesn't state this order in these videos; it is my reading of how they connect.
1. **Context:** map purpose and actions, role, audience and context, and ask "what must they also believe?" (V30).
2. **Value:** status quo, then problem (condition plus consequence, passing "so what?"), then solution preview (V28, V27).
3. **Structure:** index and discussion at every scale, and the four signals of coherence (V27).
4. **Paragraphs:** a consistent cast of topics (the underline test), then handoffs (V27, V26).
5. **Sentences:** characters as subjects, actions as verbs, complexity after the verb, concreteness, expectations (V27, V26).
6. **Compression:** fillers, doublets, redundancy, the exact word, the affirmative (V29). He says compression is "only the start" of polishing, and the system file places it late.
7. **Presence:** check that the text stays anchored in one reader beside you, looking at the same thing (V30).

### Numbers he gives
- Working memory holds three or four items (V26 3:37).
- Underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence (V27 18:19).
- Readers build momentum over nine or ten words (V27 21:35).
- His good example reaches subject and verb within five words (V27 23:26).
- The joint-attention drill runs about ten minutes (V30 3:54).
- He analysed 100+ introductions (V28 0:00).

### Development and shifts across the five
1. From reader psychology (V26), to grammar (V27), to rhetoric and value (V27 end, V28), to economy (V29), to relationship and context (V30).
2. His framing moves from the page toward the social situation around the text. By V30, clarity means matching a text to its context and holding a shared scene, not only arranging words.
3. None of the five states a change of mind, but he qualifies his own rules within each video, and the introduction model gets simpler and sharper from V27 to V28.
