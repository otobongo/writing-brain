# Writer Science: The Architecture of the Method

**What this is.** A structural reading of William Fitzpatrick's teaching across all 50 Writer Science videos (Feb 2024 to Sep 2026). Other reports list his techniques. This one explains how they fit together:
- which few ideas carry the weight;
- what stages a piece passes through;
- what depends on what;
- in what order to apply things, and why;
- what the minimal core is;
- what is optional or genre-specific.

It is built from the ten deep-read reports (`read_01.md` to `read_10.md`), with the load-bearing timestamps checked against the transcripts in `readable/`.

**Conventions.**
- Citations are V-number plus m:ss (the Source index in `writer-science-system.md` maps V1 to V50).
- **Bold** marks his own terms.
- *(Synthesis)* marks my reading of how his statements connect. He does not say it himself.
- All examples are mine, written in the spirit of his. Direct quotes are under 15 words.

**Scope.** His method is for **pointed** nonfiction: writing that answers a question or solves a problem for a reader. He excludes fiction, memoir, poetry and writing that makes no point (V50 0:48). His creative-nonfiction teaching appears below as genre modules (section 8).

---

## 1. The method in one paragraph

A text is not a container of meaning. It is a tool a particular reader uses, and it succeeds only if it moves that reader from where they are to where you want them (V33 8:53; V42 23:06). Readers spend attention only in exchange for value, and value means help with a problem they already have (V19 9:25; V31 3:35).

The trouble is that an expert's valuable ideas are too tangled to work out in the head, so the writer has to think on the page. Thinking produces text in the wrong order for a reader: question first, answer last, in the order of discovery, in private shorthand (V47 0:59 to 3:55). And the writer can't see this, because rereading your own words is remembering, not reading (V49 1:49 to 3:40).

So the method has two movements:
1. Write to think, alone, until you know your point.
2. Build a second text for a named reader. Put what they need in the places readers look: the title, the end of the introduction, the opening of every unit, and the start and end of every sentence. Then check those places on purpose instead of trusting how the draft feels (V50 4:24).

Everything else in the fifty videos refines one of these moves or is a module for a particular genre.

## 2. The load-bearing ideas

Six ideas carry the method, arranged like this:
- **One root idea** about where meaning lives.
- **Three facts about the reader** that follow from it.
- **Two facts about the writer** that explain why good writing is hard.

Almost every technique he teaches can be derived from one or two of them.

```
                   ROOT: meaning is made in a specific reader
                                     |
         +---------------------------+---------------------------+
         |                           |                           |
  READER AS PERSON            READER AS PROCESSOR          READER AS MEMBER
  with a problem              reading in a line            of a community
  -> VALUE                    -> POSITION                  -> RELATIONSHIP
         |                           |                           |
         +-------------- what the reader needs ------------------+
                                     |
                    ...but the writer faces two facts:
                                     |
         +---------------------------+---------------------------+
         |                                                       |
  THINKING RUNS THE OTHER WAY                   YOU CANNOT READ YOUR OWN TEXT
  (interference)                                (coffee stain / briefed reader)
  -> two phases, two documents                  -> check locations, not feel
```

### 2.1 The root: meaning is made in a specific reader

**The claim.** Writing is a function, not a thing: meaning comes from what a reader does with the text (V33 8:53). Coherence is not in the text either. It emerges between writer, reader and text (V49 8:21 to 10:14). His own master's thesis felt clear when he wrote it and is partly opaque to him ten years later. The text didn't change; the reader did (V49 7:34). The idea goes back to the channel's second video, where he describes his field as the effect a sentence has on a reader's mind, "good and bad rather than right and wrong" (V2 0:21).

**What follows from it.**
- **No rules, only tools.** There are no universal writing rules because there are no universal readers (V12 7:30; V31 14:02). Each of these is a tool, judged by its effect on this reader (V9 6:01; V14 7:04; V27 6:11; V42 8:28):
  - passive voice and nominalizations;
  - jargon, repetition and long sentences;
  - "I", "you" and em dashes.
- **The master test.** One question governs every language choice: does it move the reader from where they are to where you want them (V42 23:06)? Its close relatives:
  - "valuable to whom?" (V25 2:24);
  - "so what?", asked in the reader's voice (V28 11:00);
  - "will they use this to understand the world, or you?" (V43 10:09).
- **Function, not form.** His own fixed sequences look like templates: the introduction pattern, index-then-discussion, the uneven U. He reconciles this by saying they describe where readers look and what job each part does. They are not rules (V19 51:23; V31 22:48; V46 9:53). A genre is a sequence of functions that help two people reach a shared goal. Ordering coffee is his example: the wording varies from café to café, but the functions don't (V20 17:08 to 21:38). Anything that does no job for the reader gets cut, however well written (V19 20:33).
- **Name the reader before judging anything.** He won't look at a client's text until the reader questions are answered (V24 3:22). A skill built on this method can't call a sentence clear or unclear until it knows who is reading.

### 2.2 The reader as a person with a problem: value

**The claim.** Readers owe you nothing (V18 7:47). They trade attention for value, and value is a solution to a problem they already care about (V31 3:35 to 4:34; V46 8:20). Text that is clear but useless is still useless (V19 9:25). None of these count as value:
- novelty (V27 26:10);
- effort or conviction (V28 1:59);
- originality or your own interest (V25 5:42);
- the fact that something is true or interesting (V19 1:06:47).

Value also has to be **perceived**. A book can contain value that readers can't see because of how it is positioned (V18 9:14).

**Two kinds of problem.**
- A **practical** problem is solved by changing what the reader does.
- A **conceptual** problem is solved by changing what they understand.

Conceptual problems sit upstream of practical ones (V18 4:21; V22 10:19; V28 14:11). This lets even a piece about ideas promise a concrete change: swap this belief for that one.

**What follows from it.**
- **Value before clarity**, in every sense (V19 8:34 to 9:44).
- **The introduction is where value gets established**, by building a problem (V22 5:23; V27 25:12; V28; V46 7:04).
- **The gap model is replaced.** Instead of "nobody has studied this", show an error in the model the reader currently uses and what it costs them (V31 14:34 to 18:37).
- **The later checks are this idea at smaller scales:** "so what?", value words, the value audit (V19 49:20 to 1:08:47).

**An asymmetry that decides the order of work.** Motivation changes how clear a text seems:
- Readers who care read more attentively and even judge the prose clearer (V27 27:52).
- Motivated readers will struggle through clotted sentences. Incoherence defeats everyone (V27 32:42).

So sentence work can't fix a value problem, but value partly softens sentence problems. That is why value comes first (section 6).

### 2.3 The reader as a processor reading in a line: position

**The claim.** Readers take in a text one word at a time, left to right. They hold only three or four chunks in working memory. After each sentence they carry a compressed **mental briefcase** into the next, containing (V26 3:37 to 4:55):
- the sentence's last words;
- its topic;
- sensory impressions;
- expectations still open.

Because the order is fixed, readers form expectations about positions:
- **They wait impatiently for the verb.** His images are the **tunnel** (V8 0:42) and the **locomotive** (V12 0:41).
- **They take whatever comes first as what the sentence is about** (V9 1:55; V33 1:40). The subject position is a **spotlight** (V14 4:37; V24 15:13).
- **They give the end of a sentence the most weight.** That slot is the **stress position** (V2 9:38; V14 5:51).
- **They look to the opening of any unit to learn what it is about**, whether or not you meant it, and are misdirected when something else sits there (V46 9:53 to 10:50).

**What follows from it.** Nearly all his clarity teaching is about placement:
- characters as subjects, actions as verbs (V8 2:52; V27 0:41 to 5:34);
- short subject, verb early (V8 0:42; V27 21:35);
- old information in the topic, new in the comment (V15 12:00; V37 4:21);
- a consistent topic string (V9 1:55; V32; V48 3:00);
- an index at the top of each unit (V18 21:12; V27 37:51; V46 8:20).

He is explicit that these describe how readers read, not his taste (V27 24:06; V48 0:00). He also says most of clarity comes from how information is organized within sentences and paragraphs, not from vocabulary or grammar (V19 42:56; V37 0:19; V48 3:00).

### 2.4 The reader as a member of a community: relationship

**The claim.** Language is social. Words carry information, and they also signal who you are and whom you are addressing (V12 3:46 to 7:54; V31 10:11 to 13:10; V42 11:29 to 14:20).

Essays deal in judgments of better and worse, and that kind of knowledge exists only by consensus within a group. So the reader is, in his image, the "batter, not the icing" (V25 0:45 to 2:24). Writing means joining a conversation at a table of people already working on a problem (V12 14:37 to 18:09; V19 35:19 to 40:04).

**What follows from it.**
- **Register and code words.** Use the community's own value words, harvested "like an anthropologist" from texts they share (V19 16:59; V22 30:25; V35 15:55).
  - Jargon is legitimate when it is deliberate and aimed at the right community (V42 13:46).
  - He rejects "write at a fifth-grade level" (V12 3:46; V47 13:39).
- **Enter politely.** Concede what the prevailing view got right before you destabilize it (V19 35:19 to 40:04; V22 8:14; V27 29:22).
- **Persona and presence.** Readers judge the author on the page as a persona, so aim to sound like "a partner and not a preacher" (V18 35:20).
  - Personal language in openings builds connection (V4 10:27; V5 5:53; V19 1:00:28).
  - Making "you" the subject out of habit reads as lecturing (V18 27:28).
  - The ideal stance is side by side with one reader, looking at the same thing. He calls this the **classic scene** of joint attention (V30 2:44; V43 4:05 to 4:56; V9 15:37).
- **Voice and block are relational.** By 2026 he argues that flat voice and block largely disappear when you write for specific people you respect and want respect from (V44 30:39 to 31:59; V45 1:08 to 2:37).
- **Evidence depends on the community.** What counts as evidence depends on the reader and the genre (V25 8:27; V50 18:24). So do hedging norms (V19 1:02:55).

### 2.5 The writer's first fact: thinking runs the other way (interference)

**The claim.** Valuable expertise is like a box of tangled Christmas lights. You can't untangle it in your head, only by writing (V35 6:15; V47 0:59). So writing is thinking (V3 11:39; V11 6:14; V38 10:24).

But thinking on the page produces a text organized by the writer's process (V19 25:11 to 27:22; V31 20:45; V47 2:51, 12:59):
- it moves from uncertainty to certainty, so the answer comes at the end;
- it follows the order of discovery;
- it puts background first;
- it contains loops, side trails and private, compressed shorthand.

Readers want the opposite (V47 3:55, 7:16):
- certainty up front;
- a journey from a stable, familiar world to the discovery that something is wrong with it;
- a route that starts from where they are.

He calls this clash **interference** and names it the main problem of expert writing (V47 0:00). His image: giving a friend directions to a restaurant by describing your own drive from your own house (V24 4:18; V35 9:41; V47 10:18).

**What follows from it.**
- **Two functions, two phases.** First write for yourself, to find the idea. Then write for the reader (V11 2:03; V19 21:46; V31 7:55; V38 18:28; V42 16:31 to 20:01).
  - Bring the reader in while you are still thinking and you get block, and your opinions bend toward the imagined audience (V20 1:05 to 5:22; V38 18:28).
  - Leave the reader out of the second phase and you get pieces nobody uses (V38 19:34).
- **Line editing can't fix it.** A draft structured by your thinking can't be polished into a text for readers. The route has to be rebuilt (V31 8:47; V35 12:09; V47 17:30).
- **Reader complaints are symptoms.** "Unclear", "dense" and "disorganized" report friction, not its cause (V32 3:24; V47 6:18 to 7:16; V24 15:50).

*(Synthesis)* The two phases are about orientation, not strict timing. He calls "write first, fix later" "completely backwards", because writing in the thinking phase is itself recursive: you reread, find the best idea, and restructure as you go (V23 36:18). What has to be kept apart is the writer's pattern and the reader's pattern, not drafting and rereading.

### 2.6 The writer's second fact: you cannot read your own text

**The claim.** Rereading your own draft is association, not interpretation. The words remind you of the idea you already hold, like a coffee stain that means something only to the person who spilled it. He calls this the **coffee stain problem** (V24 0:52; V49 1:49 to 3:40).

- **The study he cites.** In a Carnegie Mellon study, readers briefed on a topic caught fewer structural errors than cold readers, because they filled the gaps from what they already knew. The writer is always the most briefed reader there is (V50 2:00 to 4:24).
- **Resting the draft doesn't work.** Setting it aside and rereading "as a stranger" fails (V50 2:00).
- **Your own document always feels coherent.** The curse of knowledge sees to that (V27 17:46, 33:17).

**What follows from it.** Replace feel with knowledge of the places readers look, and check those places explicitly (V49 3:40; V50 4:24). That is why he teaches mechanical diagnostics:
- underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence (V27 18:19; V32 4:29);
- read only the index positions (the skim test, V46 12:12);
- make a reverse outline (V35 8:11; V47 19:13);
- compare the point sentences of the introduction and the conclusion (V50 4:24);
- circle the value words (V19 54:13);
- colour-code characters and actions (V37 4:53).

It is also why real readers still matter. They are good at sensing *where* friction is, even though they are poor at diagnosing *why* (V47 4:44; V44 23:37).

### 2.7 How familiar advice falls out of the six ideas

| His position | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Value before clarity; fix the introduction first | 2.2 (value), plus the motivation asymmetry |
| No rules, only tools; function, not form | 2.1 (the root) |
| Characters as subjects, old before new, index first | 2.3 (position) |
| Use your readers' code words; concede before you disagree | 2.4 (community) |
| Write to think, then write a second text for the reader | 2.5 (interference) |
| Check locations, not feel; treat feedback as symptoms | 2.6 (you can't read your own text), plus 2.5 |
| Repeat key terms instead of varying them | 2.3 (readers track topics), plus 2.4 (a naming system readers have been trained in, V19 45:45) |
| The passive voice is often right | 2.3 (it moves the right thing into the topic or stress position) |
| Cut late, not early | 2.5 (thinking needs abundance, V23 34:19), plus 2.2 (you can only judge what is "needed" against a settled point) |
| Hedge to your community's norm, backed by evidence | 2.4 (community), plus the anatomy of a claim (V19 1:04:23) |

## 3. One shape at every scale

For anyone implementing the method, this is the most useful structural idea: **the reader draft repeats one shape at every scale.** He calls it **fractal** (V18 23:07; V27 42:44; V46 8:20). Every unit has two parts:
- a short, easily grasped **index** that states the point and names the key terms;
- a longer **discussion** that delivers on it.

| Scale | Beginning: where the reader orients | Middle: what the unit delivers | End: what lingers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sentence | The topic: a short, familiar subject, with the verb soon after (V27 21:35; V37 2:34) | The comment: new, complex material after the verb | The stress position: the new or emphasized item, which the next sentence picks up (V26 4:55; V14 5:51) |
| Paragraph | An index of one to three sentences that states the point and key terms (V46 12:43) | Development that returns to the key terms (V46 13:26), often descending to evidence and climbing back (the uneven U, V50 21:38) | A return to the level of the point, then a push-off to the larger question (V50 27:00) |
| Section | A short opening segment that ends on the point sentence (V27 33:44 to 37:34; V50 15:06) | Paragraphs, ordered by the questions the point raises (V50 13:57) | A transition that rounds up and pushes off (V50 15:50) |
| Document | An introduction that moves from common ground to problem to solution, ending on the point (V28; V50 8:23) | Body sections, each an index plus a discussion (V46 8:43) | A conclusion that returns to the problem, shows it solved, and pushes off to a higher level (V46 16:18 to 19:28) |
| Title or book | An intrigue trigger (V6 5:03; V46 2:16) | Three nouns that are the key terms (V46 2:55); chapter titles as promises (V18 7:12) | n/a |

**Where the point goes: "the end of the beginning".** *(Synthesis)* This resolves an apparent contradiction:
- At sentence scale, the important new item goes at the **end**.
- At paragraph and document scale, the point goes at the **start**.

Both hold because the rule is the same at every scale: the point sits at **the end of the beginning**.
- He uses that phrase for the thesis at the close of the introduction (V50 8:23).
- In a paragraph, he puts the governing idea in the comment of its first sentence and calls that slot the **index position** (V37 7:25).
- In a section, he puts the key concepts toward the end of the point sentence (V27 36:17).

In each case the beginning orients the reader with old, familiar framing; the end of the beginning delivers the point with emphasis; and the discussion pays it off.

**Start where the reader already is.** *(Synthesis)* The same logic runs through the reader's journey at every size. These are one principle:
- old before new, in the sentence (V15 12:00);
- stability before instability, in the introduction (V47 7:16);
- common ground before the problem (V28 4:20);
- in narrative nonfiction: assume the reader doesn't care, and build a bridge from what they do care about (V39 16:37).

**One diagnostic that works everywhere: read only the beginnings.**
- To test a paragraph, underline the sentence openings (V27 18:19).
- To test a section, read only the paragraph indexes.
- To test the whole piece, read only the section indexes (V46 12:12).

If the beginnings alone tell the story, the structure works.

## 4. The stages a piece goes through

The earlier system summary described two phases. The transcripts support a finer picture: three modes of writing, a bridge between them, and several passes in the reader draft. The diagram shows the whole route. Each stage is explained below, with a running example of my own.

```
 (ongoing)  0. CAPTURE + VENTING ....... no reader, no goal
                    |
            1. INQUIRY DRAFT ........... no reader; a question, not a topic
                    |      exit: "the click", you can state your point
            2. BRIDGE .................. reverse outline + reader brief + point sentence
                    |      the reader enters here
            3. BONES ................... problem, point placement, sections, anatomy, U
                    |
            4. FLOW .................... paragraph patterns, topic strings, sentences, presence
                    |
            5. FINISH .................. compression, calibration, emphasis, mechanics
                    |
            6. TEST .................... real readers, feedback tied to locations
```

**Running example (mine).** A finance operations manager at a 400-person company is annoyed that a small expense claim takes over a week to approve, and wants to write something for the company's department heads. All numbers are invented for illustration.

### Stage 0. Capture and venting (ongoing, optional)

- **Purpose.** Keep raw material flowing and let the unconscious do its work.
  - **Daily freewriting** has no goal. It opens a vent for the unconscious. It is not a first draft and is not for anyone, and words like good and bad don't apply (V41 22:53 to 24:26).
  - Later, reread it cold for the small part that has "shine" (V41 27:01).
  - An optional note system sits around it: fleeting, literature and permanent notes, where a literature note is a dialogue with the source rather than a set of highlights (V40 5:53 to 13:50; see also V12 29:53 on collecting scraps).
- **Is the reader present?** No. Not even you, as a judge.
- **Why it is peripheral to the piece but central to the writer.** It feeds pieces without being one. In his later view, block is the unconscious sealed under your self-image and social roles, and the cure is to loosen your grip, not to add discipline (V41 0:00 to 12:09). A skill should support this stage but never treat it as a draft to edit.
- **Example.** In a Tuesday freewrite, the manager rants about a taxi receipt stuck "in approval" for eleven days. A week later, one line has shine: "we built a lock and forgot what it was for."

### Stage 1. The inquiry draft (the artist)

- **Purpose.** Produce the idea by writing.
  - **Start with a question, not a topic.** Put it at the top of the page as a tether (V20 6:12; V35 6:40; V38 3:03; V42 17:29).
  - **Say "I don't know."** Then ask what evidence you would need (V38 3:23).
  - **Explain each piece of evidence to yourself, and let the claim emerge** as evidence plus reasoning. Don't start with a thesis and decorate it; he calls that "writing in reverse" (V38 2:14, 6:40 to 9:31; V17 6:42).
  - **Read when the writing gets hungry**, not before (V35 3:15).
  - **Don't prune.** You can only afford to cut when you are rich in material (V23 34:19).
  - **Reread and restructure as you go**, as the idea takes shape (V23 36:18).
- **Is the reader present?** No. Worrying about readers at this stage produces what he calls the **expert's curse**: you imagine a demanding reader too early and freeze (V20 1:05 to 5:22). If you catch yourself commenting on your own sentences or reaching for backspace, someone else's judgment has crept in (V42 17:29).
- **Exit condition.** "The click": you can say what you now think, and you feel you have the pieces (V11 6:14; V31 20:02; V47 12:03). The idea you end with is usually not the one you started with (V11 6:14 to 6:54).
- **If you skip it**, one of two things happens:
  - you write only what you already knew, which he says is the kind of writing AI can replace (V35 8:11);
  - or you give readers what they want, which he calls audience capture (V38 19:34).
- **Example.** At the top of the page: "Why does a €60 claim take nine days?" The manager pulls the approval logs and finds:
  - most of the wait sits with the third signer, a director who travels;
  - the third signature was added after a fraud case years ago;
  - in three years it has rejected only a handful of claims, and each was already queried at the second signature;
  - along the way, late supplier fees and staff paying out of their own pockets.

  By page four: "the third signature adds delay without adding protection." That is the click.

### Stage 2. The bridge

This is where the reader enters. Three things happen, in this order.

**1. Reverse outline the writer's draft.**
- Label what each part *does*. Lay the labels out on their own and look at the sequence.
- A part you can't label is fluff, or still tangled (V35 8:11 to 9:41; V47 21:09).
- Ideally, the labels will later map onto the reader's questions (V35 9:24).

**2. Write the reader brief**, before any of the reader draft (V30 10:12). It covers:
- **The reader's situation:** who exactly, what brought them to this, what problem they have, and what answer or decision they are after (V24 3:01).
- **The change you want:** what they believe now, where their knowledge stops, and what should be different afterwards (V42 23:44; V25 7:59).
- **The kind of reader:** **uninformed**, **indifferent** or **resistant**. Each needs a different opening (V24 10:50 to 12:20). Also note how aware they are that there is a problem (V27 27:00).
- **The situation as a whole:** purpose, your role, the audience and the context, which he maps with the **rhetorical planning wheel** (V30 7:46). Add one question: what else must they believe for X to be believed (V30 7:08)?
- **Their community's code words** (V19 16:59; V35 17:57).
- **A problem statement** at the top of the page, not for publication: I'm writing about [topic] to answer [question] so the reader can [gain a benefit or avoid a cost] (V12 36:40).

**3. Fix the point sentence.**
- Find the best statement of the point. It is usually near the end of the thinking draft.
- If the draft has something like an introduction and a conclusion, compare their point sentences, then keep the sharper one or combine them (V50 4:24 to 8:23).
- Check that someone could disagree with it (V3 8:00; V25 8:27; V50 17:54).
- Check that it works as "point *because* reason" (V50 17:24).

**If you skip the bridge**, you revise by instinct toward an unnamed reader. Every later choice (which subject, which evidence, which words) has nothing to be judged against.

**Example.**
- **Reverse outline:** annoying receipt / logs / history of the fraud case / rejection data / late fees / audit idea / realization. The point sits in the last label.
- **Reader brief:**
  - Readers: department heads. They are resistant: they believe the third signature is what prevents fraud.
  - Where their knowledge stops: they don't see the waiting time or the late fees.
  - Their problems: budget overruns and staff complaints.
  - What should change: they should come to see the third signature as delay without protection.
  - Their words: "control", "audit trail", "exposure".
- **Point:** "For claims under €500, one approver plus random audits would keep fraud control and remove most of the wait, because the third signature catches nothing the second one misses."

### Stage 3. The bones (the architect, part one)

Open a new document and build the reader's route.
- His most complete procedure for this stage is V50.
- The places it fills are the reader locations in V46.
- The structure, value and reader-psychology phases of V19 supply the rest.

**1. Build the introduction as a problem** (V22 5:23 to 11:17; V27 25:12 to 31:36; V28; V46 7:04). It runs through five moves:
- **Common ground** the reader already accepts, often your thesis turned inside out (V28 6:16; V27 27:52).
- **A concession** of what that view got right (V22 8:14; V27 29:22).
- **A destabilizing condition**, introduced with *but* or *however*. He reserves those words for this turn (V28 11:00; V31 4:34).
- **The cost to this reader**, found by imagining them saying "so what?" (V28 11:00).
- **A preview of your solution**, not its details (V28 12:20).

For a resistant reader, the "but" has to hit the specific belief that blocks your idea, not a side fact (V24 8:15 to 10:27).

Never open with (V19 30:43; V28 0:00; V31 0:52):
- background or definitions;
- the **martini glass**, which funnels from general to specific;
- a hook that promises nothing.

**2. Place the point** at the end of the introduction by default. "Point last" is an advanced option, and it only works if the key terms are previewed early (V50 8:23 to 11:36).

**3. Lay out the sections.**
- Make them the questions your point raises, in the order the reader would ask them. Not the order you discovered things, and not "first, second, third" by default (V50 13:22 to 13:57).
- Two equivalent ways to find that order:
  - organize by problems and subproblems (V19 33:26);
  - plan the piece as a decision tree of the reader's likely responses (V20 22:49).
- Move each section's point into its index (V50 14:49 to 15:06).
- Between sections, write transitions that round up and push off. A question can clear the palate, but only if it is one the reader is already asking (V50 15:50 to 16:32).

**4. Place the objections** where the reader will raise them. Cover the three standard kinds (V19 57:59 to 1:00:28; V3 8:57):
- other causes;
- counterexamples;
- different definitions.

**5. Check each point's anatomy.**
- A point needs a reason, evidence this reader accepts, and a **bridge** (warrant) that says why the evidence supports the reason. Evidence never speaks for itself (V25 8:27 to 11:00; V50 17:24 to 21:04).
- Use research to create tension, not to show that you have read (V19 40:04 to 42:56).

**6. Develop each unit with the uneven U** (V18 23:07 to 25:41; V50 21:38 to 29:31):
- start with an arguable claim (level 4 on his five-level scale);
- explain it;
- prepare the reader for the evidence;
- give the evidence;
- interpret it;
- return to the level of the point;
- push off to the larger question.

Never leave a **hit-and-run quotation**, and never open a point on a bare statistic.

**7. Write the conclusion.** Return to the problem, show it solved, and push off to a broader question, so you end higher than you began. Don't summarize (V46 16:18 to 19:48).

**8. Write the title last among the bones.** It depends on the key terms. Use an intrigue trigger (a question, others know something, a violated expectation, or an open sequence). A reliable format adds three nouns that are the piece's key concepts (V6 2:43 to 5:27; V46 1:04 to 2:55). *(Synthesis: the title is the first thing a reader meets and one of the last things the writer can finalize.)*

**Checks for this stage.**
- The **skim test** of all indexes (V46 12:12).
- A **key-term check**: every important noun in a discussion should link back to its index (V46 13:26 to 15:51).
- **Value words** at the top of every paragraph (V19 51:23 to 54:13).

**Output.** He calls the result of this stage the **bones**: the important parts are in the places readers expect them (V50 35:11).

**Example.**
- **Introduction:** "Since the fraud case, every expense has needed three signatures, and it worked: there's been no repeat. But in three years the third signature has stopped only a handful of claims, each already queried at the second. Meanwhile a typical claim waits nine days, suppliers charge late fees, and junior staff carry company costs on personal cards. For claims under €500, one approver plus random audits keeps the control and removes the wait."
- **Sections**, in the order a department head would ask:
  1. Is the third signature really catching nothing?
  2. What is the wait costing us?
  3. Won't fraud come back? (This section handles other causes, counterexamples, and what "control" means.)
  4. How would audits work?
- **Conclusion** pushes off: which of our controls protect us, and which only feel like protection?
- **Title**, written last: *What the Third Signature Costs: Delay, Late Fees and False Security.* It is a violated expectation plus the three key terms.

### Stage 4. Flow (the architect, part two)

With the bones set, make the reader's passage through paragraphs and sentences smooth.

**Choose a paragraph pattern by purpose** (V48). Each has a typical failure (V48 5:58, 12:20, 17:13, 20:31):

| Purpose | Pattern | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| Characterize one thing | Hold one topic constant | Stagnation |
| Narrate a process or causal chain | Link each topic to the previous comment | Running off the rails |
| Break something down | Introduce the whole, then take its parts | A missing umbrella |
| Weave several strands | Preview themes A, B and C, then take each in turn | Synonyms for the previewed terms |

**Keep a small cast in the topic positions, and repeat it.**
- Don't vary sentence openings for variety's sake (V9 1:55 to 6:01; V26 5:48 to 9:19; V32 5:46).
- If you use more than one name for something, train readers in the names early (V19 45:45).
- Choose the cast by what this reader cares about, using the reader-question test (V33 9:55 to 12:31). Then hold it in place with old before new (V32 8:32).

**Link sentences end to start.** His images are the relay race and sentences shaking hands (V9 9:17; V15 17:20; V26 4:55; V27 11:43). Handoffs alone create cohesion, not coherence. A passage also needs a unified cast and a point (V27 15:42 to 21:11).

**Inside sentences:**
- Make characters the subjects and actions the verbs, and join them with explicit logic words (V8 4:39 to 8:06; V24 18:11 to 21:04).
- Keep the subject short and the verb early, and put complexity after the verb (V27 21:35 to 25:12).
- Subordinate an interpretation with ", which" or an *-ing* phrase (V37 9:04).
- Keep weak words out of the stress position (V37 16:03).
- To put the right thing first or last, use the passive, *since*, or a noun-phrase "suitcase" (V14 4:37 to 7:04; V15 13:05; V32 6:39, 12:05, 19:16).
- Keep a nominalization when it does one of four jobs: it links back to the previous clause, it replaces "the fact that", it sits as an object, or it names a concept these readers know well (V27 5:34 to 11:43).

**Ground abstractions.**
- Use concrete stand-ins, something readers "could stub their toe on" (V22 13:48; V12 10:56).
- Use intuition pumps: scenarios the reader can reason through (V22 28:15).
- Order examples same, same, then different (V12 45:38).

**Presence.**
- Anchor the piece in you and one reader looking at the same thing. Show the problem, then the solution (V30 11:13 to 15:41).
- Replace metadiscourse with the language of looking and moving together ("as we saw", "notice") (V9 15:37).
- Heavy signposting usually signals **private structure**. Replace it with a structure the reader can see on the page or already knows from the genre (V49 10:14 to 13:01).

**Example.**
- *Before:* "Significant delays in reimbursement processing are the result of the requirement for director-level sign-off, and late fees have been incurred by suppliers." The subjects are an abstraction and suppliers, and the actions are buried in nouns.
- *After*, keeping *claims* as the paragraph's cast: "Claims wait longest for the third signature. A claim that clears two approvers in a day then sits for a week in a director's inbox, and while it waits, the supplier's late fee accrues."

### Stage 5. Finish

**Compress.** The aim is less work for the reader, not shorter sentences (V29 2:15 to 20:02):
- cut filler, doublets and redundancy;
- replace phrases with the exact word;
- write in the affirmative, unless the warning is the point.

McCarthy's question, "do you need that?", works from sections down to punctuation (V22 2:24). A sentence's length should follow what it carries, not a rhythm quota (V42 3:31).

**Calibrate certainty** between hedged and strident (V50 30:18 to 35:11; V3 10:07):
- fix the verb, and narrow the subject noun;
- treat "obviously" as a sign of a gap in the argument;
- match your community's norm, and always back confidence with evidence the reader accepts (V19 1:02:00 to 1:05:09).

**Economize emphasis.** *However*, dashes and signposts lose force the more you use them (V34 8:51 to 9:42; V32 17:37).

**Do mechanics last:** grammar, house style and platform conventions (V24 21:04 to 22:56).

**Example:**
- *Strident:* "The third signature is completely useless."
- *Hedged:* "It could perhaps be argued that the third signature may be of somewhat limited value."
- *Calibrated:* "The third signature adds a week and catches nothing the second misses."

### Stage 6. Test with real readers

- **Purpose.** Find where the friction is, since you can't feel it yourself (2.6).
  - Share the draft with a few **early readers** you respect and want respect from (V44 28:53).
  - Watch real readers use the text (V19 1:11:07).
- **Protocol.**
  - **The letter of response.** Readers first state what the piece is trying to do, then what they would steal, and only then what is missing (V44 15:30 to 18:55).
  - **Read twice.** First read as a normal reader and record a gut reaction. Days later, reread analytically and locate exactly where each effect happens (V44 22:00 to 24:38).
  - **The silent author.** The author says nothing while the piece is discussed (V44 25:15).
- **Interpreting what they say.** Treat "unclear" or "dense" as a flag on a location. Then diagnose the cause with the location checks from stages 3 to 5 (V47 6:18 to 8:19).
- **Example.** Two department heads read the draft, and both mark the "Won't fraud come back?" section as defensive. Rereading to locate the feeling shows the cause: four of its six sentences have "you" as the subject. The fix is to put the idea, not the reader, back in the spotlight (V18 27:28 to 32:35).

## 5. What depends on what

These dependencies explain why the stages come in this order. Each row says what a technique needs before it can be applied well.

| Technique | Needs first | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Any judgment in the reader phase | A named reader (2.1) | "Old", "clear", "jargon", "evidence" and "so what" all depend on who is reading (V15 5:16; V47 15:29; V50 18:24; V28 11:00) |
| The point sentence | A finished inquiry draft | The sharpest version of the point usually appears only at the end of the thinking (V50 5:58; V47 2:51) |
| The problem introduction | The point, plus the reader brief | Common ground is often the thesis turned inside out (V28 6:16); the cost has to be pitched at this reader (V28 11:00); the "but" has to target this reader's belief (V24 8:15) |
| Section order | The point, plus the reader's questions | Sections answer the questions the point raises, in the order the reader asks them (V50 13:57) |
| Section and paragraph indexes | Knowing each unit's single point | One paragraph, one message: label it or rework it (V18 22:07; V22 19:00) |
| Key terms | The point sentence | The key concepts are named near the end of the point sentence, then recur (V27 36:17; V46 13:26) |
| The title | The key terms | The title's three nouns are the key concepts (V46 2:55) |
| Handling objections | The point, plus the reader type | Which objections arise depends on the reader (V19 15:13; V20 24:03) |
| The uneven U and the bridge | Each unit's point | You descend from the point and climb back to it (V50 22:13) |
| The topic string (the cast) | The unit's index, plus the reader's interest | Topics link back to the index (V37 7:25), and the cast is whatever this reader cares about (V33 3:04) |
| Old-before-new ordering | The cast, plus the previous sentence | "Old" means whatever the reader is already holding (V15 5:16) |
| Active vs passive | Decisions about the topic and stress positions | Voice is how you get the right element into the right slot (V9 6:41; V15 16:16) |
| Fixing nominalizations | Knowing the characters and how sentences link | Some nominalizations are there to link back (V27 6:33) |
| Removing metadiscourse | A visible structure to replace it | Deleting signposts isn't enough; you have to reorganize (V9 16:45; V49 11:14) |
| Compression | A settled structure and point | "Needed" means needed for the theme and its two or three points (V22 17:32); cutting early starves the thinking (V23 34:19) |
| Calibration | The anatomy of each claim | Strong claims must be earned with evidence, and intensifiers often cover gaps (V19 1:04:23; V50 31:47) |
| Mechanics | Everything above | Polishing text that will move or be cut is wasted (V24 21:58) |
| Using feedback | Knowing the reader locations | Readers report symptoms; you diagnose by location (V47 7:16) |

**When principles collide.** *(Synthesis, from his demonstrations.)* He consistently favours the larger unit and the earlier layer:
- **The passage beats the sentence.**
  - Old before new wins over "put the human character in the subject" when the human is new information (V37 12:26 to 14:29).
  - Keeping the topic string wins over "use the active voice" (V9 6:41 to 9:17; V15 13:05).
- **Value beats clarity.** A clear passage that has no value for the reader gets cut (V19 1:05:58).
- **The reader beats the rule.** "Shorter is better" and "active is better" lose whenever they put the wrong thing in the topic position for this reader (V33 0:00 to 8:08).
- **The community beats generic simplicity.** The reader's own code word beats the plain word when it signals membership and the writer knows the community (V42 13:46).

## 6. Order of application, and why

**The order.** Generate freely and let the structure emerge. Then build from the top down. Then check by location.

```
  generate freely (no order imposed)      <- structure is found, not imposed (V42 16:31)
             \                  /
              \   the click    /
               \              /
                +------------+
                | reader     |             <- top-down from here (V24 22:56)
                | point      |
                | problem    |
                | sections   |
                | key terms  |
                | paragraphs |
                | sentences  |
                | compress,  |
                | calibrate  |
                | mechanics  |
                +------------+
```

His own editing sequence is reader, point, problem, key terms, subjects, connections, mechanics (V24 3:01 to 22:56). V50's "bones, then flow" procedure runs in the same direction (V50 35:11). Five reasons support working top-down, and they reinforce each other:
1. **Dependency.** Each lower decision is defined relative to a higher one (section 5). You can't pick a sentence's subject before you know what the paragraph is about, or the paragraph's point before you know the piece's.
2. **Waste.** Sentence work on text that will later be moved, merged or cut is lost (V24 21:58). Line editing a thinking draft, which he calls futile, is the extreme case (V47 17:30).
3. **Value dominates.** Clear and useless is useless (V19 9:25). No sentence fix makes up for a missing problem.
4. **Motivation softens surface faults, not structural ones.** Motivated readers put up with rough sentences but not with incoherence (V27 32:42). Fix the upper layers, and the lower ones matter less.
5. **Symptoms show up low; causes sit high.** Readers feel "unclear" at the level of the sentence, but the cause is often interference at the level of the route (V35 12:09; V47 7:16).

**Why generation is not top-down.** Imposing an outline before you know what you think blocks you and forces the material into the wrong shape (V42 14:20 to 20:01; V47 20:14).
- Structure should emerge from the material, the way an ant colony's nest does, and then be found and reinforced (V42 16:31, 18:34).
- So the outline comes *after* the first draft, as a reverse outline (V35 8:11).

*(Synthesis)* The overall shape is an hourglass: wide and unordered on the way in, narrow at the point sentence, then ordered from the top down.

**One tool at a time.** He repeatedly warns against applying many techniques at once, because it overwhelms and freezes writers (V12 51:02; V19 1:14:43; V23 40:48). For a skill, that means one targeted pass per issue, starting with the highest layer.

## 7. The minimal core

If a writer adopted only a handful of moves, which would give most of the benefit? He names two 80/20s himself:
- The introduction is the 80/20 of the whole essay (V46 19:48).
- Organizing information within sentences and paragraphs is the 80/20 of clarity (V19 42:56).

Combining those with the root ideas gives a core of five moves. *(Synthesis: the grouping is mine; each move is his.)*
1. **Think first, then build a second text for a named reader.** Write to find the point. Then start the reader version from the reader's position, not from your draft's order (2.5; V47 10:18). Answer his four reader questions first (V42 23:44).
2. **Open with the problem, and end the opening on your point.** Move from common ground, through *but*, to the cost for this reader, to the solution (V28; V27 25:12). Place the point at the end of the introduction (V50 8:23). He calls this his single highest-leverage fix (V46 19:48).
3. **Put the point first in every unit.** Each section and paragraph opens with an index that states its point and key terms. Units come in the order the reader's questions arise. Check with the skim test (V46 8:20 to 12:12; V50 13:57 to 15:06).
4. **Keep a small cast in the topic position, old before new.** Characters as subjects, actions as verbs, repeated rather than varied (V27 0:41 to 21:11; V32; V37).
5. **Check locations, not feel.** Read only the beginnings. Compare the points in the introduction and conclusion. Locate reader friction instead of acting on vague labels (V49 3:40; V50 4:24; V47 6:18).

**Why these five.**
- Moves 1 and 5 answer the writer's two handicaps (2.5 and 2.6).
- Move 2 delivers value (2.2), which conditions how everything else is received.
- Moves 3 and 4 apply the position principle (2.3) at the two scales that matter most: the unit and the sentence.
- Together, they put "the end of the beginning" into practice at every scale.

**The second ring.** These are worth adding once the core is in place:
- the anatomy of a claim, including the bridge (V50 17:24);
- the three standard objections (V19 57:59);
- the uneven U, with no hit-and-run evidence (V50 21:38);
- key terms and the reader's code words (V46 13:26; V35 15:55);
- conclusions that push off (V46 16:18);
- calibration (V50 30:18);
- compression, done late (V29);
- the four paragraph patterns (V48);
- joint attention and persona (V30; V18 26:30);
- the title (V46 1:04).

## 8. What is peripheral or genre-specific

Much of the channel sits outside the core pipeline. It falls into five groups.

### 8.1 Genre modules

These apply the same principles to particular forms and add moves specific to each genre. Use them only when the writer is working in that genre.
- **Academic writing:**
  - the literature review as "evidence of an organizing mind": synthesis over summary, casting studies as major and minor characters, mind map before matrix (V1; V4);
  - personal language in the abstract and introduction but not in methods and results, and present tense for your own findings (V5);
  - the hedging norms of peer-reviewed fields (V3 10:07; V19 1:02:55).
- **Expert books:**
  - choosing a book type by goal (V17);
  - positioning with the Vow, the Victim and the Villain (V16);
  - the purpose chain, and chapter titles as promises (V18 2:38 to 15:26).
- **Creative nonfiction:** the opening image as a seed, webs of related words, research inside the narrative, plant and call back (V21).
- **Narrative science writing:** one driving question, foreground what you witnessed, teach inside the scene, mark time shifts, characters not labels (V39).
- **Personal and impersonal essays:** turn passion outward, the dream test, Zadie Smith's six arrows (V43).
- **Dialogue and scenes:** no author-splaining, strategic ambiguity, ellipsis (V13).

Several genre moves look like exceptions to the core but aren't:
- point last (V50 9:37);
- withholding the thesis until the end of a narrative essay (V21 34:19);
- opening with an image rather than a problem (V21 4:20).

These are controlled variants for narrative forms. They still require previewing the key concepts, so that the ending lands (V50 10:01).

### 8.2 Rhetorical ornament

These make lines memorable or emphatic:
- the figures in V12 (antithesis, diacope, epistrophe, polyptoton, alliteration and others);
- deliberate rule-breaking (V36 9:58);
- embodied metaphor (V36 5:55);
- the three uses of the em dash (V34).

They come after the core works, and they don't make up for its absence. He himself warns against trying many at once (V12 51:02).

### 8.3 Practice regimen

These build skill over months. They are not steps in producing a piece:
- copying admired work from memory (V7 7:00; V12 27:58; V14 13:16);
- collecting sentences, and Great Sentences Mad Libs (V10);
- the joint-attention drill (V30 2:44);
- reading classics (V7 14:36).

### 8.4 Process psychology and infrastructure

This group covers:
- handling block and fear (V11; V23; V41);
- note systems (V40);
- community and feedback formats (V44).

These matter to the person but sit outside the text pipeline, apart from the feedback protocol in stage 6. His advice here also changed the most:
- In 2025 he recommended small, kind, consistent habits (V23).
- In 2026 he called discipline the wrong frame for creative work (V41 1:13, 13:46).
- By mid-2026 he treated block as partly a matter of relationship (V44 30:39).

### 8.5 Stances that constrain a tool rather than the text

His AI position is not a writing technique. It is a boundary:
- **No AI in communicative writing at any stage**, including "cleaning up" your notes, because style and substance can't be separated (V45 7:52 to 11:41).
- **Acceptable uses** are navigating your own notes and handling logistics (V40 15:54 to 22:41; V45 17:18).
- **His test:** could you keep explaining if the reader talked back (V45 9:06 to 11:41)?

For a skill, this becomes: diagnose, question and locate; don't generate the writer's ideas or rewrite their thinking (section 10).

### How to tell core from genre

*(Synthesis.)*
- **Core:** the technique follows from the six ideas, for any reader of pointed nonfiction.
- **Genre-specific:** the technique depends on an expectation that only one form's readers bring, such as reviewers of literature reviews, buyers of business books, or readers of narrative essays.

What a genre's readers bring with them is what he calls **contextual structure** (V49 12:09), and that is exactly where the genre modules live.

## 9. Tensions the architecture resolves

**Why the tensions exist.** Many of them are artefacts of chronology. *(Synthesis)* The channel was built roughly from the bottom up, even though the method is applied from the top down:
- **2024 to early 2025:** sentence mechanics from reader psychology (V2, V6, V8, V9, V14, V15).
- **Mid-2025:** value, the two-draft system and book positioning (V16 to V20), then the editing order and the essay's definition (V24, V25).
- **Late 2025 to early 2026:** introductions, positions, compression and joint attention (V26 to V35).
- **2026:** why writers can't see their own problems, and a procedure based on reader locations (V47 to V50).

His later formulations usually refine rather than reverse, and they are more precise. Where two statements differ, the later one is usually the better guide:
- V47 to V50 on structure;
- V25 on the essay's function;
- V41 and V44 on block;
- V45 on AI.

| Tension | Resolution |
|---|---|
| He rejects templates, yet teaches fixed sequences | The sequences name reader functions and locations, not content. A form is justified only by what it does for the reader (V19 51:23; V20 17:08; V31 22:48) |
| Two separate phases vs "separating writing from revision is backwards" | The separation is between writer and reader patterns, not between drafting and rereading. The thinking phase is itself recursive (V23 36:18; V47 3:55) |
| "Write a new document" vs working from the messy draft | Rebuild the route and reuse the pieces: extract the points and move them, rather than line-editing the old order (V47 17:30; V50 0:00, 15:06) |
| "Write for yourself" | Right for the thinking phase, wrong for the published piece (V38 18:28; V42 20:28 to 20:50) |
| Be bold vs concede and qualify | Boldness belongs to the stance and the thesis; courtesy to how you enter the conversation. How much to hedge follows the community, and all confidence needs evidence (V16 12:21; V19 35:19, 1:02:55; V18 32:35) |
| Personal language and "you" | Presence and ownership help, especially in openings. Making "you" the subject by habit lectures the reader (V5 5:53; V19 1:00:28; V18 27:28) |
| Persuasion vs understanding | The later framing is sharper: an essay's function is understanding, and action follows from it. The inviting, side-by-side tone serves both (V22 22:52; V25 2:24 to 3:18) |
| Readers give poor feedback vs rely on early readers | Readers are good at locating friction and poor at naming its cause (V47 4:44; V44 23:37) |
| Repetition vs synonyms | Synonyms can continue a topic, but never rename a previewed or central key term (V46 13:26; V48 13:14, 20:31) |
| Signposts are boring vs use headings and point sentences | Signals that carry content (key terms in headings, point sentences) help. Empty metadiscourse signals private structure (V27 33:44; V49 10:14) |
| Hooks are bad vs set up expectations | A specific open question that the text pays off is good. An attention-grabber that offers no problem is not (V26 11:54; V28 0:00) |
| Emergent structure vs the six arrows | Use a fixed sequence once you know your position, as a way to build or check. Don't use it as a starting outline (V42 19:27; V43 5:43) |
| "Writing is thinking" | By 2026 he softens it: writing is where thinking becomes visible to you, and some of the thinking happens asleep or in the shower (V42 16:02; V40 7:26; V41 16:41). The practice is unchanged |

## 10. What this architecture implies for a writing skill

This section turns the architecture into design constraints. It is my synthesis.

1. **Diagnose the stage before doing anything.** Is the writer still thinking (stages 0 and 1), at the bridge, or shaping for readers (stages 3 to 5)?
   - Don't line-edit a thinking draft (V38 9:31; V47 17:30).
   - In stages 0 and 1, the skill asks questions (V38 3:23 to 9:31): What question are you answering? What evidence would you need? What is your reasoning? Which part would a critic reject?
2. **Make the reader the gate.** Get the reader brief before judging any passage (V24 3:22). If the writer can't answer the reader questions, that is the first finding.
3. **Work top-down, one layer per pass.** The order is reader, point, problem, sections and indexes, key terms, paragraphs, sentences, compression and calibration, then mechanics (V24 22:56).
   - Report the highest-layer problems first.
   - Don't offer sentence fixes on a piece whose introduction has no problem.
4. **Check locations, not impressions.** Use his mechanical diagnostics and say where in the text each problem sits:
   - the point sentences in the introduction and the conclusion;
   - a skim of the indexes;
   - the first seven or eight words of each sentence;
   - the value words;
   - the stress positions;
   - the last sentence of each paragraph (for hit-and-run evidence).
5. **Diagnose, then offer a choice with its reason.** He never applies a rule blind (V27 6:11; V29 20:02; V32 6:39). The skill should name the reader effect behind every suggestion. For example:
   - a passive stays if it serves the topic string;
   - a nominalization stays if it links back;
   - a negative stays if the warning is the point.
6. **Translate vague complaints.** When the writer reports feedback like "dense" or "unclear", map it to a location and a likely cause using the tables above, rather than shortening sentences (V47 7:16).
7. **Respect his boundary on authorship.** Question, diagnose and locate. Help the writer find their own point and their own words, and don't do their thinking for them (V40 15:54; V45 7:52 to 11:41).
   - Rewriting a sentence to illustrate a principle is consistent with how he teaches.
   - Supplying the argument is not.
8. **Load genre modules only when the genre calls for them** (section 8.1).
