## V31: How Modern Schools Make Terrible Writers (Deliberately)

### 1. Who it is for, and the problem

Academic and professional nonfiction writers (researchers, people building a personal brand or influence in their field) whose writing follows what school taught them and still fails with real readers. The problem: school trained them for one very unusual reader, the teacher, who is paid to read and grades against a rubric. Every habit that serves that reader fails a reader who reads only when they see value (0:00, 1:24, 19:11).

### 2. Core argument

School writing is designed around the teacher's problems (grading at volume, checking what is in the student's head), not the reader's. He names five habits that come from that design: the martini-glass structure, writing as self-expression, obedience to universal rules, the "gap" model of value, and "think first, write later" (a single function of writing). Real readers read only in exchange for value, and value comes from their problems. So expert writing must open with problems framed in the language of instability, must aim to change what the reader thinks, must adapt language to a specific reading community, must show the error in the reader's current model rather than a hole in the literature, and must be produced in two phases: a messy draft for the writer's thinking, then a new document built for the reader (21:37 to 22:48).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Replace the martini glass with a problem-led opening
- **Name:** "martini glass" (his term, 0:52) for the thing to avoid; "language of instability" (his phrase, 5:35) for the fix.
- **What it is:** The martini glass opens wide with background, definitions and sweeping claims, narrows to a thesis, then widens again in the conclusion. The fix is to use the introduction to establish a problem and its solution instead of background and context.
- **Why it works:** Readers trade attention for value, and unless the piece is pure entertainment, value comes from their problems (3:35, 4:34). A background-first opening is built on continuity; it says, roughly, here are ideas that exist, and here is mine too. Readers scanning for value look for signs of disruption and do not find them, so the opening signals stasis (4:34). The martini glass exists because it makes grading easier: a template turns bespoke diagnosis into rubric-checking (2:16).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Delete or move opening sentences that give background, definitions or "since time immemorial" generalities.
  2. Open with something the reader believes or does now, then turn on it with *but / however / by contrast*.
  3. State what the problem costs this reader, or the benefit they are missing.
  4. Preview your solution.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** First paragraph could be pasted into any paper on the topic; the thesis arrives only after a funnel of context; the opening is stitched with *and, furthermore, also, in addition*; no *but/however* before the point; the conclusion re-widens to generalities.
- **When it does not apply:** He grants it genuinely serves one reader, the grader with a rubric (2:16). He also puts fiction written purely for entertainment outside the value-exchange frame (3:35).
- **Example (mine):**
  - Before: "Customer research has long been central to product development. Companies use many methods, and surveys are furthermore among the most common. This paper examines survey practice."
  - After: "Most product teams plan their roadmap from customer surveys. But surveys capture what customers say they will do, not what they do, so teams keep building features nobody opens."
- **Timestamps:** V31 0:52 to 5:35.

#### 3.2 Continuity-versus-instability word check (my label)
- **What it is:** A quick scan of an introduction (or any passage meant to signal value) for the ratio of continuity connectors to instability connectors.
- **Why it works:** He treats the connective words as the surface signal readers use to detect value. Continuity words (*and, furthermore, also*) signal "more of the same"; instability words (*but, however, by contrast*) signal a problem, which is where value lives (4:34).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Highlight every connector in the opening section.
  2. If they are nearly all additive, the opening is probably a background list.
  3. Find the real tension in the material and put it behind a *but* or *however*.
- **Symptoms:** A run of additive connectors; paragraphs that each add a fact without any turn.
- **When it does not apply:** He does not give an exception here, but V32 and V34 warn that instability words lose force when sprinkled everywhere (see "Across").
- **Example (mine):** "Remote work grew. Furthermore, tools improved. Also, managers adapted." becomes "Remote work grew and the tools improved. But most managers still judge output by visible hours, which is why their best remote staff quit first."
- **Timestamp:** V31 4:34 to 5:35.

#### 3.3 Let the shape emerge from function, not a template
- **Name:** "focus on function, not form" (his principle, 22:48).
- **What it is:** Choose the structure of a piece from what this reader needs it to do, not from a generic form.
- **Why it works:** A template imposes an arbitrary shape from the top down; the right shape should emerge from the bottom up, from what the reader, the writer and the ideas need (2:16, 3:35). Different reading communities expect different openings: an analysis of a film scene may be better opened with the scene itself; journalism readers expect a specific incident in the lede and then widening context (3:15, 3:35).
- **How to apply:** Ask the question he gives: what does this piece need to do so that this specific reader reaches their goal? (22:48). Then check what openings the reader's own genre uses (see 3.6).
- **Symptoms:** The structure would be identical whatever the topic or audience; the writer describes the piece by its template ("intro, three body paragraphs, conclusion") rather than by what each part does.
- **When it does not apply:** He is careful that his replacement is not "another template" (9:42).
- **Example (mine):** A post-mortem for engineers opens with the moment the outage began and what the on-call engineer saw, rather than with a paragraph on the history of the service.
- **Timestamps:** V31 2:16 to 3:35, 22:48.

#### 3.4 Start from what the reader thinks, then destabilize it
- **Name:** He frames it as sitting "on the same side of the table" (6:02). (my label: reader-belief frame)
- **What it is:** A three-move pattern for expert nonfiction: (a) here is an idea you hold; (b) here is what is wrong or incomplete about it; (c) here is what that costs you, or the benefit you are missing. The cost can be practical (real pain in the world) or conceptual (the belief stops you from answering a bigger question) (6:02 to 7:00).
- **Why it works:** Readers are not interested in what is in the writer's head; they care about what is in theirs: their beliefs, ideas and problems. School trains writers to display their own understanding because that is what the teacher assesses (6:02). The function of expert writing is to change the reader's ideas, not to express yours (6:02, 22:48).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write down the belief your reader currently holds about the topic.
  2. Write one sentence on what is wrong with it or missing from it.
  3. Write one sentence on the consequence for the reader (practical or conceptual).
  4. Only then introduce your idea as the fix.
- **Symptoms:** The piece opens with "I" or "this paper" and what the writer found; the reader's current view never appears; no consequence for the reader is stated.
- **When it does not apply:** In phase one (writing for yourself), writing about whatever interests you is valid (7:55).
- **Example (mine):** "You probably think a longer onboarding programme produces more loyal hires. It does not: what predicts loyalty is whether the new hire ships something real in week one. Long programmes delay that, so you pay for loyalty you never get."
- **Timestamps:** V31 5:35 to 7:55, 22:21.

#### 3.5 Two phases, two documents
- **Name:** "two functions of writing, two phases" (his framing, 7:55).
- **What it is:** Phase one is writer-focused: write about what interests you to work out your ideas. Phase two is reader-focused: write a completely new draft that reorganizes everything around the idea you found (8:47).
- **Why it works:** Writing is an extension of thinking, not only communication; many writers only learn what they think by writing (8:47). But a thinking draft is organized by the writer's process. No amount of line editing or vocabulary swapping makes that kind of draft valuable to readers (8:47).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Draft freely to find the idea.
  2. When you have something worth sharing, open a new document.
  3. Put ideas in the structural locations where your readers expect to find them (9:42).
- **Symptoms:** A "revision" that only changed words; the key insight appears near the end; the draft follows the order in which the writer thought.
- **When it does not apply:** Staying only in phase one is fine for private thinking; the failure is publishing it (8:47, 10:11).
- **Example (mine):** Your notes on a failed launch run chronologically through six hypotheses and land on "pricing was never the problem, onboarding was." The reader draft opens with that sentence.
- **Timestamps:** V31 7:55 to 10:11, 21:15 to 21:37.

#### 3.6 Genre anthropology and register matching
- **Name:** "almost like an anthropologist" (his phrase, 14:02).
- **What it is:** Study the language patterns your target readers use and share (prominent articles in your field, the publications they read), then reproduce those patterns and adjust your register to fit the reading community and the purpose of the text (9:42, 14:02).
- **Why it works:** Language has two jobs at once: carrying ideas and signalling social information. His story (10:11 to 12:28): his South Korean students imagine meeting a North Korean speaker. There are no grammar errors, yet the dated vocabulary and patterns mark the speaker as outside the group, and the listener gets distracted and pulls away. Readers do the same with text: they use your word choices to judge whether you are in their group, and outsider signals make the message hard to attend to (12:28, 13:10).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Collect a few texts your target readers respect.
  2. Note recurring vocabulary, sentence patterns, where they put the point, how formal they are.
  3. Revise your reader draft to match that register.
- **Symptoms:** The draft is "correct" but sounds like it belongs to another field; readers praise the grammar but do not engage; register mismatches the venue (academic phrasing in a practitioner outlet).
- **When it does not apply:** This is phase-two work. He insists it is not a template (9:42).
- **Example (mine):** For hospital administrators, "the intervention reduced mean inpatient duration" becomes "the change shortened length of stay," because "length of stay" is the phrase they use in their own meetings.
- **Timestamps:** V31 9:42, 10:11 to 14:02.

#### 3.7 Rules into tools
- **Name:** "writing tools" (his term), resting on his principle that there can be no universal rules since there are no universal readers (14:02).
- **What it is:** Treat grammar and usage conventions as a toolkit for adapting a message to specific readers, not as a checklist to satisfy.
- **Why it works:** School rules (don't start with *but*, don't end with a preposition, don't split infinitives, don't start with *hopefully*) were reinforced by years of reward and punishment because teachers needed an objective, defensible rubric (13:10). Real readers are not checking rule compliance; they are checking whether your language looks like theirs (13:10).
- **How to apply:** When tempted to "fix" something because of a rule, ask what effect the construction has on this reader and whether their community uses it.
- **Symptoms:** Contorted sentences to avoid a starting *but*; *however* substituted everywhere; time spent agonizing over comma versus semicolon or en versus em dash (10:11).
- **When it does not apply:** You still need to understand grammar, as tools rather than a straitjacket (14:34).
- **Example (mine):** "However, the pilot failed." rewritten by a rule-follower from "But the pilot failed." Both are fine; choose by the rhythm and register your readers use.
- **Timestamps:** V31 10:11 to 14:34.

#### 3.8 Replace the gap model with the model-error frame
- **Name:** "GAP model" (his term, 14:34), for the thing to avoid.
- **What it is:** The gap model establishes value by surveying a field and pointing to a niche nobody has studied ("although much has been said about X, there is a lacuna..."). The replacement: identify the error in how your specific readers currently think, the framework they use to solve problems, show what is wrong with it and what it costs them, and offer your idea as the solution with its benefits (18:10, 18:37).
- **Why it works:** He contrasts two pictures of knowledge (15:55 to 17:52). If knowledge is a finite puzzle, any new piece is valuable because it is new. If knowledge is an open-ended model the community uses to explain and predict, new information is valuable only when it improves the model: explains an anomaly or improves a prediction. Otherwise it is "just information." Since AI, readers are drowning in information, so novelty alone is worth even less (18:10). His case: a researcher seeking a grant to study newly acquired Cormac McCarthy archives justified it by "nobody has read these yet." That might interest hardcore fans but not most readers, and crucially not funders (15:00).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Name the model, assumption or framework your readers currently rely on.
  2. Find where it fails: an anomaly it cannot explain, or a prediction it gets wrong.
  3. State the cost of that failure to these readers.
  4. Present your work as what repairs the model, and name the benefit of adopting it.
- **Symptoms:** Value claims built on "no one has," "first to," "understudied," "lacuna," "gap in the literature"; the reader's current beliefs never mentioned; the pitch would be just as true of any unstudied topic.
- **When it does not apply:** A tiny audience of enthusiasts may read anything on a topic (15:00); that is not a basis for broader value.
- **Example (mine, hypothetical):**
  - Before: "No study has examined how night-shift nurses use hospital vending machines."
  - After: "Hospitals plan staff nutrition around cafeteria hours, assuming night staff eat at the start of the shift. Our logs show most night-shift eating happens after 2 a.m., when only vending machines are open, which may explain why fatigue complaints cluster on nights despite equal staffing."
- **Timestamps:** V31 14:34 to 18:37.

#### 3.9 Diagnose interference: move the epiphany to the top
- **Name:** "interference" (his term, 18:37, 20:45).
- **What it is:** The clash between the patterns a writer used to think and the patterns a reader uses to read. Fix: start fresh and put the key information where readers expect it; editing means transforming a writer-serving draft into a reader-serving text, not polishing sentences (21:37).
- **Why it works:** Valuable writing means working on a problem you do not yet understand: iterative, messy, until an epiphany "clicks." You produce the idea through the work rather than finding it (20:02). That leaves the value buried at the end. The writer uses the text to think about the world; the reader uses it to learn about the world. Readers do not want to follow blind alleys; they want the value up top (20:45). School never separated these functions because the teacher is a "hostage" reader (19:11, 21:15).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find the sentence where your draft's realization happens (usually late).
  2. Start a new document with that realization near the top.
  3. Rebuild the support in the order the reader needs it.
- **Symptoms:** The strongest claim is in the last paragraph; the draft narrates dead ends; "it turns out that" late in the piece.
- **When it does not apply:** In the thinking draft, the mess "is the point" (20:02).
- **Example (mine):** A memo that walks through three abandoned vendor options before recommending the fourth is rebuilt to open with the recommendation and one reason, then briefly explain why the others fail.
- **Timestamps:** V31 18:37 to 21:37.

#### 3.10 His five closing principles (a checklist)
At 21:37 to 22:48 he condenses the video into five principles, usable as a review checklist:
1. Lead with problems, not background; use tension language, not continuity (21:37).
2. Locate problems within your specific reading community; value is a function of readers. The text should in effect say: here is a problem keeping you from an outcome you want, and here is how my idea solves it (22:21).
3. An idea is valuable only if readers perceive the problem it solves; value does not come from style or credentials (22:21 to 22:48).
4. Function, not form: ask what the piece must do for this reader (22:48).
5. Expert writing changes how readers think; it is not transmitting what you already know (22:48).

### 4. Stances

- **Against:** templates imposed top-down (martini glass); "write about whatever interests you" as advice for published professional nonfiction, which he calls one of the most misguided pieces of writing advice (7:00); universal rules; the gap model; "think first, write later."
- **Self-correction:** He admits he taught some of these habits himself as a professor (0:00). Mid-video he catches himself opening his Korea story with background and apologizes (11:19), a live demonstration of the habit.
- **Nuance:** He keeps the "write for yourself" advice, but only for phase one (7:55). He does not want to lose the idea that writing extends thinking (8:47).
- **Warnings:** Line editing cannot make a thinking draft valuable (8:47); since AI, "new information" is a weaker selling point than ever (18:10).

### 5. Named people and sources
- Cormac McCarthy, as the subject of a researcher's archive grant (15:00); the archive is the Wittliff Collections in Texas.
- No books named. My observation, not his: his "gap model" of a niche in the field resembles the "establishing a niche" move in John Swales's CARS model of research introductions; he does not cite it.

## V32: Watch this if you want to IMPROVE the way you write

### 1. Who it is for, and the problem

Experts whose writing has to be very clear (founders writing books, executives writing white papers, researchers applying for funding) who vary how their sentences begin because they fear sounding repetitive (0:00, 0:23). The problem: that variety scatters the reader's attention and makes prose feel dense and disorganized.

### 2. Core argument

Readers assume a sentence tells the story of whatever appears first. When neighbouring sentences begin with the same small cast of concrete characters or concepts, readers see how the passage hangs together; when openings vary for variety's sake, readers feel pulled in all directions. So the fix for "unclear" prose is strategic repetition: put familiar, concrete characters at the start of each clause, save new and complex information for the end, and let each sentence end on what the next one picks up. The passive voice, *since*, noun-phrase packaging and occasional signposts are the tools that make this possible. He demonstrates by revising one dense historical passage sentence by sentence (2:14 to 24:05). His closing summary: clear writing comes from beginning sentences with old, familiar information (24:26).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Movie-poster test
- **Name:** the movie poster (his framing, 0:23 to 1:14).
- **What it is:** Imagine the passage as a movie and ask who its star is: whose story it tells.
- **Why it works:** Sentences about the same entities tell different stories depending on what comes first. His four versions of one pie-in-the-face event (1:14) show that fronting the person, the victim, the pie, or the nominalized act changes whose story readers think they are in. Readers implicitly assume the sentence is about what shows up first (2:14).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Ask: who or what is this passage really about?
  2. Could you draw that star? If the candidate is an abstraction you cannot picture, look for the real characters hiding in the passage (5:16).
  3. Make those characters the openings of the clauses.
- **Symptoms:** The only candidates for the poster are abstractions ("achievement by merit," "palace revolt"); the actual people or things in the story are buried mid-sentence.
- **When it does not apply:** He does not give an exception in this video; V33 adds that the star should be what the reader cares about.
- **Example (mine):** A paragraph about a hospital's new triage rules whose sentences open with "Implementation," "Reduction," "Compliance" has nurses and patients as its real cast; the poster should show a nurse.
- **Timestamps:** V32 0:23 to 2:14, 5:16.

#### 3.2 Underline the openings (first seven or eight words up to the verb)
- **Name:** (my label: opening-words underline). He describes it as a diagnostic, 4:29.
- **What it is:** Underline the first seven or eight words of every sentence or clause, up to the verb, including any opening phrases and the subject. Read only the underlined parts.
- **Why it works:** Reader attention is trained on the start of each sentence (4:29). When neighbouring openings share a small set of concrete characters, readers get a sense of unity and can "zoom out" to see the global shape (5:46).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Underline each opening up to the main verb.
  2. Check coherence: from the underlined words alone, could someone predict what the passage is about? (4:49)
  3. Check concreteness: could you draw them? (5:16)
  4. If either fails, rewrite so the openings name the passage's recurring characters.
- **Symptoms:** The underlined fragments share nothing; they are mostly abstract nouns; reader feedback says "dense" or "badly organized." His image: the reader feels like a spectator at a tennis match, head snapping back and forth (3:50).
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Example (mine):** Openings "Budget overruns / In Q3, a vendor review / Delays / Staff turnover" share nothing. Rewritten, they read "The project / The project team / Its vendor / The team," and the passage coheres.
- **Timestamps:** V32 3:50 to 5:46.

#### 3.3 Strategic repetition
- **Name:** "strategic repetition" (his term, 13:05, 14:35).
- **What it is:** Repeat key characters and concepts deliberately in the topic position of successive clauses, rather than varying openings.
- **Why it works:** Repetition of the cast is what lets readers perceive a global structure; variety for its own sake breaks it (5:46). He ends by reading the revision and asking whether it sounds repetitive; his point is that it does not, it sounds clear (23:05 to 24:26).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Identify the passage's small cast (2 to 4 characters or concepts).
  2. Start each clause with one of them, or with something that links back to the previous sentence.
  3. Count pronouns (*she, he*), synonyms and related forms as repetition (see 3.11).
- **Symptoms:** A writer's stated fear of "sounding repetitive"; every sentence starting with a different kind of element (date, abstraction, name, participle).
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated; he frames it as what he uses with "pretty much everything I write" (0:23).
- **Example (mine):** "The committee met in May. A proposal to merge the departments was tabled. Opposition came from the faculty." becomes "The committee met in May to debate merging the departments. It tabled the proposal after the faculty objected."
- **Timestamps:** V32 0:23, 5:46, 13:05, 24:26.

#### 3.4 Passive voice as a moving tool
- **Name:** "the passive voice" (his term), used to shift information around the sentence (7:00).
- **What it is:** Use the passive to move a character into the opening of a sentence when that character is on the receiving end of the action.
- **Why it works:** Many teachers say never to use the passive, but it is what lets you put the familiar, recurring character first and push abstract material to the end (6:39 to 7:46).
- **How to apply:** If the character you want to front is the one acted upon, rewrite "X verbed Character" as "Character was verbed by X."
- **Symptoms:** A recurring character stranded at the end of an active sentence whose subject is an abstraction.
- **When it does not apply:** He does not say; the test is whether it puts the right thing up front.
- **Example (mine):** "Rising rents pushed out three of the street's original bakeries." becomes, in a passage about the bakeries, "Three of the street's original bakeries were pushed out by rising rents."
- **Timestamps:** V32 5:46 to 7:46, 11:43, 23:05.

#### 3.5 Concrete and simple first, abstract and complex last
- **Name:** (my label: concrete-to-complex order).
- **What it is:** Begin the sentence with simple, concrete, familiar information; save complex or abstract information for the end.
- **Why it works:** Something easy to grasp at the start gives the reader a foothold, so they can manage complexity later in the sentence (7:46).
- **How to apply:** Where a sentence opens with an abstraction and ends with a concrete character, reverse them (often via passive; see 3.4).
- **Symptoms:** Abstract nominalizations as openings; concrete names parked at the end.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Example (mine):** "Systematic underestimation of maintenance costs characterizes most city bridge budgets" becomes "Most city bridge budgets systematically underestimate maintenance costs."
- **Timestamps:** V32 7:46.

#### 3.6 End on what the next sentence picks up (look ahead)
- **Name:** He describes old-to-new flow and having a concept "lean forward into the next sentence" (8:32, 10:31).
- **What it is:** When revising a sentence, look ahead at the next one. Whatever the next sentence begins with should appear at the end of this one, so sentences link like a chain.
- **Why it works:** The start of each sentence links back to something known, the end introduces what comes next; that is how readers move without losing the thread (8:32, 10:31, 24:26).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Read sentence N+1 and find its opening concept.
  2. Rearrange sentence N so that concept (or a relative of it) is in its final position.
  3. Start sentence N+1 with it.
- **Symptoms:** A new term appears at the start of a sentence with nothing introducing it; the previous sentence ended on something that never recurs.
- **When it does not apply:** When the passage deliberately zooms into an example (see 3.12), the chain breaks and needs a signpost.
- **Example (mine):** "Our returns doubled last quarter. Sizing errors on the website caused most of them. The fix was a new fit guide." Reworked: "Last quarter our returns doubled, mostly because of sizing errors on the website. To fix those errors, we added a fit guide."
- **Timestamps:** V32 8:32 to 10:31, 21:41 to 22:37.

#### 3.7 Order clauses by cause and effect; shrink the effect clause
- **Name:** (my label: sequence order plus -ing reduction).
- **What it is:** Order clauses so the sentence mirrors the real sequence (cause, then effect). Then, if the effect is secondary, reduce it to a nonfinite *-ing* clause so it is lighter and subordinate (9:08 to 10:11).
- **Why it works:** The sentence reflects the actual sequence of events, and reducing the effect clause gives prominence to the cause while keeping the relationship visible and compact (9:42, 10:11).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find clauses in effect-then-cause order ("X happened when Y did").
  2. Swap them.
  3. Turn the effect into "..., making / leaving / forcing ..." if it is subordinate.
- **Symptoms:** "A became B when C did D" with C as the familiar actor stranded at the end.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Example (mine):** "Remote work became company policy in 2021 when the lease on the office was ended by the board." becomes "In 2021 the board ended the office lease, making remote work company policy."
- **Timestamps:** V32 9:08 to 10:31.

#### 3.8 Empty-verb repair
- **Name:** "empty verb" (his term, 10:53, 18:32).
- **What it is:** Verbs like *resulted in* or *occurred* carry no action. When you see one, the real action is hiding in a noun. Find it, restore it to the verb, and make the doer the subject.
- **Why it works:** Verbs should carry action, events performed by characters that readers can picture (10:53).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Flag empty verbs (*resulted in, occurred, took place*).
  2. Ask "who does what?" (18:32).
  3. Make the doer the subject (or, if the familiar character is the one acted upon, use the passive) and the action the verb.
  4. If the empty verb was carrying a causal link, move that meaning into an adverb (he uses *consequently*, 11:43) or a connector.
- **Symptoms:** "resulted in," "led to," "occurred"; nominalizations like *dispute, appointment, termination* sitting in object position.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Example (mine):** "Budget uncertainty resulted in managers regularly postponing hires." becomes "Since the budget was uncertain, managers regularly postponed hires."
- **Timestamps:** V32 10:31 to 11:43, 18:32 to 19:16.

#### 3.9 Noun-phrase suitcases
- **Name:** "suitcases" (his metaphor for noun phrases, 13:05).
- **What it is:** When a sentence ends in a string of prepositional phrases, or a modifier has drifted away from what it modifies, repack the sprawling material into one noun phrase.
- **Why it works:** A single noun phrase makes long information compact and "nimble," reduces the number of nouns competing for prominence, and lets you keep a member next to its category so a modifier attaches correctly (12:05 to 13:05).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Spot chains like "by X before Y-ing, including Z."
  2. Convert the verbal part into a noun phrase ("before the Y of X").
  3. Place any "including..." right after the category it belongs to.
- **Symptoms:** Three or more prepositional phrases in a row; "including" attached to the wrong noun (a dangling modifier).
- **When it does not apply:** He says the awkward version is not wrong, only less than ideal (12:05).
- **Example (mine):** "Reports were not filed by many managers before leaving, including Dana." becomes "Reports were not filed before the departure of many managers, including Dana."
- **Timestamps:** V32 12:05 to 13:05.

#### 3.10 Put the familiar before the new inside a phrase
- **Name:** (my label: possessive reorder).
- **What it is:** When a phrase contains a familiar entity and a new one, and the new one will be the next topic, order the phrase familiar-first, then continue with a relative pronoun.
- **Why it works:** Same old-to-new logic, applied inside a phrase: the familiar character links back, the new one sits at the end ready to become the subject of what follows (15:35 to 16:34).
- **How to apply:** Turn "NewPerson, relation of FamiliarPerson" into "FamiliarPerson's relation, NewPerson, who ..." Use a pronoun for a character the reader already has (*she*) (15:35).
- **Symptoms:** An appositive where the familiar name comes second.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Example (mine):** "The founder stepped down and was replaced by Maria, a protégé of the chair, who cut the budget." becomes "...and was replaced by the chair's protégé, Maria, who cut the budget."
- **Timestamps:** V32 15:35 to 16:34.

#### 3.11 Different words, same concept, still count as repetition
- **Name:** He uses "hyponym" (21:13).
- **What it is:** The topic string can be maintained by related forms (*succession/successor*) and by category-member pairs (his analogy: watermelon is a hyponym of fruit).
- **Why it works:** Readers track concepts, not strings of letters; a member of a category already introduced reads as familiar (10:11, 21:13).
- **How to apply:** When a sentence opens with a new word, check whether it names the same concept as, or a member of a category from, the end of the previous sentence. If so, it links; if not, restructure.
- **Symptoms:** Hard to see, because the writer thinks of the words as related while the reader might not; check that the umbrella term was actually introduced.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Example (mine):** "...the city's transport rules." then "E-scooters were banned from sidewalks in 2022." Here the scooters rule is a member of "transport rules," so the link holds.
- **Timestamps:** V32 10:11 to 10:31, 21:13 to 21:41.

#### 3.12 Signpost the zoom-in; use transitions sparingly
- **Name:** He describes an example as "double-clicking into" a sentence (17:01).
- **What it is:** When the passage leaves its main thread to illustrate it, the topic string breaks. Mark the zoom-in with a signpost like "for example."
- **Why it works:** Clarity is not only about each sentence being interpretable; readers need to see how the sentences hang together at the macro level. A signpost tells them this is an illustration, not a new thread (17:37).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Notice where a key concept drops out of the topic string for a stretch.
  2. If that stretch is an example or elaboration, add "for example" (or similar) at its start.
  3. Keep transitional words rare; reserve them for this job of navigating structure.
- **Symptoms:** A run of specific detail with no marker, after which readers ask "why are we talking about this?"; conversely, transitions on every sentence.
- **When it does not apply:** He warns transitional phrases "can be overused" (17:37).
- **Example (mine):** "Teams that skip retrospectives repeat mistakes. For example, our payments team shipped the same rounding bug three times in 2023."
- **Timestamps:** V32 17:01 to 18:32.

#### 3.13 *Since* versus *because*
- **Name:** (my label).
- **What it is:** Use *since* to state a cause that links back to something already known, so the cause can come first while still reading as old information.
- **Why it works:** He argues that since language rarely has two words with identical function, *since* exists to let a cause-effect relation be stated while marking the causal clause as previously known (20:19).
- **How to apply:** When you want the familiar cause at the start and the new effect at the end, open with "Since..."
- **Symptoms:** "Because" clauses that force familiar material to the end.
- **When it does not apply:** He is explicit that *because* is not wrong here (20:19).
- **Example (mine):** "...the vendor missed the March deadline. Since the parts arrived late, the launch slipped to June."
- **Timestamps:** V32 19:16 to 20:19.

#### 3.14 Translate generic reader feedback into a diagnosis
- **Name:** (my label).
- **What it is:** Treat "unclear," "dense," "badly organized" as descriptions of how the text made readers feel, not instructions for what to change (3:24 to 3:50).
- **Why it works:** Untrained readers can report the experience but not its cause. The writer's job is to find what in the text produced the feeling and create the opposite experience (clarity, understanding, flow) (3:50).
- **How to apply:** On receiving vague feedback, run the diagnostics above (movie poster, opening-words underline, empty verbs).
- **Symptoms:** Revising in response to "unclear" by swapping words or adding explanation.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Timestamps:** V32 3:24 to 3:50.

### 4. Stances
- **Against:** the grade-school advice to vary sentence openings to avoid repetition (0:00, 0:23); the blanket ban on the passive voice (6:39); overuse of transitions (17:37).
- **Hedged:** He repeatedly says a less-good version is "not wrong" and asks viewers not to argue grammar with him (12:05, 20:19). His concern is the reader's experience, not correctness.
- **Warning:** Clarity is also about what you cut (24:26, pointing to another video).

### 5. Named people and sources
- He names none. My observation, not his: the Romanov passage he revises appears to come from Joseph M. Williams's *Style* textbook, where it illustrates topic strings. He does not credit it in this video; worth checking before attributing.

## V33: Writing Online Is Hard, Until Experts Do This

### 1. Who it is for, and the problem

Experts writing online (essays, newsletters, reports, B2B client copy, thought leadership) who follow standard advice ("shorter is better," "use active verbs") and still do not engage readers, while others succeed with worse prose (0:00, 0:53, 4:54). The problem: rules produce writing that is not about what the reader cares about, and the habit of following rules stops writers from thinking about readers at all.

### 2. Core argument

"Bees make honey" is not better than "Honey is made by bees." Readers take the start of a sentence as its topic, what it is about. A reader who cares about honey, handed a sentence about bees, has to translate it into their own terms, which makes it seem less clear, less persuasive and less valuable. So the better sentence is the one whose topic is what this reader cares about, even if it is longer and passive. Rules like "shorter is better" and "avoid passives" fail twice: they produce sentences about the wrong thing, and, worse, they train writers (and AI tools) to assume all readers are the same, until writers become suspicious of thinking about readers at all. Meaning is not in the text; it is in what readers do with it. He adds that none of this belongs in the first draft (14:11).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 The topic position
- **Name:** "topic position" (his term, 1:40).
- **What it is:** The start of the sentence is where readers locate what the sentence is about.
- **Why it works:** Readers do not take a sentence in all at once; they move through it left to right in time, so whatever comes first frames everything after (1:40).
- **How to apply:** For any sentence, identify the topic (the start) and ask whether it is what this reader wants the sentence to be about.
- **Symptoms:** Sentences whose subjects are the writer's product, the writer's source, or an abstraction the reader is only interested in indirectly.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Timestamps:** V33 1:40 to 2:39.

#### 3.2 Choose the subject by what the reader cares about
- **Name:** (my label: reader-interest subject). His examples are the "bees/honey" pair (0:00).
- **What it is:** Put what the reader cares about in the subject position, using a passive verb or a longer sentence if that is what it takes.
- **Why it works:** If the sentence's topic is not the reader's concern, the reader must do extra work to translate it, which lowers clarity, persuasiveness and above all perceived value (3:04). Some readers may not even read on (3:04).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Decide who the reader is and what they care about.
  2. Find that element in the sentence.
  3. Move it to the subject position, using the passive if needed.
- **His worked cases (paraphrased):**
  - B2B founders write "this new feature permits businesses to...". Clients do not care about the feature; they care about their business (echoing "sell benefits, not features"). Passive "businesses are permitted to..." puts them first (4:54, 5:47).
  - A finance newsletter sentence that makes an abstract economic factor the subject, when investors care about the ratio it affects; put the ratio first (5:47, 6:43).
  - A sentence about a CEO's offer to pay employees to leave: readers may care more about the pay or about company loyalty than about the CEO (6:43, 7:18).
- **Symptoms:** "Our product / this feature / this study / [famous person]" as the subject of sentences aimed at readers who care about their own outcomes; the reader has to reframe each sentence to see its relevance.
- **When it does not apply:** Some readers, in some niches, do care about what a named expert did (11:10, 12:10). And if rules are already getting you successful writing, he does not want to meddle (8:08).
- **Example (mine):**
  - Before (to clinic managers): "Our scheduling software reduces no-show rates for clinics."
  - After: "Clinics that use our scheduling software see fewer no-shows," or, passive, "No-show rates are cut by..." if the managers' concern is the rate itself.
- **Timestamps:** V33 2:39 to 8:08.

#### 3.3 The reader-question test
- **Name:** He describes it as thinking "backwards" about a sentence (9:55). (my label: reader-question test)
- **What it is:** Treat your sentence as the answer to a question. Phrase that question in your reader's voice. The subject of their question should be the subject of your sentence (9:55 to 10:48).
- **Why it works:** A reader's question reveals what they care about; matching its subject means the sentence answers them directly, with no translation (10:48, 11:10).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Take a sentence from the draft.
  2. Write the question it answers, as this reader would ask it.
  3. Note the subject of that question.
  4. Rewrite the sentence so that subject is its subject.
  5. If different reader groups would ask different questions, pick the group you are writing for.
- **Symptoms:** The draft's subjects do not match the questions readers would ask; the same sentence would need to serve managers and staff at once.
- **When it does not apply:** He notes readers often do not care who said something even when they want to know what was said (12:10), so do not default to the source as subject.
- **Example (mine):** Sentence: "The city council cut Sunday library hours to save $80,000."
  - Parents ask "When can we still take the kids to the library?" so: "Sunday library hours were cut by the city council to save $80,000."
  - Council watchers ask "What did the council decide?" so keep the original.
- **Timestamps:** V33 9:55 to 12:31.

#### 3.4 Catch the rules habit (the "second danger")
- **Name:** He calls it the second, deeper danger of rules-based advice (12:31).
- **What it is:** A self-check for when a rule (or an AI tool applying one) is doing your thinking about readers for you.
- **Why it works:** Rules give an illusion of certainty (you at least know you followed the rule) and can be automated (8:08). But they assume all readers are the same, and writers trained on them stop thinking about specific readers, even grow suspicious of it (13:04). Writing succeeds with particular readers, not because it follows general rules (14:11).
- **How to apply:** When a suggestion is "make it shorter" or "make it active," ask what this reader wants the sentence to be about before accepting it.
- **Symptoms:** Automatic acceptance of style-checker edits; the writer cannot say who the reader is; resistance to the question "who is this for?"
- **When it does not apply:** If rules are already getting you to successful writing, he says to leave them alone; nothing is broken (8:08).
- **Example (mine):** A grammar tool flags "Late fees are waived for members" as passive and suggests "We waive late fees for members." For a members' newsletter, the original may be better.
- **Timestamps:** V33 8:08 to 9:55, 12:31 to 14:11.

### 4. Stances
- **Against:** "shorter is better" and "active beats passive" as universal rules (0:00 to 0:53); writing mavens who impose rules they do not themselves follow. Mavens have internal "alarm bells" that tell them when a rule makes writing worse; most domain experts lack those alarms, so rules given to them do harm (8:53 to 9:55); AI agents that entrench one-size-fits-all rules (13:04).
- **Philosophy:** Writing is a function, not a thing. Meaning is not in the text the way oxygen is in water; it arises in what readers do with the text: use, process, apply, respond, reject (8:53).
- **Concession:** He will not meddle with someone whose rules already work for them; he meddles only when rules are prescribed to others (8:08, 8:53).
- **Uncertainty:** He says "guess" deliberately; you often cannot know what readers care about, and tolerating that uncertainty is part of the job (8:08).
- **Warning:** Ignore all of this during the first draft (14:11).

### 5. Named people and sources
- "Sell benefits, not features," as the first rule of marketing (4:54), unattributed.
- Ben Thompson, as the model of a finance/strategy newsletter writer (5:47); the example sentence appears to be his own construction.
- Eric Ries (*The Lean Startup*), as author of a passage about Matt Mullenweg's offer to Automattic employees (6:43).

## V34: Afraid of the EM dash?

### 1. Who it is for, and the problem

Nonfiction writers who have stopped using em dashes for fear of being accused of using AI (0:00). The problem: avoiding a legitimate tool makes their writing less clear. A related problem: writers who do use dashes often use them carelessly, which drains their force.

### 2. Core argument

The em dash is one of the most powerful tools a writer has. Avoiding it to escape the visible risk of an AI accusation creates an invisible risk: clogged, ambiguous sentences. Grammatically, the dash has one job: it marks where a statement pauses and resumes, so the material inside does not have to obey the host sentence's grammar. That captures the way people actually think and talk. Its meaning (semantics, as opposed to syntax) is emphasis. Good writers use it for three purposes: examples, asides and mitigations. It is wasted on definitions, which belong in a ", which..." clause, and it loses force when overused, just as *however* does. The dash is only a symptom; the real problem with AI prose is a voice that is confident and hollow at once (12:17).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 The headlights check (visible versus invisible risk)
- **Name:** His analogy (1:42): turning off your headlights to avoid a ticket for a possibly broken one.
- **What it is:** Before removing a construction because it "looks like AI" or might draw criticism, compare that visible risk with the invisible cost to the reader.
- **Why it works:** Writers fear the "grammar police"; to avoid a hypothetical accusation they create real problems that make writing harder to follow (1:42, 2:31).
- **How to apply:** For any self-censored tool, ask what the sentence loses in clarity or emphasis without it, and whether that loss is worse than the accusation.
- **Symptoms:** A draft where every natural interruption has been flattened into commas or split into choppy sentences; writers asking "will this look AI-written?" rather than "does this help the reader?"
- **When it does not apply:** He grants the reader backlash against AI em dashes was a "helpful correction" at first (0:00); it only went too far.
- **Timestamps:** V34 0:00 to 2:31.

#### 3.2 Read punctuation silently
- **Name:** (my label). He asks viewers to take five seconds and read quietly (2:31).
- **What it is:** Judge punctuation by reading silently, not aloud.
- **Why it works:** Reading aloud lets your voice supply the emphasis the punctuation is supposed to supply, hiding the difference (2:31).
- **How to apply:** Read the sentence silently with commas, then with dashes; notice which makes the interruption stand out and the structure visible.
- **Symptoms:** A writer who reads drafts aloud and never notices that their parenthetical clauses are lost on the page.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Timestamps:** V34 2:31 to 4:12.

#### 3.3 Em dashes for emphasized interruptions: examples, asides, mitigation
- **Name:** His three patterns: "exemplification," "aside comment," "mitigation" (6:26, 7:18, 7:45).
- **What it is:** A pair of dashes injects a thought into the middle of a sentence and gives it emphasis. He finds three uses in skilled essayists:
  - **Exemplification:** slip examples into the flow without starting a new clause or sentence (6:26).
  - **Aside:** a slight digression, usually the writer's opinion or a separate train of thought (7:18).
  - **Mitigation:** a hedge that addresses naysayers or counterarguments without derailing the main point (7:45).
- **Why it works:** Grammatically the dash marks the end and resumption of a statement, so what is inside does not have to follow the host sentence's syntax (7:45, 8:09). That mirrors how people think: not in complete sentences, pulled in different directions as thoughts occur (8:09). Semantically, it gives the interrupting material more emphasis than commas do (4:12).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find interruptions inside a sentence.
  2. Classify each: example, aside, mitigation, or something else.
  3. If it is one of the three and deserves emphasis, use a dash pair.
  4. If it is a definition or low-importance, use another device (3.5, 3.6).
- **Symptoms:** An important interrupting phrase set off by commas and lost among other commas ("clogged," 3:06); a list of examples wedged between commas in a sentence that already has commas.
- **When it does not apply:** Definitions (3.5); indiscriminate use (3.7).
- **Examples (mine):**
  - Exemplification: "The three costs of a bad hire—lost time, lost morale, lost clients—rarely show up in a budget."
  - Aside: "Most onboarding programmes—and I have sat through a dozen—confuse information with instruction."
  - Mitigation: "Remote teams can match office productivity—not in every role, but in most desk jobs—if managers measure output."
- **Timestamps:** V34 2:31 to 4:12, 5:35 to 8:51.

#### 3.4 Dashes to show structure and avoid comma splices
- **Name:** He calls the problem "syntactic ambiguity" (4:36).
- **What it is:** When an interruption is a whole clause, commas give either a comma splice or a clunky break, and they give no clue to the sentence's underlying structure. Dashes show where the main statement pauses and resumes.
- **Why it works:** Punctuation should tell the reader how the sentence is built; commas used for several jobs at once hide that (4:36).
- **How to apply:** When a full clause interrupts a sentence, try dashes around it and read silently.
- **Symptoms:** Three clauses strung with commas where the middle one is a comment on the first.
- **When it does not apply:** He notes that online writers may not care about comma splices as errors (4:36); his argument is about emphasis and structure, not correctness.
- **Example (mine):** "Skip the formatting, the designer fixes that, focus on the data." becomes "Skip the formatting—the designer fixes that—and focus on the data."
- **Timestamps:** V34 4:12 to 4:36.

#### 3.5 Definitions go in a ", which" clause
- **Name:** He calls the fix "the non-restrictive relative clause" (10:44).
- **What it is:** Do not use dash pairs to define a term. Use a comma plus *which* (or similar).
- **Why it works:** A definition is not the main focus of the clause, so it belongs in a subordinate position; a ", which" clause puts it there. Dashes should be kept for what you want to emphasize (10:44, 11:48).
- **How to apply:** Find dash pairs that contain a definition or gloss; recast as ", which refers to..." or ", meaning...".
- **Symptoms:** "Term—the definition of the term—" patterns; dashes used as a glossary.
- **When it does not apply:** It is grammatically fine either way; his objection is semantic, that it wastes the dash's emphasis (10:44).
- **Example (mine):** "We use triage—the sorting of patients by urgency—to decide who is seen first." becomes "We use triage, which sorts patients by urgency, to decide who is seen first."
- **Timestamps:** V34 9:42 to 11:48.

#### 3.6 The loudness scale: parentheses, commas, dashes
- **Name:** (my label).
- **What it is:** Three ways to interrupt a sentence, from quietest to loudest: parentheses, commas, em dashes (11:48).
- **Why it works:** Parentheses present a clause gently and unobtrusively; commas are neutral; dashes emphasize without resorting to bold or italics (11:48).
- **How to apply:** Decide how much attention an interruption deserves, then choose the matching device.
- **Symptoms:** All interruptions punctuated the same way regardless of importance; bold or italics used for emphasis that a dash could carry.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Example (mine):** "(see Table 2)" in parentheses; "the second study, run in winter," in commas; "the one result that survived replication—the winter study—" in dashes.
- **Timestamps:** V34 11:48.

#### 3.7 Emphasis economy
- **Name:** (my label). He speaks of "principled" versus "unprincipled usage" and "rhetorical force" (8:51, 9:42).
- **What it is:** Emphasis devices lose force the more often they appear. Reserve dashes for what matters most, and *however* for major turns in the argument.
- **Why it works:** Nothing is grammatically wrong with three *howevers* in a paragraph or dashes on every line, but at the level of meaning, overuse weakens each one (8:51, 9:42).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Count dash pairs and *however/furthermore* per page.
  2. For each, ask whether this is one of the most important moments. If not, downgrade it.
- **Symptoms:** Dashes "all over the place" (he names two prominent essayists as examples, 4:36 to 5:35); undergraduate-style transitions on every sentence (8:51).
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Timestamps:** V34 4:36 to 5:35, 8:51 to 9:42.

### 4. Stances
- **Against:** AI witch-hunts; writers giving up the dash ("the em dash is ours," 0:00); careless, indiscriminate dash use; dashes for definitions.
- **Tone about rules:** He says he is not there to wag a finger or lay down rules, and derives his guidance from patterns in expert writing (5:35). He still states firm preferences.
- **Distinction:** syntax is mechanics; semantics is meaning, and skill comes from understanding the semantics (8:51).
- **On AI:** The dash is only a symptom; the disease is a voice both confident and hollow (12:17). He calls himself "no fan of AI writing" (0:00).

### 5. Named people and sources
- Paul Graham and Marc Andreessen, as overusers of dashes (4:36).
- Maria Popova (Brain Pickings, now The Marginalian), example of exemplification, from a passage on the ship of Theseus (5:35 to 6:26).
- Simon Sinek, example of an aside (6:26).
- Ryan Holiday, example of mitigation (7:18).
- Marc Andreessen (a manifesto passage on accelerationism) and Gwern (on *hikikomori*), examples of definition-in-dashes, which he calls a weak use; he praises Gwern's blog (9:42 to 10:44).

## V35: The Top 1% of Experts Think on Paper, Here's How

### 1. Who it is for, and the problem

Experts and researchers whose writing is slow or bland, who try to stand out with better prompts, outlines or templates, and who are stuck between reading and writing (0:00, 1:43). The problem: treating writing as the record of finished thinking produces derivative work AI can replace, and treating a thinking draft as something to edit produces text readers call unclear.

### 2. Core argument

Valuable expertise answers questions and solves problems that are too complex to work out in your head, so experts use writing to think: write first, understand later. Early drafts are excavation sites, not products; reading feeds the writing when the writing gets hungry, not before it starts. After the thinking draft, a reverse outline shows what each part actually does. Then comes the second step: the route you took to an idea is almost never the route the reader needs ("directions to the restaurant"). Your thinking patterns interfere with the reader's reading patterns, and no amount of line editing fixes that; you have to rebuild the path from where the reader starts. Half of expert writing is knowing the reader, and the W. Brian Arthur / Cormac McCarthy case shows what that means in practice: community code words that signal value to a specific audience.

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Write to think (write first, understand later)
- **Name:** "writing as an extension of your thinking" (0:50); "Write first, understand later" (his line, 4:54).
- **What it is:** Use drafting as the method of solving the problem, not as the record of a solution. In phase one, forget about being good, clear or organized; write badly enough to find the gold on the other side of the draft (3:56, 4:54).
- **Why it works:** Real problems are too complex to hold in your head (0:50). Expert knowledge is like tangled Christmas lights, not a Lego set you assemble mentally; you only find what is connected by untangling (6:15). Putting confusion into the structure of language forces the solution to appear. His evidence: a subscriber's email in which the answer arrived while he typed the question (2:28, 3:56). Waiting until you feel ready is a trap because writing is what makes you ready (3:56).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Stop waiting for readiness or an outline.
  2. Write about the problem directly, messily.
  3. Postpone all concern for style and mechanics until ideas exist (4:54).
- **Symptoms:** Procrastination until "I know what I want to say"; an outline that cannot be filled; bland drafts that restate known ideas; a writer asking for style feedback on material with no clear idea yet (3:56).
- **When it does not apply:** This is phase one only; phase two is separate (see 3.6).
- **Example (mine):** Instead of outlining "Why our churn rose," write "I don't understand why churn rose in March when nothing changed. What did change? Support hours. Pricing page copy. The onboarding email..." and keep going until something connects.
- **Timestamps:** V35 0:50, 3:38 to 6:40.

#### 3.2 The daily question-and-timer brain dump
- **Name:** He calls it a "fleeting note" and "brain dump" (6:40).
- **What it is:** Each morning, open a new note; if you have a problem, write it as a question at the top; set a timer; write freely, letting yourself "dream." Without a question, just see what comes up (6:40).
- **Why it works:** The question gives the dump a direction; the timer lowers stakes; free association surfaces unexpected combinations (6:40, 7:25).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Open a fresh note.
  2. Write your current question at the top.
  3. Set a timer.
  4. Write without editing until it rings.
  5. Return to the question if you lose the thread.
- **Symptoms (of needing it):** No regular thinking practice; ideas only arrive under deadline.
- **When it does not apply:** Not an artifact for readers; you do not hand it over (6:40).
- **Example (mine):** Top line: "Why do our best engineers stop writing design docs after a year?" Then fifteen minutes of unfiltered notes.
- **Timestamps:** V35 6:40.

#### 3.3 Reading is food, not fuel
- **Name:** The formulation came from his subscriber Alexi (2:28, 3:15); Fitzpatrick endorses it.
- **What it is:** Do not read everything before writing, and do not write with no reading. Read when the writing gets hungry: when you hit a wall or find a gap. Guide that reading with your research question (3:15).
- **Why it works:** Reading everything first is like cramming: satisfying for curiosity, unproductive. Writing without reading leaves you "starving" halfway through a draft (1:43, 2:28). Food solves hunger; reading solves the specific gaps writing reveals (3:15). The research question keeps reading from becoming "productive procrastination" (3:15).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Start writing with what you know.
  2. When stuck, name the specific gap.
  3. Read to fill that gap, guided by your question.
  4. Return to writing.
- **Symptoms:** A reading list that grows while the draft does not; or a draft that runs out of material mid-argument.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Example (mine):** Midway through a piece on hospital readmissions you realize you cannot say whether readmission rates differ by discharge day. You stop, find two studies on exactly that, and resume.
- **Timestamps:** V35 0:50 to 3:38.

#### 3.4 Drafts as excavation sites and labs
- **Name:** "excavation sites" and a "science lab" (his images, 6:40, 7:25, 7:50).
- **What it is:** Treat early drafts as places to dig or to combine ideas and see what reacts, not as artifacts of finished thought.
- **Why it works:** Most combinations are duds, but odd combinations, followed by curiosity, are where inspiration comes from (7:25). If you know exactly what you will say before you write, you are only reshuffling known material, which is what AI can replace (8:11).
- **How to apply:** Deliberately bring two ideas or sources into contact in a draft and write about how they relate; keep what reacts.
- **Symptoms:** Drafts that simply transcribe a plan; nothing in the finished piece the writer did not know at the start.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Timestamps:** V35 6:40 to 8:11.

#### 3.5 Reverse outline
- **Name:** "reverse outline" (his term, 8:11).
- **What it is:** A three-step check that brings order to a thinking draft after it exists (8:57 to 9:41).
- **Why it works:** It replaces the pre-planned outline, which suits neither thinking (you do not know the idea yet) nor readers. Labeling reveals what the draft actually does rather than what you meant it to do (9:41).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write the draft (brain dump, ideally timed).
  2. Go back section by section and label what each part actually does. If you cannot label a section, it is fluff or a jumble of ideas (8:57).
  3. Lay the labels out in order and look only at them. Where the flow feels off, you have found something to fix (9:24).
  4. Ideally, the labels should line up with the reader's questions and expectations, so the sequence becomes the map from the reader's current understanding to the new one (9:24, 9:41).
- **Symptoms (the reverse outline reveals):** Sections you cannot label; two sections with the same label; the key claim appearing late; labels in the order of discovery rather than of the reader's questions.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated. He says skipping it means doing only step one of the process (9:41).
- **Example (mine):** Labels from a draft: "company history / define churn / customer call story / CLAIM: churn is an onboarding problem / more history / usage data." Laid out, the problems are visible: the claim sits fourth, history is split in two, the data comes after the claim it should support.
- **Timestamps:** V35 8:11 to 9:41.

#### 3.6 Directions to the restaurant (rebuild the route from the reader's start)
- **Name:** His analogy (9:41 to 10:31); "interference" (his term, 11:09).
- **What it is:** When you give directions by phone, you cannot recite your own route; you start from where the caller is. Likewise, the reader draft must be rebuilt from where the reader starts, step by step, to where you want them (10:31, 13:02).
- **Why it works:** The patterns you use to think (rambling exploration, background, working through your own confusion step by step) interfere with the patterns readers use to process a text (11:09). Experts wrongly assume there is only one route to the idea, their own, and blame "lazy" readers or their own lack of "clarity" (10:31). The issue is not clarity but interference, so line edits (moving commas, swapping words) cannot fix it (12:09, 13:02).
- **How to apply:**
  1. State where the reader starts: what they know and believe.
  2. State where they must end up.
  3. Write a new document that walks from the first to the second, ignoring the order in which you figured things out.
- **Symptoms:** Readers say "unclear," "disorganized," "I just couldn't get it" (12:09); these describe friction between thinking and reading patterns, not the content. The draft follows the writer's chronology.
- **When it does not apply:** The thinking draft is supposed to be a mess (12:09); this is step two only.
- **Example (mine):** You concluded, after months of testing, that nurses' handover notes fail because of the form's layout. Your route: data audit, interviews, a failed training pilot, then the form. The reader, a ward manager, starts from "we need more training," so the reader draft starts there, shows why the pilot did not help, and then reveals the form.
- **Timestamps:** V35 9:41 to 13:27.

#### 3.7 Community code words and the code-word hunt
- **Name:** "code words" (his term, 15:55); "community-specific code words" (16:49).
- **What it is:** Words and phrases that a specific reading community uses and responds to, which signal "this is about your world." Hunt them in the texts the community shares and use them to express your ideas (16:49, 17:57).
- **Why it works:** Value is not set by the writer or even by the ideas; it is in the eye of the reader (15:55, 16:49). The same point needs different language for a startup founder than for a Fortune 500 executive (16:49). Code words act as a secret handshake: they show you understand the readers and speak their language (15:55). His case: W. Brian Arthur, an academic economist writing in the Harvard Business Review for practitioners, revised his 1996 article over four days with Cormac McCarthy. What is instructive is the words they kept. Fitzpatrick contrasts a phrasing he invents for the purpose ("early market entry produces compounding benefits") with the article's plainer, return-focused wording, and lists practitioner words the article uses: strategy, competitive advantage, lock-in (14:50 to 15:55). Some value words work across communities: *but, however, although*, signs of instability that curious readers cannot resist (16:49).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Identify the community (for example, the readers of one publication).
  2. Find their shared cultural artifacts: a journal they all read, an article they have all been sharing (16:49).
  3. Read them like an anthropologist, listing the words that recur and carry value (17:57).
  4. Rewrite your key claims in those terms.
  5. Keep the universal instability words for real turns.
- **Symptoms:** Claims phrased in your discipline's technical terms for readers outside it; a piece "for everybody" (writers agnostic about audience "never quite make it," 17:57); readers who agree but do not care.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated. The hunt is phase-two work.
- **Example (mine):** The same finding for two audiences.
  - Academic: "Response latency is inversely associated with twelve-month subscriber retention."
  - SaaS founders: "Answer support tickets within the hour and customers stay a year longer."
  - Hospital administrators (a different point, same move): "patient flow," "length of stay," "throughput" in place of "processing time."
- **Timestamps:** V35 13:27 to 18:54.

### 4. Stances
- **Against:** better prompts, perfect outlines and proven templates as a way to stand out; they make writing blend in (0:00); think-first-write-later (5:51); waiting until ready (3:56); editing a thinking draft into a reader draft (12:09); writing for everybody (17:57).
- **Reframe:** The school habit of writing the essay first and filling in the outline afterwards, which he did as a student, turns out to be the right strategy at the expert level (5:51, 6:15).
- **On AI:** AI produces homogeneous "slop" (0:00); if you know exactly what you will say before writing, AI can replace that writing; thinking on the page is what AI cannot do (8:11).
- **Emphasis:** Only half of expert writing is knowing your subject; the other half is knowing your reader (13:27). He admits writers resist hearing that they cannot simply edit their thinking draft (12:09).

## Across these five videos

**1. One engine: value is decided by a specific reader.** Every video comes back to this. V31 grounds it in an exchange model: readers trade attention for value, and value comes from their problems (V31 3:35, 4:34). V33 applies it at the smallest scale: even choosing a sentence's subject is a question of what this reader values (V33 3:04, 3:56). V35 applies it to word choice: value is in the eye of the reader, so code words must fit the community (V35 16:49). The philosophical base is stated most directly in V33: meaning is what readers do with a text, not something inside it (V33 8:53). For a skill, this means no revision should be recommended without first establishing the reader.

**2. The two-phase model is the backbone, and it gets fuller.** V31 introduces it twice, as the cure for "self-expression" (7:55) and for "think first, write later" (19:11 to 21:37), and names the clash between the phases "interference." V33 adds a boundary: everything about sentence subjects is reader-side work and should be ignored in the first draft (14:11). V35 fills in both halves: what phase one looks like in practice (daily question-plus-timer dump, reading as food, drafts as excavation sites), the bridge between them (reverse outline), and phase two's governing image (directions to the restaurant). V31 and V35 make the same claim almost word for word: a thinking draft cannot be line-edited into a reader draft; it must be rebuilt (V31 8:47, 21:37; V35 12:09, 13:02).

**3. Reader complaints describe feelings, not causes.** V32 (3:50) and V35 (12:09) both say that "unclear," "dense," "disorganized" are reports of friction. V32 locates the friction at sentence level (scattered topics); V35 locates it at document level (writer's route versus reader's route). A skill should treat such feedback as a trigger to run diagnostics at both levels, not as an instruction.

**4. The topic position has two criteria.** V32's criterion is cohesion: open with familiar, concrete, recurring characters and end on what the next sentence picks up (V32 5:46, 8:32, 24:26). V33's criterion is value: open with what this reader cares about, found by the reader-question test (V33 3:04, 10:48). He does not reconcile them explicitly. My synthesis for a skill: the ideal subject is both familiar and of interest to the reader. Use V33 to pick the passage's cast (who is on the movie poster for this reader), then V32 to keep that cast in topic position and chain old to new. Both videos rehabilitate the passive voice for the same reason: it moves the right thing to the front.

**5. Rules versus tools, applied to three different "rules."** V31 attacks school rules (don't start with *but*, never split infinitives) as grading conveniences that ignore readers' in-group signals (V31 13:10). V33 attacks style-guide rules (shorter is better, avoid passives) and adds the deeper danger that rules, and AI tools that apply them, stop writers thinking about readers (V33 13:04). V34 addresses a new, crowd-enforced rule (avoid the em dash because it looks like AI) with the same reasoning: judge the tool by its effect on the reader (V34 1:42). V34 also shows what he offers instead of rules: patterns found in skilled writers, explained by their function (V34 5:35).

**6. A tension on punctuation, resolved by the rules/tools distinction.** In V31 he says the choice between comma and semicolon, or en and em dash, "doesn't matter" (V31 10:11). Two weeks later V34 argues that a well-placed em dash changes clarity and emphasis considerably. These are compatible: V31 dismisses punctuation as a correctness worry; V34 treats it as a rhetorical tool. V34 even shrugs at comma splices online (V34 4:36). For a skill: do not police punctuation correctness; do suggest punctuation changes when they change emphasis or reveal structure.

**7. Instability words: signal of value, but only if rare.** V31 says *but / however / by contrast* signal value where *and / furthermore / also* signal stasis (V31 4:34). V35 calls *but, however, although* universal value signals (V35 16:49). V32 says transitions are overused and should serve only macro navigation (V32 17:37), and V34 says *however* keeps its force only when reserved for major turns (V34 9:42). The consistent principle is emphasis economy: these words carry value because they mark real turns, and overuse destroys that signal. A skill should check both for their absence in an introduction and their overuse in the body.

**8. Language as social signal, from theory to method.** V31's Korean story gives the theory: language carries in-group signals as well as ideas, and outsider language distracts (V31 12:28). V31 proposes an anthropologist's study of the reader's genre (V31 14:02). V35 turns that into a concrete method, the code-word hunt, with the Arthur/McCarthy article as a case (V35 17:57). Cormac McCarthy appears in both: in V31 as the subject of a gap-model grant, in V35 as the editor who knew the readers.

**9. AI moves from background to main frame.** In V31, AI is one reason novelty is worth less (V31 18:10). In V33, AI is a rule-executing tool that reinforces the illusion that all readers are the same (V33 8:08, 13:04). In V34, AI patterns drive writers into self-censorship, and the real "AI disease" is a confident, hollow voice (V34 12:17). In V35, thinking on the page is presented as the thing AI cannot do, and pre-known content as what it can replace (V35 8:11). The through-line: value comes from new thinking about a specific reader's problem, which is what generic tools and generic rules cannot supply.

**10. Small signs of self-revision.** V31 opens with an admission that he taught some of these habits (V31 0:00) and catches himself opening with background (V31 11:19). V33 concedes that if rules work for a writer he will not interfere, and says "guess" to acknowledge uncertainty about readers (V33 8:08). V34 grants that the backlash against AI dashes began as a helpful correction (V34 0:00). V35 recasts his own school "cheating" (writing first, outlining after) as the expert method (V35 6:15). There is no outright reversal of a position within these five videos.
