## V11: How to Start Writing for Beginners (2025-01-30)

### 1. Who it is for and what problem it addresses

People who want to write (he mentions helping writers publish their first newsletters) but produce little because they judge every sentence as they type it. Their problem: editing *while* drafting. It blocks output, drains the joy out of writing, and makes early ideas look too stupid to keep. Second problem: the blank page, where you wait for inspiration instead of producing it.

### 2. Core argument

The critical eye that makes you a good writer is the same thing that stops you from finishing, when you switch it on during drafting (0:00). Writing and rewriting are two different jobs with two different audiences. Drafting is for you. Rewriting is for the reader. Keep them apart (2:03 to 2:24). Practise timed "non-editing" to get raw words out. Treat half-formed ideas as living things that grow as you write about them: you often start writing about X and finish with a better idea, Y (6:14 to 6:54). Then build a loop. Free generation ("working with words") alternates with zooming out to name the ideas that surfaced ("working with ideas"), so ideas "ferment" together into richer ones (13:47 to 15:07). Only after that do you shape the material for readers. Readers don't care about grammar and organisation. They care whether your ideas solve their problems (15:32 to 15:50).

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 Door closed / door open (separate writing from rewriting)
- **What:** Two distinct processes. Writing is done "with the door closed": you are the only audience, telling yourself the story. Rewriting is done "with the door open": you polish, cut and clarify so someone outside your head can understand. The line he quotes is John Gould's advice to Stephen King: "Write with the door closed. Rewrite with the door open." (2:03)
- **Why it works:** The problem isn't editing. It is editing *at the same time* as producing. That is like having an editor behind you criticising each word before the sentence is done (2:24). He draws on his language-teaching experience: learners improve through comfort and confidence in using the language, not through error correction. Using the language is how they learn to spot and fix their own mistakes (1:05 to 1:36). Writers do the same damaging thing to themselves.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Before each session, decide which mode it is: producing or perfecting (5:13).
  2. In a producing session, fix nothing.
  3. Put editing in its own time slot. He notes many writers draft in the morning and clean up after lunch (4:27).
  4. Once the door is open, switch to the reader's view: cut, clarify, polish.
- **Symptoms in a draft or a process:** Progress one sentence at a time. Deleting almost as much as you write. Rereading the opening again and again before going on. Not finishing. Drafting that feels joyless.
- **When it does not apply:** He is explicit that editing matters and is "a powerful tool if used properly" (0:00, 5:13). The rule is about *when* you edit, not whether.
- **Example:** Before: an analyst spends 90 minutes polishing the first two paragraphs of a board memo and never reaches the recommendation. After: 25 minutes drafting the whole memo badly, door closed. The next day, a separate hour of editing for the board.
- **Timestamps:** V11 2:03 to 2:24, 4:27 to 5:13.

#### 3.2 Non-editing: timed sessions (his term: "non-editing")
- **What:** A short timed burst of continuous writing: no backspacing, no second-guessing, fingers never off the keys. His scale: 2,000 words in two hours is hard, but 250 words in 15 minutes is easy, about one sentence a minute (3:01).
- **Why it works:** The small target makes starting easy. The timer marks the mode, so the inner editor has no licence to speak. It gets ideas out of your head and onto the page, where they can grow (5:30).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Set a timer for 15 minutes, or whatever works for you (3:01).
  2. Type without deleting.
  3. Stuck on a word? Type the first word that comes, add a bracketed note like [find a better word], and keep going (3:32).
  4. When a voice says you need more research, another cup of tea, a clean living room or your taxes done, refuse. While the timer runs, it is writing time (3:32 to 3:49). He plays the refusal for laughs as a slap in the mouth.
  5. Remove distractions. He writes in a plain-text editor, Obsidian, to stay out of the browser. He doubts anyone can write in Google Docs because the browser pulls you everywhere. He also uses a blocker app, Opal, to lock other apps and sites during writing time (3:49 to 4:27).
- **Symptoms:** Reaching for backspace. Switching tabs to check a fact mid-sentence. "Just one more source" before starting. Long pauses hunting for the perfect word.
- **When it does not apply:** It is a production method. What it produces is a mess you "could never share with readers" as-is (12:01).
- **Example:** Before (editing while drafting): "Retention drops because... [deletes] The main reason customers leave is... [opens browser to check the churn number]." After (non-editing): "The main reason customers leave is [better word: timing?]: they quit in week one, maybe [check: 40%?] before setup is done, and the thing is nobody on the team owns week one..."
- **Timestamps:** V11 3:01 to 4:27.

#### 3.3 Spot virtuous procrastination (his phrase)
- **What:** Seeing that editing, and its cousins research and tidying, is often avoidance that feels like diligence.
- **Why it works:** You feel you are doing something good, but it is a way of avoiding the discomfort of writing (5:13 to 5:30).
- **How to apply:** 1) During writing time, notice when you have drifted into fixing or looking things up. 2) Name it. 3) Go back to producing, and leave a placeholder for whatever you wanted to check.
- **Symptoms:** A writing session that ends with a better-formatted document or a longer reading list but no new paragraphs.
- **When it does not apply:** In your scheduled editing time, editing is the job.
- **Example:** A researcher "prepares" to write a discussion section by reformatting all the references. The first paragraph gets written only when the hour is gone.
- **Timestamps:** V11 5:13 to 5:30. (Also 3:32 to 3:49 on the voice that sends you to research.)

#### 3.4 Let ideas grow: X becomes Y
- **What:** "Writing is thinking" (6:14). If you write about anything important, you are thinking thoughts you have never put into words. There is no ready-made handle for them, so they come out sounding stupid, and at first they *are* half-baked (7:51). Writing about them before they are fully formed lets them develop. You start with belief X. While writing you find X is shakier than you thought. You write through the confusion. Then a new idea, Y, clicks. You finish somewhere other than where you started (6:14 to 6:54).
- **Why it works:** Ideas grow in stages, each more complex than the last. He uses the acorn and the tree, and his own awkward teenage self turned professor (5:30, 6:54). Think of fresh ideas as single-celled organisms. Your job is to give them energy, give them room to interact, and stay out of their way (7:51 to 8:12). Without editing getting in the way, connections richer than anything you could have planned appear. You don't need to be a genius, only to show up and help the ideas get where they are going (8:12 to 8:33).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Start a timed session on a belief you hold.
  2. When doubt appears, keep writing, now about the doubt.
  3. When a new idea clicks, follow it and develop it.
  4. Accept ending on a different claim from the one you began with.
- **Symptoms:** Dropping a line of thought because it "sounds dumb". Forcing a draft to stay loyal to its opening thesis. Disappointing work that came from rushing. He says his weak pieces almost always came from rushing and his good ones from letting the process run (6:54).
- **When it does not apply:** He gives no limit. [my inference] It is a drafting-phase stance. The reader-facing version should present Y, not retell the journey from X.
- **Example:** Start: "Code reviews slow teams down." Midway: "Actually, the slow reviews were all on huge pull requests." End: "Review speed is set by the size of the change, not by the review process."
- **Timestamps:** V11 5:30 to 8:33.

#### 3.5 Address yourself as "you"
- **What:** In private drafting, write to yourself in the second person: "You believe X is true" instead of "I believe X is true."
- **Why it works:** For him, the switch lets him step outside himself and watch his own thinking without being attached to the ideas. He picked it up from the diaries of Krishnamurti (captions: "Krishna Mury").
- **How to apply:** 1) Write the belief as "You think...". 2) Question it the way another person would: "Why do you think that? When did you see it?"
- **Symptoms:** Defensive drafting. Being unable to doubt your own position on the page.
- **When it does not apply:** [my inference] It is a thinking device for the private draft, not a style for the published piece.
- **Example:** "You think the pricing page is the problem. But you've only watched three users. What did the other two do before they even reached pricing?"
- **Timestamp:** V11 8:33.

#### 3.6 When a sentence feels wrong, write about why
- **What:** Don't stop when a sentence feels wrong. Write about why it feels wrong, how you noticed, or whatever else you think about it.
- **Why it works:** It breaks down the barrier between your *real* ideas and your *good* ideas, and it frees your voice (9:14). The discomfort is itself material.
- **How to apply:** 1) Leave the wrong sentence where it is. 2) On the next line, write "This feels wrong because..." and keep going.
- **Symptoms:** Freezing on one sentence and rewording it again and again.
- **When it does not apply:** Drafting only.
- **Example:** "Pricing should be simple. That feels wrong because simple for whom? The buyer wants one number. The finance team wants every line item. So maybe the real point is..."
- **Timestamps:** V11 8:33 to 9:14.

#### 3.7 Starter placeholders for openings
- **What:** Don't let beginnings stop you. Start with filler such as "the thing is", "what I'm trying to say is", or "what I'm writing about is". Revise later (9:14).
- **Why it works:** Anything that gets you started is good enough. Openings are revisable.
- **How to apply:** Type one of those phrases and finish the sentence without stopping.
- **Symptoms:** A blank page. Several abandoned first lines.
- **When it does not apply:** [my inference] Delete these crutches in the reader version.
- **Example:** "What I'm trying to say is that our onboarding assumes the buyer is the user, and in most of our accounts they aren't."
- **Timestamp:** V11 9:14.

#### 3.8 Imagine an audience and a deadline
- **What:** When stuck, pretend you are speaking to an audience and have only 30 minutes to get your point across. Set a timer, start anywhere, keep going (10:00).
- **Why it works:** An audience helps. His own evidence: he can stress over a lesson plan all night, then walk into the seminar room and have the class fall into place (9:14 to 10:00). Midway through, waves of clarity often arrive: "that's what I was trying to say."
- **How to apply:** 1) Picture a specific room of listeners. 2) Set a 30-minute limit. 3) Start anywhere and keep talking on the page.
- **Symptoms:** Circling without landing. Drafts that explain nothing to anyone.
- **When it does not apply:** He offers it as one tip among several for when you are stuck.
- **Example:** A data scientist stuck on a methods essay imagines explaining the model to the new product managers at Monday stand-up, with 30 minutes on the clock.
- **Timestamps:** V11 9:14 to 10:00.

#### 3.9 Switch to talking (dictation)
- **What:** If writing feels impossible, talk instead. Speak out loud, as if someone is listening, into voice-to-text software.
- **Why it works:** Speech keeps the stream going. His source is a Tim Ferriss podcast guest, possibly a scientist, whose large output was all dictated. He could not find the episode again (10:00 to 10:41). Such software is now free.
- **How to apply:** 1) Set a timer. 2) Speak as if to a listener. 3) Keep talking. The point is simply to keep going (10:41).
- **Symptoms:** Typing stalls, but you can explain the idea aloud to a colleague with no trouble.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** A surgeon records 12 minutes explaining a new consent process "to a resident" while walking between buildings, then works from the transcript.
- **Timestamps:** V11 10:00 to 10:41.

#### 3.10 Don't force it: listen to the part that resists
- **What:** If you have tried all of the above and it still isn't working, stop. Don't swim upstream. Step away, take a walk or journal, and listen to the part of you that is resisting. That voice is still you and may have something important to say (10:41 to 11:16).
- **Why it works:** Resistance may carry information about the idea or the project.
- **How to apply:** 1) Stop the session. 2) Walk, or journal about the resistance itself. 3) Ask what it is telling you.
- **Symptoms:** Repeated failed sessions on the same piece.
- **When it does not apply:** Don't confuse it with 3.2, where the voice pulling you away mid-timer is to be refused. [my inference] The difference is persistence. Refuse momentary distraction during a timer. Investigate resistance that survives every method.
- **Example:** After three dead sessions on a strategy essay, a walk turns up the real issue: the writer no longer believes the strategy.
- **Timestamps:** V11 10:41 to 11:16.

#### 3.11 Working with words (his term)
- **What:** His name for the messy, iterative, non-judging production stage. You don't judge an idea's quality. You keep the stream of consciousness flowing one word at a time, like a painter who shows up at the studio not knowing what to paint and just plays with the paint (11:16).
- **Why it works:** Inspiration isn't pre-planned. It strikes in the middle of the process. Given energy and freedom, the words throw up patterns you can then recognise. The result is better than anything you could have imagined at the start (11:16 to 12:01).
- **How to apply:** Time-boxed, non-editing production (see 3.2). Judge nothing.
- **Symptoms:** Waiting for the idea before writing.
- **When it does not apply:** It makes "a total mess" you could never share (12:01). You need a second step, 3.12.
- **Example:** Ten minutes of loose writing about why onboarding calls run long turns up a phrase, "customers ask permission questions, not product questions", that becomes the seed of a piece.
- **Timestamps:** V11 11:16 to 12:01.

#### 3.12 Working with ideas, alternating with words ("fermentation"; he jokes about how to "funkify your ideas")
- **What:** A two-mode loop. Words from timed sessions are raw ingredients. Ideas get richer when you bring them together on the page so they interact. He compares this to kimchi, which is not a salad of ingredients but a ferment that becomes more than the sum of its parts (12:44 to 13:30). Even ideas that don't seem to belong together interact: an idea that felt too simple becomes a metaphor for another. An idea that sounded clichéd becomes a lens on another (13:30 to 13:47).
- **Why it works:** Your best ideas come from those interactions, not from lightning strikes (13:47). It also breaks the cycle he describes: you are either writing passionately or passively waiting for inspiration (12:01).
- **How to apply** (13:47 to 15:07):
  1. *Start with words:* set a 10-minute timer and write about anything.
  2. When it ends, zoom out, read it over, and at the bottom write a one-sentence summary of each idea that stands out.
  3. *Or start with an idea:* jot down a thought that intrigues you. Set a timer for 10 to 15 minutes. Let your mind wander while keeping that idea in view, still writing whatever comes.
  4. Repeat, alternating between the modes, harvesting summaries each time.
  5. Over time your body of work builds up and "takes on a life of its own" (15:32).
- **Symptoms:** A blank page. Not knowing what to write about. A folder of unrelated fragments that never connect.
- **When it does not apply:** It generates material. Readers need that material prepared, positioned as a solution to a problem they care about (15:32 to 15:50).
- **Example:** Two summaries from separate sessions: "My grandmother's recipe cards leave out quantities" and "Our runbooks fail when on-call engineers face something new." Together they ferment into "Good runbooks, like good recipe cards, leave room for judgment instead of scripting every step."
- **Timestamps:** V11 12:44 to 15:32.
- **A demonstration inside the video:** at 12:01 he acts out the inner editor. He opens a line with a stock "bolt of lightning" simile, stops to call it a cliché, tries absurd replacements, then restarts. That is a live picture of editing interrupting production.

### 4. His stances

- **Against:** editing while drafting (2:24). Correcting errors as the way to teach (the Montreal French teacher who cut students off mid-sentence, 0:47 to 1:36). Writing inside a browser (4:27). Rushing ideas before they mature (6:54). Writing advice built on formal elements. Readers "don't give a toss" about grammar and organisation (15:50).
- **Warnings:** the voice that says you need more research or chores first (3:32). Editing as virtuous procrastination (5:13). Don't force it when the resistance persists (10:41).
- **Changes of mind:** none stated as a reversal. He frames non-editing as something learned from ten years of teaching academic and professional writing to non-native speakers. They needed confidence, not correction (1:05 to 1:36).

### 5. People and books he names as sources

- Stephen King, and his early newspaper editor John Gould ("door closed / door open") (1:36 to 2:03).
- Krishnamurti's diaries (writing to yourself as "you") (8:33).
- A Tim Ferriss podcast guest, unidentified (dictation) (10:00).

## V12: 20 Years of Writing Advice in 52 mins (2025-02-24)

### 1. Who it is for and what problem it addresses

Any writer, fiction or nonfiction, who has absorbed writing as "a list of rules rather than a set of tools" (0:41). That leaves them stuck with safe, flat, rule-following prose and no repertoire for clarity, emphasis or memorable lines. He frames this as the biggest roadblock he has seen in years of teaching academic and professional writing. He also warns that writing is supposed to feel hard: only amateurs say it's easy (0:00, with a Thomas Mann line to that effect).

### 2. Core argument

Everything in writing is a tool you pick for an effect on a specific reader, never a universal rule. He runs through 31 tools, taken from literature, film, music, rhetoric and composition teaching. They cover several kinds of job: sentence clarity driven by how readers read left to right and hold things in short-term memory; memorable lines built from classical figures of speech; word choice as a *social* signal to a specific reader community; concreteness and trust-building; and, above all, *value*. Writing that is clear, organised and persuasive but solves no problem the reader cares about is useless (35:29 to 36:13). He closes by warning against trying many tools at once. Pick one (51:02).

### 3. Techniques (his 31 tools, in his order)

A map of how they cluster [my grouping]:
- **Sentence clarity:** 1, 18, 19, 29, 30, 31.
- **Word choice as social signal:** 3, 4, 22.
- **Figures for memorable lines:** 2, 5, 6, 8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15.
- **Concreteness and sense:** 7, 11.
- **Structure and reader trust:** 20, 25, 27, 28.
- **Value and conversation:** 10, 21.
- **Tone:** 23.
- **Generating and practice:** 16, 17, 24, 26.

#### Tool 1. The locomotive effect (his term)
- **What:** In a sentence loaded with detail, put the subject and verb right up front. That core clause is the engine, and the details hook on behind it like train cars.
- **Why it works:** We read left to right. If many words separate subject from verb, the reader has to hold the subject in short-term memory until the verb arrives, which is hard work. His contrast, from Ryan Holiday's *Stillness Is the Key*: one sentence full of detail is clear because its first four words say what happens. Another from the same book leaves about 15 words between subject and verb (0:41 to 1:31). Clear writing is easy for the reader.
- **How to apply:** 1) Find the subject and main verb. 2) Count the words between them. 3) If the gap is long, move the modifiers after the verb or into another sentence.
- **Symptoms:** A long subject phrase, or a relative clause, before the verb. You find yourself re-reading to find "who did what".
- **When it does not apply:** He targets sentences "with a ton of details". Short sentences don't need it.
- **Example:** Before: "The committee, which had been meeting since March to review budget requests from all eleven departments and two external partners, approved the plan." After: "The committee approved the plan after reviewing, since March, budget requests from all eleven departments and two external partners."
- **Timestamps:** V12 0:41 to 2:26.

#### Tool 2. Deliberate grammatical "mistake" (captions: "analogy", almost certainly *enallage*)
- **What:** Bending a grammar rule on purpose, for instance an adjective where an adverb belongs or a verb in an odd slot, to make a moment strange. His examples are Cormac McCarthy's odd verb choice at the end of *Suttree* (captions: "Sury") and Dylan Thomas's "go gentle" (captions: "And Thomas").
- **Why it works:** It "wrench[es] readers awake". Amateurs cling to right and wrong and stick to safe language. Experts bend rules to create moments of strangeness (2:45 to 3:46). His framing question: if you mean to make a mistake, is it really a mistake?
- **How to apply:** 1) Find a line that should land hard. 2) Try swapping one word into a grammatically "wrong" but meaningful form. 3) Keep it only if a reader would see it as deliberate.
- **Symptoms fixed:** A key line that is correct but inert.
- **When it does not apply:** He gives no limit. [my inference] It needs the reader to trust you know the rule. Used often, or in a routine report, it reads as error.
- **Example:** "Most startups don't die slow. They die sudden."
- **Timestamps:** V12 2:45 to 3:46.

#### Tool 3. Forget the fifth-grade reading level: use your readers' code words
- **What:** Reject the online advice to write at a fifth-grade level and swap technical terms for plain ones. The advice is attributed to someone the captions call "Gwind", probably the writer Gwern. Language is social. A word carries your relationship to the information and to the reader, not just its content.
- **Why it works:** Following Richard Hanania: "I have a prior" and "I have an assumption" carry the same information. But "prior" comes from Bayesian reasoning and signals membership in a community that values statistics. "Stochastic" likewise frames randomness from a specific discipline (5:04). Writing everything at a fifth-grade level also says something about how you see your readers (5:04).
- **How to apply:** 1) Identify your specific reader community. 2) Collect their in-group terms. 3) Use them where they carry the community's meaning. 4) Don't pick puffy words just to sound smart. That makes you sound like "an ass", in his word (5:57).
- **Symptoms:** Prose that flattens specialist ideas into generic words, so insiders read the writer as an outsider.
- **When it does not apply:** Showy vocabulary chosen for its own sake (5:57). [my inference] Readers outside the community won't know the code.
- **Example:** For clinicians, "the patient decompensated overnight" signals insider status in a way "the patient got worse overnight" does not. For a lay reader, the reverse holds.
- **Timestamps:** V12 3:46 to 5:57.

#### Tool 4. Don't fear $10 words, and don't fear cheap words (a two-parter)
- **What:** Use the precise rare word even if it sends someone to the dictionary. He has been sent there by McCarthy, Joyce and David Foster Wallace and never regretted it. Equally, use blunt or profane everyday language when it resonates, as Mark Manson's bestseller does (7:04 to 7:30).
- **Why it works:** Plain language connects, but always keeping it simple risks underestimating your audience and dulling your own edge. That was Faulkner's side of his famous jab at Hemingway, set against Hemingway's defence of older, simpler words (5:57 to 7:04). The deeper principle: "There are no universal rules for writing because there are no universal readers" (7:30). Writers have specific readers, not general audiences. Using their language signals in-group status (7:54).
- **How to apply:** 1) Ask who your specific reader is. 2) Pick the register, high or low, that "pops" for them. 3) Allow a precise rare word where no common word does the job.
- **Symptoms:** A monotone middle register. Every word safe.
- **When it does not apply:** When the word doesn't resonate with *these* readers.
- **Example:** Precise: "The draft policy is anodyne: it offends no one because it commits to nothing." Cheap: to a founder audience, "The roadmap is a junk drawer."
- **Timestamps:** V12 5:57 to 7:54.

#### Tool 5. Ironic juxtaposition
- **What:** Juxtaposition (placing two things side by side to highlight likeness and difference) combined with irony (the literal meaning splitting from the meaning in context). You place two things together so that each changes how you see the other (9:14). His examples: T. S. Eliot's romantic evening compared to an anaesthetised patient, and the cheerful song at the crucifixion in Monty Python's *Life of Brian* (7:54 to 8:56).
- **Why it works:** It creates tension. The reader is jolted into asking why these belong together.
- **How to apply:** 1) Find the mood or claim you want to crack. 2) Place a concrete detail from the opposite register right next to it.
- **Symptoms fixed:** Scenes or claims with one flat tone.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** "The all-hands opened with a slide celebrating record engagement. The next slide announced the third round of layoffs."
- **Timestamps:** V12 7:54 to 9:14.

#### Tool 6. Alliteration
- **What:** Repeating initial sounds.
- **Why it works:** Following Mark Forsyth (captions: "Mark Forsen"), we naturally love words that start with the same letter. They roll off the tongue, and they make phrases *more persuasive*, even when the meaning is nonsense. Proverbs like "curiosity killed the cat" "sound true" (10:18 to 10:56). His other example is the closing line of *The Great Gatsby*.
- **How to apply:** For a line you want remembered, pick key words that share an opening sound.
- **Symptoms fixed:** A key takeaway that nobody remembers.
- **When it does not apply:** [my inference] Because it makes claims sound truer than they are, keep it for claims you can support. Overuse sounds like a slogan.
- **Example:** "Small, steady, shipped beats big, bold, buried."
- **Timestamps:** V12 9:14 to 10:56.

#### Tool 7. The ladder of abstraction
- **What:** A vertical scale. At the bottom are concrete things you can touch, see or smell, "something you can... stub your toe on". At the top are abstractions like freedom, greatness, the American dream. His model is the Gatsby green light: a tangible light at the end of a dock that becomes a symbol of the American dream (10:56 to 11:52).
- **Why it works:** Writing that stays at the top feels disconnected from reality, "up there in the clouds". Writing that stays at the bottom feels mundane, like journalism, with no larger meaning (11:52 to 12:09).
- **How to apply:** 1) Label each paragraph high or low. 2) Where you have run high, drop to a concrete case. 3) Where you have run low, climb to what it means.
- **Symptoms:** All-abstract prose (vague, preachy) or all-concrete prose (a list of facts with no point).
- **When it does not apply:** He asks for *balance*, not a fixed ratio.
- **Example:** High only: "Healthcare must rebuild trust through relational continuity." Moving on the ladder: "A nurse writes her mobile number on the discharge sheet. That small act of continuity is how trust gets rebuilt."
- **Timestamps:** V12 10:56 to 12:09.

#### Tool 8. Polyptoton
- **What:** Repeating one word in different grammatical forms or parts of speech. His examples come from Jay-Z, the Beatles' "Please Please Me", and Carl Jung's observation that the tortured become torturers (12:09 to 12:59).
- **Why it works:** Repetition in different structural slots makes the reader do a double take, and the line "hangs in the air" (12:59).
- **How to apply:** Take the key word of a claim and reuse it as a different part of speech in the same line.
- **Symptoms fixed:** A closing line with no snap.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** "Writers who won't be edited rarely edit well."
- **Timestamps:** V12 12:09 to 12:59.

#### Tool 9. Antithesis (with the semicolon)
- **What:** To make a line sound deep, don't be original. Take something ordinary and turn it upside down. His formula, shown with Oscar Wilde's one-liners: X is Y; not-X is not-Y (12:59 to 13:51).
- **Why it works:** The sudden contrast is striking. It covers both sides at once, what is true and what is not, so the assertion feels forceful (14:37). The semicolon suits it perfectly because the two halves are two sides of one coin and belong in one sentence, and you can't join two sentences with a comma (13:51 to 14:21).
- **How to apply:** 1) State the familiar claim. 2) Add its inversion. 3) Join them with a semicolon.
- **Symptoms fixed:** A central claim stated only positively, which reads as weak.
- **When it does not apply:** On semicolons generally, many people dislike them because they don't know how to use them, and in many cases you'd be better off not using one. This is the place where one belongs (13:51).
- **Example:** "Meetings are easy to schedule; decisions are not."
- **Timestamps:** V12 12:59 to 14:37.

#### Tool 10. Writing as entering a conversation (the table of researchers)
- **What:** Writing is a social act, even though we do it alone. He cites Darwin: speech is instinctive, writing is not, and because we write alone we forget its social nature (14:37 to 15:05). His scenario for students: researchers have sat at a table for a long time on a hard, shared problem. Each has done a piece of it. You walk in wanting a seat (15:05 to 16:14).
- **Why it works:** The social dynamics decide whether your ideas get a hearing. You can get yourself thrown out regardless of idea quality: ignore their work and present your ideas in a vacuum; elbow in and call them all wrong with no qualification and no admission of your own limits; refuse their insider language, as jargon-averse online advice tells you to (16:14 to 17:33). He credits Paul Millard (captions: "Paul Miller" / "Paul Millard") with the line that writing is entering a conversation, and says David Foster Wallace also saw this as the most important feature of good writing (15:05, 18:09).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Take an interest in prior work and acknowledge it.
  2. Show deference to the field's leading figures.
  3. Reproduce the community's speech patterns to show in-group status.
  4. State disagreements politely, noting where others are right and where your own work has limits.
  5. Present yourself as a helping hand, not an adversary (17:33 to 18:09).
  6. Make sure you are contributing to an ongoing problem.
- **Symptoms:** No reference to what readers already know or believe. "Everyone is wrong" framing. Ideas presented as if from nowhere. Stripped-out community vocabulary.
- **When it does not apply:** He says this applies to any writing context, not only academic. Writing purely for yourself is not really writing, "just journaling" (18:09).
- **Example:** Before: "My onboarding framework replaces every model in use today." After: "The standard activation model rightly focuses on the first session. Where it struggles is with teams whose buyer never logs in. This piece extends it to cover that case."
- **Timestamps:** V12 14:37 to 18:09.

#### Tool 11. Synesthesia
- **What:** Describing one sense in terms of another, or grounding an abstract idea in a sense. His examples: Raymond Chandler describing a smell by how a building looks, and the smell line from *Apocalypse Now* (18:45 to 19:19). Everyday cases include a cold stare, a gravelly voice, the sweet taste of victory (19:58).
- **Why it works:** Smell links strongly to memory and emotion (19:19). He cites David Abram's *The Spell of the Sensuous*: perception is fundamentally multi-sensory, so mixing senses is closer to lived experience, not a trick (19:58 to 21:21).
- **How to apply:** 1) Take an abstract idea. 2) Ask what it would sound, smell, taste or feel like. 3) Blend one or more senses into the description.
- **Symptoms fixed:** Abstract ideas with no sensory anchor.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified. [my inference] The everyday forms are now clichés (see tool 22).
- **Example:** "Technical debt has a sound: the half-second pause before an engineer says 'it depends.'"
- **Timestamps:** V12 18:45 to 21:21.

#### Tool 12. Diacope
- **What:** A word or phrase repeated after a brief interruption, a "verbal sandwich". His examples: the Bond self-introduction, and the fact that people misremember a *Wizard of Oz* line into this shape. Variants: vocative (addressing someone, as in "Drill, baby, drill"), elaboration ("Captain, my captain") and extended ("Romeo, Romeo") (21:48 to 22:50).
- **Why it works:** The repetition "etches the line" into memory. He says the best one-liners use it (22:50).
- **How to apply:** Repeat the key word, put a short interruption in between, then repeat it again.
- **Symptoms fixed:** Lines that should stick but don't.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** "Start small. Really, start small."
- **Timestamps:** V12 21:21 to 22:50.

#### Tool 13. Epistrophe
- **What:** Repeating the same word or phrase at the *end* of successive clauses or sentences. His example is the "I'll be there" speech in Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath* (22:50 to 24:22).
- **Why it works:** Like a song's chorus, it keeps readers on your core message while you keep adding details (24:22).
- **How to apply:** 1) Choose the core message phrase. 2) End each item in a run of details with it.
- **Symptoms fixed:** A list of details that drifts away from the point.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** "When users get lost, it's the design. When support queues overflow, it's the design. When sales keeps apologising, it's the design."
- **Timestamps:** V12 22:50 to 24:22.

#### Tool 14. The ambiguous opening pronoun (captions: "prolapsus", almost certainly *prolepsis*; linguists call a forward-pointing pronoun *cataphora* [my note])
- **What:** Open with a pronoun whose noun hasn't appeared yet. His examples: Philip Larkin's "They... your mum and dad" opening, Hemingway's "He was an old man..." and "It was a dark and stormy night" (24:22 to 25:23).
- **Why it works:** Pronouns normally point back. When one points forward, readers "cannot tolerate that ambiguity". It opens a loop they want to close, which sparks curiosity and launches them into the piece (25:23 to 25:40).
- **How to apply:** Start the first sentence with "They", "He", "She" or "It", and name the referent later.
- **Symptoms fixed:** Throat-clearing openings that name everything up front and raise no question.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified. [my inference] It works best in narrative openings. In reports where readers scan for facts, it can frustrate.
- **Example:** "They told us the migration would take one weekend. It took eleven months."
- **Timestamps:** V12 24:22 to 25:40.

#### Tool 15. Piling up (captions: "conju", almost certainly *congeries*)
- **What:** A rhetorical flood: piling adjectives, nouns and images into an immersive, almost overwhelming experience. His model is the "I have seen..." catalogue that opens David Foster Wallace's cruise-ship essay, "A Supposedly Fun Thing I'll Never Do Again" (25:40 to 27:40).
- **Why it works:** The images are disjointed and surprising, but together they form a mosaic that establishes rich context. After pages of it, you feel you are standing on the deck with the author (27:11).
- **How to apply:** 1) List many specific, sensory, first-hand observations. 2) Use a repeated frame ("I have seen..."). 3) Let the variety build the atmosphere.
- **Symptoms fixed:** A setting or experience summed up in one abstract sentence.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** "I have sat in kickoff calls where no one could name the customer. I have read roadmaps with forty 'P1' items. I have watched a VP present a slide titled 'Synergy' in 48-point type."
- **Timestamps:** V12 25:40 to 27:40.

#### Tool 16. Steal style (his thesis adviser's term)
- **What:** Bring a writer's voice into your own. Find a writer you admire, analyse their sentences and sections, and slot your own ideas into their structures (28:49). The tip came from his thesis adviser, who developed it during a PhD under Walter Benn Michaels, a critic known for a very clear style. The supervisor then praised the adviser's writing without realising they were the supervisor's own sentence patterns (27:58 to 29:15).
- **Why it works:** Style is open to all; ideas belong to their authors (29:15). The more you do it, the more their style seeps into your voice (29:53).
- **How to apply** (29:15 to 29:53):
  1. Ask writer friends which authors they admire, and why.
  2. Pick a couple of their articles or stories.
  3. Find a phrase or passage that stands out.
  4. Read it two or three times.
  5. Close the book and write it out from memory. He stresses this step.
  6. Start with sentences, then work up to paragraphs and whole sections.
- **Symptoms fixed:** A flat voice, or ideas stuck in weak structures.
- **When it does not apply:** It is about style, not ideas (29:15).
- **Example:** A policy writer takes a critic's pattern: a concession, then a sharp reversal, then a one-line verdict. She pours her own argument about zoning into that shape.
- **Timestamps:** V12 27:40 to 29:53.

#### Tool 17. Collecting scraps (his term)
- **What:** Keep small notes of what moves or interests you, with key words that let you recover the feeling later. He points to Kendrick Lamar's description to Rick Rubin, and to Robert Greene, who taught the practice to Ryan Holiday (29:53 to 30:46).
- **Why it works:** It isn't systematic. Scraps pile up, some stay orphans, others cluster round larger ideas. You then notice the through-lines that join them into "constellations", and that is how scraps become your own ideas (30:46 to 31:16).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Capture scraps constantly, with trigger words that bring back the emotion.
  2. Drop them into a store where any note can link to any other. He recommends Obsidian: horizontal linking rather than Notion's nested hierarchy, and local files for privacy and against data loss.
  3. Forget them, then retrieve and link them when something new comes up, weeks or years later (31:16).
- **Symptoms fixed:** Starting every piece from zero.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** A note, "client: 'we don't need a strategy, we need a Tuesday'", sits for months. It later clusters with two other notes about teams mistaking plans for routines, and becomes an essay.
- **Timestamps:** V12 29:53 to 31:16.

#### Tools 18 and 19. Main characters as subjects; specific actions in verbs
- **What:** Two diagnostic questions. *What is this story about?* Its main characters should be the subjects of verbs. *What is happening?* Its main actions should be verbs, not nouns (32:55 to 33:24).
- **Why it works:** Subjects and verbs are the top of a sentence's hierarchy and its "heart". In his tangled example, the characters sit in subordinate slots and the actions are buried in nouns, so it is hard to read (32:10 to 32:55).
- **How to apply:**
  1. List the characters, meaning people or things the story is about.
  2. List the actions.
  3. Rewrite so each clause has a character as subject and an action as verb.
- **Symptoms:** "As the X-ing of Y was taking place on the part of Z". Actions as -ing or -tion nouns. Characters inside "of" or "on the part of" phrases.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated here. V14 and V15 later give cases where the subject should be something else (see "Across" below).
- **Example:** Before: "An evaluation of the vendor's proposal on the part of our team resulted in its rejection." After: "Our team evaluated the vendor's proposal and rejected it."
- **Timestamps:** V12 32:10 to 33:24.

#### Tool 20. The engine of the story
- **What:** One moment in the opening that starts everything, the first domino. His model is the moment in *No Country for Old Men* when the main character finds the case of money (33:24 to 34:43).
- **Why it works:** It immediately raises questions (will he take it, whose is it, will he get away with it) that demand answers and pull the reader through the rest (34:43 to 35:03).
- **How to apply:** Make sure you can point to that one moment in your opening that drives everything after it (35:03).
- **Symptoms:** An opening that sets the scene but raises no question.
- **When it does not apply:** He frames it for stories. [my inference] In nonfiction, the equivalent is the opening incident or anomaly that raises the questions the piece answers.
- **Example:** A case study opens on the finance lead discovering that 40% of revenue comes from a single customer whose contract ends in 60 days.
- **Timestamps:** V12 33:24 to 35:03.

#### Tool 21. Value: write about problems, not topics (the problem statement)
- **What:** Readers don't care about clarity, organisation or persuasion. Those only deliver something. Writing must deliver *value*, and readers perceive value when it solves a problem they care about (35:29 to 36:13). So don't write about topics. An opening like "In this piece, I will explore the topic of..." has already lost the reader (36:40).
- **Why it works:** Topics give no reason to read. Self-expression and opinion are fine, but if they aren't aimed at a problem readers care about, nobody reads them (36:13).
- **How to apply:**
  1. At the top of your page (not for publication), write a problem statement in this shape: I am writing about [topic] because I want to find out [question] so I can help my reader [gain a benefit / avoid a consequence] (36:40).
  2. Use it to keep the piece on track.
  3. Delete anything that doesn't serve it (37:20).
- **Symptoms:** "In this piece I will explore...". Sections that are true and interesting but serve no reader problem. Clear prose that nobody needs.
- **When it does not apply:** The statement is a planning tool and doesn't have to appear in the published piece (37:20).
- **Example:** "I am writing about hospital discharge instructions because I want to find out why patients misread them, so I can help nurse managers cut avoidable readmissions."
- **Timestamps:** V12 35:03 to 37:20.

#### Tool 22. Beware the cliché
- **What:** Avoid stock phrases. His counter-example of a living metaphor is the nickname New York garbage collectors use for maggots (37:40).
- **Why it works:** Following George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language", clichés are "dying metaphors". They have lost their power and are used because they save the trouble of inventing a phrase: a shortcut for creative thought. Bad writing comes from bad thinking and corrodes the culture. Writers have a civic duty to keep language alive, and their role is to spot cultural change and give the culture new images (37:58 to 38:43).
- **How to apply:** 1) Flag phrases you have heard a thousand times. 2) Replace each with a specific fact or a fresh image, or cut it.
- **Symptoms:** "Game changer", "move the needle", "at the end of the day", "low-hanging fruit".
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** Before: "At the end of the day, the new tool was a game changer." After: "The new tool cut contract review from three days to one."
- **Timestamps:** V12 37:40 to 38:43.

#### Tool 23. Understate the serious, overstate the light
- **What:** Match your tone *against* the weight of the subject: restraint for heavy material, exaggeration for light material. He takes Orwell's point that good writing is like a windowpane, which lets readers see the world rather than the writer (38:43). He compares Spielberg's restraint in *Schindler's List* with the explicit brutality of the *Saving Private Ryan* opening. Both are powerful, but he finds restraint more engaging for the imagination (39:24 to 40:20).
- **Why it works:** Restraint makes the audience imagine the worst and fill in the gaps themselves. Let the weight of the story do the work (40:20).
- **How to apply:** 1) With a grave subject, strip intensifiers and state plain facts. 2) With a light subject, you may inflate for comedy.
- **Symptoms:** "Devastating", "catastrophic", "horrific" stacked on a subject that is already grave.
- **When it does not apply:** He grants that the explicit approach also works. It is a choice, not a law.
- **Example:** Before: "The devastating, catastrophic layoffs shattered hundreds of lives." After: "On Friday at 9 a.m., 212 badges stopped working." Light, overstated: "The new espresso machine has, by some measures, saved the company."
- **Timestamps:** V12 38:43 to 40:20.

#### Tool 24. Collect interesting names
- **What:** Keep a list of striking names of people and places and let them suggest stories. His examples are Thomas Pynchon's characters, and a Quebec village near where he grew up whose name has exclamation marks in it (40:37 to 41:39).
- **Why it works:** A name can come before the character or place and "invite you to write their story" (41:39).
- **How to apply:** Write the name down, then ask what it invites you to write.
- **Symptoms fixed:** No starting point.
- **When it does not apply:** [my inference] It is aimed mainly at fiction and narrative. For nonfiction, the equivalents are odd project codenames, place names, or jargon labels that hint at a story.
- **Example:** An internal project codenamed "Lazarus" prompts the question: what died, and who brought it back?
- **Timestamps:** V12 40:37 to 41:39.

#### Tool 25. The Bilbo moment / Bilbo effect (his term)
- **What:** Before you dive into your argument or big idea, build trust with a clear, simple, concrete example or story the reader can "nod along to". Gandalf visits Bilbo and wins a commitment before the adventure starts (41:39 to 42:40).
- **Why it works:** The simple story "opens up the conceptual field" in which the argument unfolds, and sets the frame for how stakes will be read. His examples: Sam Harris's *Waking Up* opens with a teenage camping trip and a trip to Nepal before a demanding argument. Nat Eliason's *Crypto Confidential* opens with learning he would soon be a father, which has nothing to do with crypto but frames the stakes (42:40 to 43:38).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Open with a concrete, accessible story or example.
  2. Only once the reader is on board, bring in abstract ideas.
  3. Order examples from simple to complex, literal to metaphorical (43:38 to 44:07).
- **Symptoms:** Opening with definitions, theory or the abstract claim. Readers who don't know why they should care yet.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified. He says most popular nonfiction does this.
- **Example:** Before: an essay on working-memory limits opens with a definition of cognitive load. After: it opens with "Try holding a new phone number in your head while someone reads you a shopping list", then builds to the theory.
- **Timestamps:** V12 41:39 to 44:07.

#### Tool 26. Rip a solo (after Frank Zappa)
- **What:** Treat a fixed block of time as a live, improvised guitar solo. Zappa said he knows the instrument and has imagination, and when the solo comes he doesn't know what he'll play (44:07 to 45:17).
- **Why it works:** Over-rehearsing makes you play "the same solo every night". Improvising within a time box is raw and alive. You already have the basics (grammar and vocabulary), imagination and taste (45:38).
- **How to apply:** 1) Set a fixed time, say 20 minutes. 2) Take a deep breath and let it rip, unpredictable and raw. 3) Leave the results to nature (45:17 to 45:38).
- **Symptoms fixed:** Overthinking every word before it is written.
- **When it does not apply:** He concedes it won't be perfect. Zappa's solos weren't, and "the best stuff never is" (45:38). It is a drafting mode (compare V11).
- **Example:** A 20-minute session drafting a conference talk's opening with no notes open.
- **Timestamps:** V12 44:07 to 45:38.

#### Tool 27. AA-B, not A-B-A (the comedy "rule of three" applied to examples)
- **What:** When developing an idea with examples, give two similar examples that hammer the point home (AA). Only then give a contrasting example (B) that challenges or deepens it (46:10 to 46:41).
- **Why it works:** With A-B-A (example, contrast, back to the example), the reader never fully grasps the core idea before the contradiction muddies it. A steady AA builds a solid base, which makes the B twist more surprising and more powerful, like a comic's two setup beats before the punchline (45:38 to 46:41).
- **How to apply:** 1) Order examples same, same, then different. 2) Put the complication last.
- **Symptoms:** A paragraph that gives an example, qualifies it at once, then returns to it, leaving the reader unsure of the point.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** Before (A-B-A): "Short onboarding worked at Firm 1. At Firm 2 it failed because the product is complex. Firm 3 also succeeded with it." After (AA-B): "Short onboarding worked at Firm 1 and at Firm 3. At Firm 2, with a far more complex product, it failed. That shows the method depends on complexity."
- **Timestamps:** V12 45:38 to 46:41.

#### Tool 28. Analytical, not descriptive
- **What:** Descriptive writing names parts and repeats facts, as in "this is the minute hand, this is the second hand". Analytical writing finds patterns, unpacks what lies underneath, and uses the information to solve problems readers care about, as in how the gears move the hands and how that matches our experience of time (46:41 to 47:33).
- **Why it works:** "It's not information but analysis" that makes writing valuable (47:33).
- **How to apply:** 1) After listing facts, ask how they connect, why that is, and what it means for the reader's problem. 2) Write that answer.
- **Symptoms:** Paragraphs that inventory. Sentences a reader could get from a spec sheet.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** Before: "The dashboard shows signups, churn and revenue." After: "The dashboard's three lines tell one story: signups rise every month, churn rises faster, so revenue stays flat."
- **Timestamps:** V12 46:41 to 47:33.

#### Tool 29. Beware zombie nouns
- **What:** Action represented as a "lifeless noun" instead of an active verb. It hides who did what, as in "There was worry that there would be extra homework assigned" (47:33 to 48:04).
- **Why it works:** Putting real characters in the driver's seat and actions in verbs lets the reader picture the story. "The best writing is visual" and creates a movie in the mind's eye. How you arrange information in a sentence also tells readers how to interpret it (48:04 to 48:28).
- **How to apply:** 1) Ask who is doing each action. 2) Make that actor the subject. 3) Turn the action noun into a verb.
- **Symptoms:** -tion, -ment and -ence nouns doing the work of verbs. "There was..." or "It was..." openings. Missing actors.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified here.
- **Example:** Before: "There was a decision to implement a reduction in spending." After: "The board decided to cut spending."
- **Timestamps:** V12 47:33 to 48:28.

#### Tool 30. Move long introductory clauses
- **What:** When a sentence opens with a long "since..." or "because..." clause before the main point, move that clause to the end or split it into its own sentence (48:28 to 49:26).
- **Why it works:** Long introductory phrases make readers hold details in short-term memory while they wait for the main point, which slows comprehension. Getting to the heart of the message sooner lowers cognitive load (49:26).
- **How to apply:** 1) Find the main clause. 2) Put it first. 3) Move the reasons after it, or into a second sentence.
- **Symptoms:** Sentences whose first 15 or more words are a subordinate clause.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified. (V12 tool 31 and V15 add that what comes *next* can decide placement.)
- **Example:** Before: "Because most customers never read the terms and regulators increasingly treat that silence as non-consent, firms relying on buried clauses should expect challenges." After: "Firms relying on buried clauses should expect challenges, because most customers never read the terms and regulators increasingly treat that silence as non-consent."
- **Timestamps:** V12 48:28 to 49:26.

#### Tool 31. Don't split verb from object; place the interrupter by what comes next
- **What:** An interrupting clause between a verb and its object turns the sentence into "a mental obstacle course". Move the interrupter to the start or the end. Choose which by looking at what the next sentence begins with (49:26 to 51:02).
- **Why it works:** Readers want to get through the core of the sentence, past the verb and onto its object. The linking rule: if the next sentence picks up the object's idea, end with the object so the two join. If it picks up the interrupter's idea, end with the interrupter (50:23 to 51:02).
- **How to apply:** 1) Spot anything wedged between a verb and its object. 2) Read the start of the next sentence. 3) Put whichever element the next sentence picks up at the *end* of this one.
- **Symptoms:** Commas splitting a verb from its object. Readers losing track of what the verb acts on.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** Before: "We should, if we want to keep our best engineers, fix the on-call rotation." If the next sentence is about the rotation: "If we want to keep our best engineers, we should fix the on-call rotation. The rotation currently..." If it is about the engineers: "We should fix the on-call rotation if we want to keep our best engineers. Those engineers..."
- **Timestamps:** V12 49:26 to 51:02.

#### Closing meta-advice: use one tool at a time
- **What:** While researching the video he wanted to try 15 of these tools in his next piece, then realised that would cause overwhelm and confusion. Focus on one (51:02).
- **Skill implication [my inference]:** In revision, run one targeted pass per issue rather than applying everything at once.

## V13: The Silent Dialogue Hack Expert Writers Use to Hook You (2025-03-29)

### 1. Who it is for and what problem it addresses

Writers of fiction and creative nonfiction. He mentions students in his creative nonfiction workshop, and by extension anyone who writes scenes or reports conversations. Their problem: stiff, over-explained dialogue, where the author keeps stepping in to say what characters feel. That flattens the scene and leaves the reader nothing to do.

### 2. Core argument

Dialogue engages readers when they take part in making the meaning. When authors interrupt to spell out emotions and intentions, which he calls "author-splaining", they act like a friend who keeps pausing a film to explain it (1:21). His contrast is Stephen King's explained dialogue against Cormac McCarthy's bare exchange (2:05 to 5:00). Real people can't see into each other's minds, so an author who claims to know, and puts readers in that position, makes dialogue feel less real (5:00). Remove the explanation. Write lines that can be read more than one way. For the most painful moments, especially shame, go further and leave the words out entirely (ellipsis), so readers feel the weight of what isn't said. He closes with the muted exchange in *There Will Be Blood* and his own reading of it (15:28 to 19:06).

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 Stop author-splaining (his term)
- **What:** Author-splaining is when the author butts into a scene to clarify every emotion or thought behind a character's words. He calls it "one of the cardinal sins of dialogue" (5:00 to 5:54). His comparisons: the subtitles scene in Woody Allen's *Annie Hall*, and a passage from Stephen King's *Carrie* (2:05 to 3:21).
- **Why it works:** "Pure dialogue" invites readers into the meaning-making. It feels more genuine because in real life we don't know what others are thinking (5:00). McCarthy "trusts his readers to connect the dots" and is "nowhere to be found" in his scene (4:43 to 5:00). When you step back, you invite readers forward (6:24).
- **How to apply** (5:54 to 7:18):
  1. The way to avoid it, as with mansplaining, is to assume the other person (the reader) has ideas of their own.
  2. Find adverbs, usually "-ly" words, attached to speech verbs like "said". Delete them. They are a crutch, however helpful you meant them to be.
  3. Find phrases that name emotional states outright. Cut them too; they add commentary on what action and dialogue can show.
  4. Put yourself in the emotion and pick the words, the unsaid words, and the body language that would flow naturally from it.
  5. Trust readers to read the dialogue against its context.
- **Symptoms:** "she said bitterly", "he replied sheepishly", "she felt ashamed", "the question wrote surprise on his face", narrated inner states right after a line of speech.
- **When it does not apply:** He doesn't give an exception. [my inference] In expository nonfiction, stating *your own* claims explicitly is still right. This concerns characters' inner states in scenes and anecdotes.
- **Example:** Before: '"Fine," she said bitterly, feeling betrayed by her cofounder.' After: '"Fine." She closed the laptop before he reached the last slide.'
- **Timestamps:** V13 1:21 to 7:18. (McCarthy's cafe scene, with its long coffee-stirring action beat in place of commentary, is at 3:21 to 4:43.)

#### 3.2 Ask why the text switches to dialogue here
- **What:** A reading and writing check. Dialogue and narrative are two modes with different purposes. Ask why, at this moment, the story breaks from narrative into dialogue (7:18 to 7:51).
- **Why it works:** "Flat dialogue" is really just narrative, a plain description of speech events. Dialogue earns its place when it carries more than one meaning (7:51).
- **How to apply:** For each passage of dialogue, state what it does that narration couldn't. If it only reports information, turn it into narrative or add layers.
- **Symptoms:** Characters trading information the reader needs, with no tension.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** '"The meeting is at three," he said. "I'll be there," she said.' That is flat. It could just be "They met at three."
- **Timestamps:** V13 7:18 to 7:51.

#### 3.3 Write lines with multiple meanings (strategic ambiguity)
- **What:** Dialogue in which each line shifts the meaning of the whole and is open to more than one reading. His model is a short husband-and-wife exchange about the husband going to the pub. Every line can be read several ways (neediness? irritation? a hidden wish to be left alone?). He doesn't name its source (7:51 to 9:45).
- **Why it works:** Leaving room for interpretation makes readers co-creators. They fill in the emotional gaps, and that interpretation becomes "an extension of themselves". It deepens their bond with the story and gives them something to ponder and debate long afterwards (9:45, 10:21 to 10:52).
- **How to apply** (9:45 to 10:52):
  1. Resist spelling out every emotion or intention.
  2. Leave deliberate gaps, or subtle contradictions.
  3. Watch for long passages that reveal too much.
  4. For each line, ask whether different readers could take it differently.
  5. Useful moves from his example: answer a question with a question, hesitate, hedge, press for an answer.
- **Symptoms:** Every line has exactly one possible reading. Characters say exactly what they mean and feel.
- **When it does not apply:** Don't make dialogue "totally vague and vapid" (10:21). Ambiguity must be charged, not empty.
- **Example:** A manager says, "Do you have a minute?" "Is this about the review?" "It can wait." "Then why ask now?" The reader has to decide whether this is a warning, guilt, or an attempted kindness.
- **Timestamps:** V13 7:51 to 10:52.

#### 3.4 Silent dialogue: ellipsis (the video's "silent dialogue hack")
- **What:** Leave the dialogue out on purpose at the most charged moment, and report the scene from outside, "as though the author set up a camera outside the shop window" (13:24), like a silent film. His technical name for it is *ellipsis*, "when you strategically leave something out" (19:06). His examples: V. S. Naipaul's *A House for Mr Biswas*, where a man who can't afford it buys his daughter a doll's house and the transaction is never shown (12:03 to 14:39). And *There Will Be Blood*, where after a humiliating baptism one character says something the audience cannot hear that wipes the smile off the other's face (15:28 to 18:48).
- **Why it works:**
  - In waking life we hide embarrassment and shame rather than announce them (11:44).
  - Leaving the transaction out reads as sympathy, as if the narrative looks away from something too painful to report (14:17).
  - The reader, left outside, has to piece it together, which raises engagement.
  - The strongest emotional impact often comes from what is left unsaid (15:28).
  - He also points to passive constructions in the Naipaul scene ("words were exchanged"; the character is accosted, and the doll's house is placed on his bicycle). They stress the character's lack of control (13:57 to 14:17).
- **How to apply** (15:03):
  1. When a character, or you in a memoir, feels deep embarrassment or vulnerability, experiment with silence.
  2. Describe the scene indirectly, from outside: arrivals, departures, objects, physical movement.
  3. Summarise the speech ("words were exchanged") instead of quoting it.
  4. Consider passive constructions to show someone being moved by events.
  5. Save it for the key moment. In the film it happens once (18:34).
- **Symptoms:** Scenes of shame or humiliation that narrate every painful word and label the feeling. The "hit you over the head" effect he criticises in King (10:52 to 11:44).
- **When it does not apply:** He calls it "only one writing tool" and not enough on its own for masterful dialogue (19:06). He ties it specifically to embarrassment and vulnerability.
- **Example:** Before: '"You're being let go," she said. He felt humiliated and stammered, "But I've been here nine years."' After: "HR asked him to step into the small room. The door was closed. Twelve minutes later he came out carrying a cardboard box that someone had already assembled."
- **Timestamps:** V13 10:52 to 15:28 (Naipaul). 15:28 to 19:06 (*There Will Be Blood*; term "ellipsis" at 19:06).

### 4. His stances

- **Argues against:** author-splaining. Adverbs on speech verbs. Explicit labels for emotional states (5:54 to 6:24). Stephen King's dialogue, though he calls King a master storyteller overall (1:21 to 2:05). Flat dialogue that simply narrates speech (7:51).
- **Warnings:** Ambiguity must not become vague and vapid (10:21). Ellipsis is one tool among many (19:06).
- **Changes of mind:** On his tenth-plus viewing of *There Will Be Blood* he noticed the muted line and formed a new reading of the film and its title, so his view of the film changed (0:49, 18:48 to 19:06).

### 5. People and works he names as sources

Stephen King, *Carrie* (the counter-example). Woody Allen, *Annie Hall* (the subtitles scene). Cormac McCarthy, *All the Pretty Horses*. V. S. Naipaul, *A House for Mr Biswas* (captions "Nightpool"). Paul Thomas Anderson, *There Will Be Blood*. An unnamed film clip about hearing the notes a musician isn't playing (15:03). The husband-and-wife pub dialogue is given without a named source.

## V14: The Biggest Mistake in Nonfiction Writing (2025-05-04)

### 1. Who it is for and what problem it addresses

Writers, including nonfiction experts, who learned grammar and style mainly from Strunk and White's *The Elements of Style*, from school, and from grammar checkers. The "biggest mistake" is rigidly following those rules. It costs writers opportunities, confidence and their own voice (0:00), and leads them to "fix" perfectly good sentences on the basis of false alarms (9:31).

### 2. Core argument

Most of *The Elements of Style*'s style tips are "mostly harmless": empty, obvious or useless (0:57 to 2:09). Its *grammar* advice is actively harmful, and for many English writers it is their only grammar textbook (2:09). The main example is the ban on the passive voice. Strunk and White "stack the deck" with a deliberately awkward example. The awkwardness actually comes from putting something already known into the emphatic end slot, the *stress position*, not from the passive itself (3:03 to 4:37). The passive has at least five legitimate uses (4:37 to 7:04). Strunk and White can't reliably recognise the passive: three of their four examples aren't passive (7:44 to 9:04). They break their own rules in the same book (10:59 to 12:35). Their split-infinitive rule gets emphasis backwards (13:50 to 16:03). That bad advice is now built into grammar checkers and AI (7:44). The fix: see writing as "not a list of rules, but a set of tools" (7:04), learn to identify the passive correctly, and learn style from models by copying from memory (13:16).

He promises "four principles" at the start (0:00). The captions don't list four clearly. What he delivers is: the five uses of the passive, how to identify the passive, copy work, and the stress position (via split infinitives).

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 Five situations for the passive voice
- **What:** The passive is a tool. Use it when:
  1. You want the sentence to *focus on the thing acted upon*. The subject position "is like a spotlight" (4:37 to 5:17).
  2. *The actor doesn't matter* (a couple married on a summer day; whoever officiated is beside the point) (5:17 to 5:51).
  3. *The actor is unknown* ("my wallet was stolen"). Otherwise you need an empty subject like "someone" (5:51).
  4. You want an important word *in the stress position* at the end of the sentence. He compares award shows, which announce that the award "goes to" the winner so the name lands last (5:51 to 6:43).
  5. *It simply sounds better*, as in the proverb that rules exist to be broken, against its clumsy active version (6:43 to 7:04).
- **Why it works:** Sentence positions carry weight. The subject position spotlights. The end position stresses. The passive lets you choose what goes where. His restaurant contrast: "The bill was paid by me" is odd because you, already present, get end-of-sentence emphasis. "The bill was paid for by the last customer" (a pay-it-forward) sounds natural and even elegant, because the new, surprising actor lands in the stress position (3:30 to 4:37).
- **How to apply:** 1) Decide which element the sentence is about (the spotlight) and which deserves emphasis (the end). 2) If the active voice puts them in the wrong slots, try the passive. 3) If the actor is unknown or beside the point, the passive lets you leave it out.
- **Symptoms of misuse or missed use:** Awkward "someone did X" sentences. The known element landing in the emphatic end slot. The focus element buried at the end.
- **When it does not apply / warning:** Politicians love the passive because it lets them talk about mistakes without saying who made them ("mistakes were made", 5:51). He doesn't call that a legitimate use. [my inference] A skill should flag passives that hide responsibility the reader needs to know.
- **Example:**
  - Focus: "The vaccine was developed in eleven months."
  - Actor irrelevant: "The bridge was closed at noon."
  - Actor unknown: "The server was breached overnight."
  - Stress position: "The flaw was found by a first-year intern."
  - Sounds better: "Some promises are made to be quietly forgotten."
- **Timestamps:** V14 3:03 to 7:04.

#### 3.2 Identify the passive correctly (the false-alarm check)
- **What:** Two tests. *Grammar:* a form of "to be" plus a past participle ("was eaten", "were built", "is chosen"). *Meaning:* the receiver of the action comes first, and the doer is left out or placed after the verb in a "by" phrase (9:57).
- **Why it works:** Many sentences that feel passive aren't. Of Strunk and White's four "passive" examples, only the one where something "could be heard" is passive. The others are an existential "there were..." sentence, a plain past-tense sentence, and a sentence where the participle-looking word is an adjective after "became". "Became" isn't transitive, so it can't go into the passive (8:25 to 9:04). Misidentifying the passive leads to pointless edits, confusion and weaker writing decisions (9:04 to 9:31).
- **How to apply:** 1) When a checker or reviewer flags "passive", check for be + past participle. 2) Check whether the receiver of an action is in the subject slot. 3) If both fail, it's a false alarm; leave it. 4) If it really is passive, decide using 3.1. He suggests pausing to ask whether you're really changing a passive or chasing another false alarm (9:57).
- **Symptoms:** Rewriting "there were..." sentences, linking-verb sentences or past-perfect sentences because a tool called them passive.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** Passive: "The file was deleted by the script." Not passive: "There were errors in the file." "She had left early." "The office became quiet."
- **Timestamps:** V14 7:44 to 10:59.

#### 3.3 Copy work (from memory)
- **What:** A copywriting practice. Take an effective text and copy it out word for word until you understand its structure and rhythm. It works for any genre (13:16).
- **Why it works:** There are "no secrets" in style manuals that will improve your writing (12:35 to 13:16). You learn from examples you admire. He believes working from memory has more effect than copying with the book open (13:16 to 13:50).
- **How to apply:** 1) Choose a passage you think is exceptional. 2) Commit it to memory. 3) Write it out from memory, as often as possible. 4) [my inference, consistent with V12 tool 16] Compare with the original to see where your version drifted.
- **Symptoms fixed:** Knowing the terminology but lacking the skill, which is his diagnosis of Strunk and White themselves (12:35).
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** A grant writer memorises a strong one-paragraph abstract from a funded proposal in her field, writes it from memory each morning for a week, and notes the move she keeps dropping: the one-sentence statement of stakes.
- **Timestamps:** V14 12:35 to 13:50.

#### 3.4 Split infinitives and the stress position
- **What:** The rule against splitting infinitives ("to boldly go") is wrong. He says E. B. White added it, not Strunk, even though White admitted elsewhere that splits can help clarity (14:19 to 14:44). White's rationale, that splitting emphasises the adverb, has it backwards. The end of the sentence is the stress position. That is a principle Strunk and White themselves state elsewhere (14:44 to 15:24).
- **Why it works:** Words at the end of a sentence get greater emphasis (15:24). The real issue is where emphasis belongs, not split infinitives (15:24 to 15:45).
- **How to apply:** 1) To bind the adverb tightly to its verb, splitting is fine. 2) To *highlight* the adverb or a key idea, move it to the end of the sentence (15:45 to 16:03).
- **Symptoms:** Contorting a sentence to avoid a split. A key qualifier buried mid-sentence when it should land last.
- **When it does not apply:** Splitting is "often completely natural and clear" (15:45).
- **Example:** Tight binding: "We need to carefully review every contract before renewal." Emphasis: "The new policy changes our pricing entirely."
- **Timestamps:** V14 13:50 to 16:03.

#### 3.5 Test any rule against its author's own practice (a critical-reading move)
- **What:** Check whether a style authority follows its own rules. He shows Strunk and White breaking several rules in single sentences within their clarity advice: negative form, passive voice, heavy adjectives, and related words split apart (11:15 to 12:35).
- **Why it works:** If the authors can't apply the rules, the rules don't describe good writing. They "know the terminology" but "lack the skill" (12:35). E. B. White is a great writer because he doesn't follow his own advice (10:59 to 11:15).
- **How to apply:** When a rule is offered as absolute, look for good writing by its advocates that breaks it. If good writing routinely breaks it, treat it as a tool with conditions, not a law.
- **Symptoms:** Deference to a rule only because a respected writer stated it. He says the most common objection he hears is that Strunk and White are themselves good writers (10:59).
- **When it does not apply:** He is careful to say not all style tips are harmful ("mostly harmless", 0:57).
- **Example:** A rule says "never start a sentence with 'But'". Checking three essays by writers you admire finds "But" as a sentence opener at the key turn in each.
- **Timestamps:** V14 10:59 to 12:35.

### 4. His stances

- **Argues against:**
  - Strunk and White as a grammar authority (2:09).
  - The blanket "use the active voice" rule (3:03).
  - Grammar checkers and AI that flag passives. Microsoft Word gets named, and "AI cannot think", so it can't make these decisions (7:44, 9:31).
  - The split-infinitive rule (14:19).
  - "Omit needless words": if beginners knew which words were needless, they wouldn't need the advice (0:57).
  - "Do not inject opinion": sometimes opinions are exactly what readers want (1:46).
  - Hiding behind rules instead of finding a voice (0:00).
  - "Grammar Nazis" and dogmatic teachers (7:04 to 7:44).
- **Warnings:** Misunderstanding the passive leads to pointless editing and worse decisions (9:31). Politicians use the passive to evade responsibility (5:51).
- **Concessions:** Some style tips are harmless (0:57). He admits he may be "getting a little bit unfair" to dead authors (14:19). Strunk and White are right that the end of a sentence carries emphasis (15:24).
- **Changes of mind:** None stated. He says he has spent "years" begging writers to stop following the book (10:59).

### 5. People and books he names as sources

William Strunk Jr. and E. B. White, *The Elements of Style* (the target). E. B. White's *Charlotte's Web* (he concedes White's skill). *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* ("mostly harmless"). *Star Trek*'s "to boldly go". Copy work from the copywriting world. Microsoft Word's grammar checker.

## V15: EXACTLY How to Create Flow in Your Nonfiction Writing (2025-05-29)

### 1. Who it is for and what problem it addresses

Nonfiction writers whose prose feels "choppy" or confusing, and writers leaning on grammar rules or AI tools. He says AI writing tools are creating "really bad habits" (0:00). Their problem: sentences that either tell readers what they already know (boring) or hit them with new material they can't connect to anything (lost). And blanket rules like "never use the passive" push their sentences into the wrong order.

### 2. Core argument

Writers have two goals: *clarity* (readers understand) and *value* (readers learn something new) (0:50 to 1:51). Grammar rules and AI tools ignore the thing that joins them, which is reader psychology (1:51). Readers learn by attaching new "leaves" to the trunk and branches of what they already know. He takes that image from Elon Musk (2:09 to 2:58). So every sentence needs both old information (context the reader can attach to) and new information (the payoff), like a battery with a positive and a negative end (3:45). Order matters as well: old first, new last (12:00 to 12:29). The new material at the end of one sentence sets up what the next sentence begins with, which creates a "clear focus" and a coherent passage (10:09 to 12:29). The passive voice exists in English precisely so writers can keep old information in the subject slot despite fixed subject-verb-object order. The "always use the active voice" rule therefore often breaks flow (13:05 to 16:16).

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 The two-charge sentence (old + new; his battery metaphor)
- **What:** Every sentence should give the reader two kinds of information: something they already know and something they don't. Like a battery, a sentence needs both a positive and a negative end, or no light comes on (3:45 to 4:19).
- **Why it works:**
  - A sentence's one job is to inform (3:45).
  - Only-old sentences are a "closed loop". The reader understands but goes nowhere. He demonstrates with a string of facts about the 2024 US election, each of which informs only some viewers (4:19 to 5:16).
  - Only-new sentences are like a paving tile disconnected from the path, so the reader has to leap. He demonstrates with a golf result given first as bare numbers, then with growing context (5:16 to 7:03).
  - All old and readers tune out. All new and readers get lost (7:03).
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each sentence, mark what the reader already knows (old) and what it adds (new).
  2. Any sentence with no new element: cut it, or merge it into another.
  3. Any sentence with no old anchor: add familiar context (a known name, place, earlier term).
  4. Always pair new, complex information with something the reader knows (7:58).
- **Symptoms:** "I know this already, why am I reading it?" (boredom), or "I have no idea what this means" (lost) (3:45). Runs of figures or terms with no anchor. Runs of truisms.
- **When it does not apply:** He points out that "old" depends on the reader. The same sentences inform some viewers and not others (5:16). You need a specific reader in mind.
- **Example:** Only old: "Email is a common way businesses communicate." Only new: "A p=reject DMARC policy with aggregate reporting cut spoofing 60%." Both: "Most companies already verify their email with a record called SPF. Adding a second setting, DMARC, tells receiving servers to reject forged messages, and in our tests it cut spoofing by 60%."
- **Timestamps:** V15 2:58 to 7:58.

#### 3.2 Old-to-new order: known at the start, new at the end
- **What:** Put old information at the beginning of the sentence and new information at the end (12:29).
- **Why it works:** We read left to right, and we learn by attaching new things to what we know. So readers expect familiar first, then unfamiliar (12:00). Reverse it and readers must keep backtracking. The chains of reference turn into "a plate of spaghetti with the sauce underneath" (12:00). He demonstrates with a product description of a pen. Every sentence contains old and new information, yet the passage fails to persuade because the order is backwards "like... batteries into the container backwards" (7:58 to 10:09).
- **How to apply** (10:09 to 12:00):
  1. Keep the content of each sentence. Change only the order.
  2. Move the familiar element (something already mentioned, or the passage's main theme) to the front.
  3. Move the new concept to the end.
  4. Let the new concept at the end of one sentence become the opening of the next. In his pen rewrite, ending a sentence with two new qualities (durability and comfort) sets the reader up to expect the next two sentences to open with those qualities, one each.
  5. Once those are dealt with, reconnect to the main theme and end with the next new concept.
  6. Put transition words that contrast one sentence with the one before (e.g. "however") at the *head* of the sentence, between the two ideas. Don't leave them at the end (11:34).
- **Symptoms:** Sentences that open with a new, heavy noun phrase and end with something already mentioned. Readers rereading earlier sentences. A passage with all the right information that still feels disorganised or unpersuasive.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified. (See 3.4: when active voice forces the wrong order, use the passive.)
- **Example:** Before: "Speed, full-text search and offline access make the Fieldnote app useful. A rebuilt sync engine gives it speed. Every word in every note is indexed for search. Offline access, however, is its most valued feature." After: "The Fieldnote app is useful for three reasons: speed, search and offline access. Its speed comes from a rebuilt sync engine. Its search indexes every word in every note. However, its most valued feature is offline access."
- **Timestamps:** V15 7:58 to 12:29.

#### 3.3 Clear focus (sentence openings that link to one entity)
- **What:** When you follow old-to-new, the beginnings of your sentences keep pointing back to a single entity. He calls that a "clear focus", and it makes the passage a "coherent unit of discourse" (12:29).
- **Why it works:** A steady focus guides readers through the ideas and shows how each new piece connects to the larger idea (12:29).
- **How to apply:** 1) Read only the openings of each sentence in a paragraph. 2) Check they refer back to one theme or its parts. 3) Rewrite the openings that wander.
- **Symptoms:** Sentence openings that jump between unrelated subjects, so the paragraph has no visible center.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** Openings that wander: "The pilot... Budget constraints... Nurses... A new form..." Focused openings: "The pilot... The pilot's first month... Its main finding... That finding..."
- **Timestamp:** V15 12:29.

#### 3.4 Use the passive to keep old information in the subject slot (the three-step conversion)
- **What:** When an active sentence would put new, heavy information first and old information last, switch it to the passive so the order becomes old to new (14:21 to 16:16). He draws on David Foster Wallace: telling students "never use the passive" is foolish. The passive is sometimes exactly right, though probably in no more than about 5% of sentences (13:34 to 13:51).
- **Why it works:** English has fixed subject-verb-object order. The passive lets you put the thing acted upon (old information) in the subject position. "Languages are organisms". They evolve toward efficiency and don't keep forms that serve no purpose, and English kept the passive for exactly this job (15:15 to 16:16). His demonstration is a short Bitcoin passage in which every sentence is active and "a-okay" by Strunk and White, but the third sentence opens with a long, new list of users and ends with the old topic (14:21 to 15:15).
- **How to apply** (16:16):
  1. Find the "goal" (his technical word for the thing that receives the action) and move it to the subject position.
  2. Move the "agent" (the thing that performs the action) to the end, in a "by" phrase.
  3. Add a form of "to be" and switch the main verb to its past participle.
  4. Check the result: old, simple information at the start, new, complex information at the end.
- **Symptoms:** An active sentence mid-paragraph whose subject is a long new noun phrase and whose object is the paragraph's established topic.
- **When it does not apply:** Following Wallace, it isn't needed most of the time (roughly 5% of sentences). He also grants that the passive "can create some problems" (13:05 to 13:34).
- **Example:** Before: "Our team built a new triage tool last spring. Nurses at four clinics, from night-shift veterans to first-week graduates, now use it daily." After: "Our team built a new triage tool last spring. It is now used daily by nurses at four clinics, from night-shift veterans to first-week graduates."
- **Timestamps:** V15 13:05 to 17:20.

#### 3.5 The handshake principle (the German proverb)
- **What:** His summary image. He glosses a German proverb, whose German wording appears on screen and isn't captured, as the beginning and the end shaking hands (17:20).
- **Why it works:** It captures the whole system: each sentence's end reaches toward the next sentence's beginning.
- **How to apply:** Same as 3.2. Check each sentence boundary: does the end of sentence N hand something to the start of sentence N+1?
- **Symptoms:** Choppy prose. Each sentence feels like a fresh start.
- **When it does not apply:** Not specified.
- **Example:** "...the delay came from one missing approval. That approval sat with a manager who was on leave."
- **Timestamps:** V15 0:00 (proverb introduced), 17:20 (glossed).

### 4. His stances

- **Argues against:**
  - AI writing tools, which he says won't make you a good writer and are creating bad habits (0:00, 1:51).
  - Rules-based advice that ignores reader psychology (15:15).
  - "Never use the passive voice. Always prefer active verbs", which he calls "pretty boneheaded" and which Strunk and White and Grammarly enforce (13:05).
- **Warnings:** Sentences with only old information bore readers. Sentences with only new information lose them (7:03). The passive can cause problems and is needed only in a minority of sentences (13:34 to 13:51).
- **Changes of mind:** None stated.
- **Scope note:** He says good writing is "everywhere if you have the eye to see it". He draws examples from product reviews and the sports pages as well as literary writers (0:00).

### 5. People and books he names as sources

Elon Musk (knowledge as a tree: trunk, branches, leaves). David Foster Wallace (on the passive; about 5% of sentences). Strunk and White and Grammarly (targets). Examples, not sources: Tom Watson and Jack Nicklaus's 1977 Open at Turnberry (the golf demo), the 2024 US election (the old-information demo), an Amazon-style pen description, and a Bitcoin passage. [my note] The terms he uses here (old/new information, stress position, topic/focus) match the tradition of Joseph Williams's *Style* and George Gopen, but he names neither in these five videos.

## Across these five videos

### How the ideas connect

**1. One idea runs through all five: writing elements are tools for an effect on a specific reader, not rules.** V12 opens with it as the biggest roadblock he sees (0:41). V14 turns it into a demolition of Strunk and White and repeats the phrase "not a list of rules, but a set of tools" (7:04). V15 names what should replace rules: reader psychology (1:51, 15:15). V12's "no universal readers" (7:30) is the premise behind all of it. Any choice (a word's register, voice, word order, how much to explain) is judged by what it does for *this* reader.

**2. A process split: produce first, perfect later.** V11 separates writing (door closed, for you) from rewriting (door open, for the reader), and treats editing during drafting as the main cause of block. V12 tool 26 (Zappa's timed solo) is the same idea in a new costume. V12 tools 16 and 17 (steal style, collecting scraps) and V11's working-with-words/working-with-ideas loop form the generating side. Everything else in V12 to V15 belongs to the door-open side.

**3. Reader cognition drives sentence advice, and the model deepens over time.**
- *V12* explains sentence tips by *memory load*. Readers go left to right and must hold the subject until the verb arrives (tool 1), hold intro clauses until the main point (tool 30), and cross interruptions between verb and object (tool 31).
- *V14* adds two *positional* ideas: the subject position is a spotlight, and the end of the sentence is the stress position.
- *V15* folds these into one principle about *information status*: old at the start, new at the end. The end of each sentence sets up the start of the next.
- V12 tool 31 is an early case of V15's principle. Placing the interrupting clause according to what the next sentence begins with is really old-to-new linking, stated before he had the general theory. A skill can treat V15 as the master principle and V12's tools 1, 30 and 31 as special cases.

**4. The passive-voice arc, and a tension to settle.**
- *V12* tools 18, 19 and 29 push "characters as subjects, actions as verbs". Tool 29 says to use "active verbs" and put real characters "in the driver's seat". There, "active verbs" means actions expressed as verbs rather than zombie nouns, not the active voice as such.
- *V13* already uses the passive expressively. Naipaul's passives ("words were exchanged") show a character's powerlessness (13:57).
- *V14* gives five legitimate uses and a correct identification test.
- *V15* gives the flow use and a three-step conversion, with Wallace's rough limit of about 5% of sentences.
- **Resolution for a skill** [my inference, consistent with all five]: make characters the subjects and actions the verbs by default (V12). Override the default and use the passive when (a) keeping old information in the subject slot needs it (V15), (b) the focus is on what was acted upon, (c) the actor is unknown or beside the point, or (d) a word needs to land in the stress position (V14). Flag passives that hide responsibility the reader needs (V14's point about politicians).

**5. Two kinds of respect for the reader, which sound opposite.**
- *Make processing easy:* never make the reader hold, leap or backtrack. This is V12's clarity tools and V15's flow principle.
- *Leave interpretation to the reader:* don't explain emotions or intentions (V13's author-splaining), understate the serious so the imagination fills the gaps (V12 tool 23), leave the charged words out (V13's ellipsis).
- These don't conflict [my inference]. The first is about *parsing* the sentence. The second is about *meaning and feeling* in scenes. For expert nonfiction, the claim itself should be easy to parse and explicit. Restraint applies to anecdotes, case scenes and emotional stakes.

**6. Value, then clarity as value's partner.** V11 closes by saying readers "don't give a toss" about grammar and organisation, only about solutions to their problems (15:50). V12 tool 21 says clear but useless is still useless, and introduces the problem statement (35:29 to 37:20). V15 gives clarity its place back. It defines the writer's two goals as clarity and value (0:50 to 1:51) and brings value down to the level of the sentence: each sentence must carry *new* information. So "value" works at two scales. At the scale of the whole piece it means solving a reader's problem (V12). At the scale of the sentence it means telling the reader something they didn't know, attached to something they did (V15).

**7. Writing is social.** V12 tools 3, 4 and 10 treat words as signals of group membership, and treat writing as asking for a seat at a table of people already working on a problem. That explains his hostility to "avoid jargon" and "fifth-grade level" advice. It also fits V15's point that "old information" depends on who is reading.

**8. Learning by imitation, from memory.** V12 tool 16 (steal style: read two or three times, close the book, write from memory) and V14 (copy work, with memory better than an open book) are the same exercise, repeated three months apart. The stress on memory is consistent.

**9. The shared enemy: a voice that judges.** V11's Montreal teacher who corrected students mid-sentence, the inner editor, V14's grammar checkers and "grammar Nazis", and Strunk and White's rules all do the same thing. They make writers timid and cost them their voice. V11 answers with separation and self-compassion. V14 answers with better knowledge, so writers can reject false alarms.

### What develops or shifts between them

- **From literature toward plain nonfiction.** V12 and V13 draw mostly on fiction, poetry, film and music. V14 and V15 use nonfiction material: a restaurant bill, award shows, a pen description, golf results, Bitcoin. V14 introduces him in the past tense, as someone who "spent 10 years as an English professor" and now helps writers "sharpen their ideas and reach serious readers" (0:00). V11 and V12 had him currently teaching at a top Korean university. By May 2025 the channel is turning toward expert nonfiction.
- **The anti-AI stance first appears in V14 and V15.** V11 to V13 don't mention AI. V14 warns that bad rules are built into grammar checkers and AI, and that "AI cannot think" (7:44, 9:31). V15 opens by blaming AI tools for bad habits (0:00).
- **From toolbox to principle.** V12 is a list of 31 tools, and he warns against using many at once (51:02). V14 and V15 each go deep on one principle (voice and emphasis, then information flow). The later principle explains several of the earlier tools.
- **A tension over "writing for yourself".** V11 says that while drafting "the only audience is you" (2:03). V12 says that writing purely for yourself isn't really writing, "just journaling" (18:09). Reconciled [my inference]: V11 describes a private *stage* inside a process aimed at readers. V12 describes the *purpose* of the finished piece. V11 itself ends by pointing to the reader-value step (15:32 to 15:50).
- **A tension over the procrastination voice.** Within V11: refuse the voice pulling you away during a timed session (3:32 to 3:49), but if resistance survives every method, stop and listen to it (10:41 to 11:16). Both hold. They cover different timescales.

The "four principles" promised at the start of V14 aren't listed clearly. The source of V13's husband-and-wife exchange isn't named.
