## V41: "give up DISCIPLINE, you'll write freely" (2026-05-05)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it addresses

It is for writers stuck on a long-term creative project they chose themselves, such as a book or an essay collection, who respond to block by pushing harder. They set daily word counts, use timers, keep fixed desk hours and read habit books. He tells the story of his own year-long block during the pandemic. He was living alone in rural Korea, teaching online and trying to write a novel on a typewriter (V41 0:50 to 2:06). The problem is that forcing makes block worse and breeds resentment of the work. He limits the claim to creative work that is uniquely yours. For routine writing such as emails and reports, a forcing function may work (V41 13:46).

### 2. Core argument

Block is not a failure of discipline. It means the unconscious, the source of creative ideas, can't get past the layers of self-image and social role sitting on top of it. "Discipline" is really a polite word for trying to force the unconscious, and the unconscious can't be forced. What unblocks you is the opposite of control. You loosen your grip on who you imagine you are (for him, "a novelist") and on who others expect you to be. Sometimes that means dropping a project that belongs to an earlier version of you. The daily practice that makes room for the unconscious is freewriting with no goal. You vent a little raw material every day and let it cool. Only later do you bring in the analytical mind to find the small part worth shaping. His one-line version is that you have to "let yourself die in order for your work to live" (V41 12:09).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Block as a signal, not an obstacle (reframe and diagnostic)
- **What it is:** Treat block as information. The project you are forcing may no longer be the one your deeper self wants, and a breakthrough may be close (V41 0:00).
- **Why it works:** The conscious mind picks destinations years in advance. The unconscious may have moved on, and it may see further. Misery and resentment while you "live out" a goal show that the self-image behind the goal has stopped serving you (V41 9:08 to 9:34, 15:59).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Recognise the pattern: you log the hours, produce almost nothing, and resent the sessions (V41 1:13).
  2. Ask who chose this project and when: you now, or a past version of you with a particular self-image?
  3. Ask whether you are acting out a fantasy of who you should be, or meeting other people's expectations (see 3.2).
  4. Consider putting the project physically away, out of sight and not just off the calendar. He put his typewriter in the closet (V41 1:13 to 2:06).
  5. Watch what arrives in the days after, especially ideas that don't fit your current self-image. His arrived two nights later, felt foreign to him (a typewriter user who barely used social media, suddenly thinking about YouTube), and still turned out to be "truly me" (V41 2:06 to 3:07).
- **Symptoms (in the writer rather than the draft):** hours spent with no output; dread and resentment; ever-stricter rules; a project defined long ago that you now avoid; ideas for something else that keep interrupting.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He is "all for goals" and for discipline in the rest of life (V41 21:17). Forcing functions may suit low-value writing such as emails and reports (V41 13:46). He also warns that letting go hurts, because you are attached to the self-image and afraid of losing face (V41 11:28). The flood of ideas that follows can be almost too much to bear (V41 4:06, 12:09).
- **Example (mine):** Three years ago a consultant promised herself "the definitive book on leadership." She blocks out her mornings for it and writes nothing. Meanwhile her notes app fills with observations about how junior analysts learn by overhearing partners on calls. The block is not laziness: the unconscious has already chosen the overhearing essay.
- **Timestamps:** V41 0:00, 1:13 to 3:07, 9:08 to 10:46, 14:41 to 16:41.

#### 3.2 Lacan's three registers (an explanatory model he borrows)
- **What it is:** The self has three layers, or "registers":
  - **Imaginary:** the coherent story you tell yourself about who you are and ought to be, with a beginning, middle and end.
  - **Symbolic:** your social self, meaning the masks you wear for job, relationships and reputation.
  - **Real:** who you are behind closed doors. It is incoherent, excessive and can't be contained.
  - **Jouissance:** a raw energy that bubbles up through the Real (V41 5:36 to 8:13). The captions mention a diagram (Real to Imaginary to Symbolic and back) that was shown on screen and is not captured.
- **Why it matters for writing:** The Imaginary and Symbolic act as protective floodgates. They hold back the Real so you don't embarrass yourself or lose your sense of self, and the price of that protection is your creative work. When these layers harden, the energy can't rise to refresh them, and that is his definition of writer's block (V41 8:13 to 10:46, 12:09).
- **How to apply (as a diagnostic):**
  1. When blocked, ask which layer is clamped down.
     - Imaginary: are you defending an identity ("I'm a novelist", "I'm the rigorous one")?
     - Symbolic: are you writing to satisfy a role, a reputation or other people's expectations?
  2. For the Imaginary, let the self-image go. He describes packing away the typewriter as killing his novelist self-image (V41 9:08).
  3. For the Symbolic, make time away from social roles. His second breakthrough came around day five or six of a 10-day silent retreat: no phone, no speaking, no eye contact (V41 4:40 to 5:36, 9:34).
  4. Don't try to get rid of either layer. They are necessary; the aim is to stop them hardening (V41 9:34).
- **Symptoms:** you can describe the project fluently in terms of what it makes you, but not in terms of what excites you about it. Or every sentence is written with an eye on how colleagues will judge it.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He says outright that you are not going to dispense with these layers (V41 9:34). The model explains block; it does not tell you to quit your job or your relationships.
- **Example (mine):** An academic writing a popular book finds that her Symbolic layer ("serious scholar in my field") vetoes each sentence before it lands. She spends a week writing on the understanding that no colleague will ever see those pages. That week is her small version of the retreat.
- **Timestamps:** V41 5:36 to 10:46, 11:28 to 12:09.

#### 3.3 Elephant and rider (metaphor)
- **What it is:** The conscious mind is a rider holding the reins; the unconscious is an elephant. The elephant accepts suggestions, but if it decides to go somewhere, it goes (V41 14:41).
- **Why it works:** Every blocked writer is a rider yanking the reins toward a destination chosen long ago: a book or a self-image. He then asks: what if the elephant knows better, sees further and has your interests at heart? (V41 14:41 to 16:41)
- **How to apply:** Loosen your grip. Follow, at least on paper, the direction the elephant keeps pulling you: the topics that keep interrupting.
- **Symptoms:** "I should be writing X, but I keep thinking about Y."
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say. (Inferred: he is not against goals as such. His target is a creative goal set by a past self and enforced against present evidence.)
- **Example (mine):** An architect writing a book on microservices keeps drafting side notes about why teams are afraid to delete code. The elephant is heading for the deletion essay.
- **Source note:** He credits no one. The metaphor is widely associated with the psychologist Jonathan Haidt (my note, not his).
- **Timestamps:** V41 14:41 to 16:41.

#### 3.4 The night shift (his label, which he says scientists and mathematicians use)
- **What it is:** The unconscious keeps working on your problems while you sleep or rest. It reports back in images, sensations, dreams, hunches and nagging "itches," not plain sentences (V41 16:41 to 20:09).
- **Why it works:**
  - The unconscious is millions of years older than language, the operating system of an animal, so it doesn't talk in words (V41 19:06).
  - Kekulé, stuck on the shape of benzene, dreamt of a snake eating its tail and realised it was a ring (V41 16:41).
  - A musician who reaches 15% of a new piece on day one starts day two at 25 to 30%, because sleep consolidates the practice (V41 17:55 to 19:06).
  - Seen this way, the unconscious is an ally, not a chaotic force to manage. It finishes yesterday's unfinished sentence and connects ideas you never thought to connect (V41 20:09).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Work the problem consciously until you stop making progress, then stop (V41 17:55).
  2. Hand it over: sleep, walk, rest.
  3. Treat recurring images, hunches and itches as reports back, and write them down.
  4. Expect an image that needs translating, not a finished sentence.
- **Symptoms:** grinding at the desk long after progress stops; dismissing odd images or hunches as noise.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.
- **Example (mine):** A policy analyst can't find the organising idea for a report on building permits, so she stops at 4 pm. The next morning a picture of a toll booth keeps nagging her. The report's frame becomes: every permit charges a toll in time, not money.
- **Timestamps:** V41 16:41 to 20:09.

#### 3.5 Daily freewriting as venting magma (his volcano and magma metaphor)
- **What it is:** Sit down every day and write whatever comes, with no filter and no plan. He is emphatic about what it is not: not material for a newsletter, not the first phase of an essay, not even a first draft (V41 22:53). Its only "goal" is to **open a vent** (his phrase) (V41 24:26).
- **Why it works:**
  - The unconscious is like **magma** (his term): pressurised energy with no shape and no language. When you are blocked, the layers above are sealing it in.
  - A small daily vent lets the energy out, and that energy refreshes the images and symbols you use to understand yourself and the world (V41 22:53 to 24:59).
  - Once magma hits the page it cools into a shape you don't choose, like magma poured into a bucket. Later you may be able to refine that shape (V41 24:59).
  - The order matters. You cannot sculpt magma: generating and judging at the same moment blocks the flow (V41 27:53).
  - Ideas feel as if they come through you. He links this to the Greek idea of gifts from the gods (V41 27:53).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Show up every day and write without filtering or planning.
  2. Expect nothing meaningful. It is not for anybody, including you (V41 25:56).
  3. Don't use good, bad, better or worse. Those words only make sense against a goal, and there isn't one (V41 24:03).
  4. Let it cool, for moments, hours or days (V41 27:01).
  5. Then read it with the analytical mind. Expect most of it, roughly 90% in his estimate, to be dross (V41 31:10). Look for bits with **shine** or **energy** (his terms): something true that you can work with (V41 27:01).
  6. Shape those bits into something useful, like a tool, or beautiful, like a sculpture. This is where craft lives: the manuscript, essay or script gets made at this stage (V41 27:01 to 27:53).
  7. Keep the scraps where they can resurface. A note app's "random note" feature can bring back fragments months or years later that you didn't see the day you wrote them (V41 27:53).
- **Analogy:** It is a painter playing with paint on the canvas, not a sketch or study for a painting (V41 25:56).
- **Symptoms that you are doing it wrong:** you judge the freewrite's quality; you try to turn today's freewrite into a publishable piece; you start with a planned point; you stop to fix things.
- **When it does NOT apply / limits:** It is separate from drafting a piece, so it does not replace a purposeful draft (V41 22:53). You can't make what comes up prettier or more useful by effort (V41 25:56). Even done regularly, it only gives the good mode a chance to happen and never guarantees it (V41 31:10).
- **Example (mine):**
  - Raw freewrite: "hospital parking again. why does everyone at work talk about burnout like it's weather, like it just happens. the nurse who counts ceiling tiles. I hate the word resilience, it means you absorbed it so they don't have to fix it..."
  - A week later, reading it cold, one line has shine: resilience as the name for a cost someone else avoided paying. That becomes the seed of an essay; the other 90% stays in the drawer.
- **Timestamps:** V41 22:17 to 28:56, 31:10.

#### 3.6 Two kinds of conversation (diagnostic)
- **What it is:** He contrasts two conversations.
  - In the first you are self-conscious and dry-mouthed. The ideas come slowly and clumsily, and any fluent patch is something you have said before. The listener feels talked *at*.
  - In the second you hit a topic you care about and stop monitoring yourself. New ideas pour out and somehow hold together, as if from one source (V41 28:56 to 29:57).
- **Why it works:** The good mode happens when you get out of the way and stop generating and judging at once. Rehearsed routines, including teaching routines ("say this, then that, joke here"), belong to the first mode (V41 29:57). Freewriting is the practice that makes the second mode more reliable (V41 31:10).
- **How to apply:** Use it as a self-check while writing: am I rehearsing or discovering? If you are rehearsing, return to freewriting on what you actually care about.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** familiar arguments in the usual order; a lecturing, "talked at" tone; text that sounds like your stock presentation.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.
- **Example (mine):**
  - Rehearsed: "As I always say, culture eats strategy for breakfast. There are three levers..."
  - Discovering: "What surprised me last quarter was that the team with the worst tools shipped fastest, and I think it's because nobody could hide behind the tools..."
- **Timestamps:** V41 28:56 to 31:10.

### 4. His stances
- **Against:**
  - Disciplining your way through creative block: word counts, timers, fixed desk hours, habit systems (V41 0:00, 1:13).
  - The Seinfeld-style locked room, where you sit for a set time and are allowed to do nothing else. He tried it and calls it more myth than practical advice (V41 13:10 to 13:46).
  - Trying to summon, set deadlines for, or bully the unconscious. You won't reach the book by brute willpower (his David Goggins joke) (V41 21:17).
  - Treating the unconscious as an adversary to be managed (V41 20:09).
  - Judging freewriting by quality (V41 24:03).
- **Not against:** goals, discipline in life generally, or forcing functions for low-value writing (V41 13:46, 21:17).
- **Changed his mind:** During his pandemic block he doubled down on discipline and habit science, and he now says that made things worse (V41 1:13). The earlier summary records that his 2025 videos recommended small, kind, consistent habits. V41 is where he openly drops discipline as the frame for creative work.
- **Warnings:**
  - Letting go of self-image and "face" is painful (V41 11:28).
  - Released energy can overwhelm you; the protective layers exist for a reason (V41 12:09).
  - Your plans are made with incomplete knowledge, and "you don't know what you don't know" (V41 21:17).
- **Belief:** meaning is what you end up with, not what you start with (V41 31:10).

## V42: "Writing Advice I've Changed My Mind About (After Teaching It For 10 Years)" (2026-05-10)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it addresses

It is for nonfiction writers of academic and professional writing, newsletters, essays and blogs who have absorbed the standard advice. That advice comes from teachers and viral memes, and is now built into AI writing tools (V42 0:00). The problem is that it sounds authoritative but points attention at the wrong things. He admits that some of it, taught confidently, made his own students worse.

### 2. Core argument

Five widely taught rules fail because they judge writing by surface form, detached from content and reader. The rules are: vary your sentence length; use the active voice only; jargon means showing off or hiding empty thinking; outline before you write; write for yourself, not readers. His replacements:
- A sentence's length follows what it carries, and the real "music" of prose is thematic echo.
- Active and passive voice are a choice about agency.
- Jargon is a social signal. It is legitimate when the writer knows the audience and chooses deliberately.
- Structure should be found in a free first draft (the artist) and then reinforced (the architect).
- Nonfiction exists to change the reader's ideas. So the one test for any language choice is whether it moves the reader from where they are to where you want them.

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Sentence length follows the load (replaces "vary your sentence length")
- **What it is:** Stop engineering length for variety. A sentence is long because it has a lot to carry, and short because the point has landed and nothing is left to say (V42 3:31).
- **Why it works:**
  - Length has no link to readability. Long sentences can be perfectly clear if well coordinated and balanced, and short ones can be incomprehensible (V42 1:18 to 2:12).
  - The music comparison is at best a loose proxy for rhythm, and it breaks down quickly (V42 2:12).
  - Lengthening a short sentence because three short ones came before it isn't music; it is padding (V42 3:31).
  - The rule is really about word count, and readers don't care about word count; they care about value (V42 3:31 to 4:27).
  - Preoccupation with surface features (length, grammatical structures, word choice) pulls attention away from the meaning those forms carry (V42 3:05 to 3:31).
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each sentence, ask what it has to carry: how many ideas, relations and qualifications.
  2. If it carries one thing, keep it short. If it carries linked parts, let it run long, but coordinate and balance the parts.
  3. Never lengthen or chop a sentence only to change the rhythm.
  4. If you want music, get it from recurring themes (3.2).
- **Symptoms:** filler phrases that restate; reviewer comments like "vary your sentence length"; a writer tinkering with rhythm while the argument is still unclear.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He allows no exception.
- **Example (mine):**
  - Before (lengthened for variety): "Revenue fell. Costs rose. Staff left. And it was at this point, in what many observers would later come to regard as a significant turning point in the company's history, that the board decided the time had come to act."
  - After (length follows load): "Revenue fell. Costs rose. Staff left. The board acted only once the losses had run three quarters, the lead investor had threatened to pull out, and the founders had stopped speaking."
  - The last sentence is long because it carries three causes, not for the sound.
- **Timestamps:** V42 0:00 to 4:27.

#### 3.2 Music as motif (the "chord")
- **What it is:** Real musicality in prose is thematic coherence: a motif that recurs across scenes like a chord. He attributes his example to Joyce: a character with kidney disease, later someone eating kidneys, later someone kicked in the kidneys (V42 2:12 to 3:05).
- **Why it works:** Each echo makes the earlier material resonate. It works at the level of substance, not surface (V42 3:05 to 3:31).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Choose an image or key term that is central to your point.
  2. Introduce it early.
  3. Bring it back in new contexts, and let each return shift its meaning.
- **Symptoms:** images that appear once and vanish; "musicality" pursued through rhythm alone.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.
- **Example (mine, nonfiction):** An essay on hospital efficiency opens on a nurse's stopwatch. It returns to the countdown on the scheduling software, and ends with a patient counting the minutes to discharge. The clock is the chord.
- **Timestamps:** V42 2:12 to 3:31.

#### 3.3 The passive voice as agency control (replaces "active voice only")
- **What it is:** The passive is not weak. It is a tool for controlling agency: who appears in the sentence and whether they are named at all (V42 6:16).
- **Why it works:**
  - **Why his Korean science and engineering students overused the passive:** Korean doesn't require a grammatical subject, so translation tools default to the passive, and science signals objectivity by removing the person (V42 5:01). Banning the passive fixed the symptom by removing a real choice.
  - **Darwin example:** the closing passage of *The Origin of Species* exists in versions with and without the Creator named as the one who breathed life into the first forms. That single decision about agency changes the book's meaning and final note (V42 6:37 to 7:58). The agentless version gave him breathing room: readers on either side of the God question could stay with him. Naming the agent pushes each reader further in the direction they already lean (V42 7:58 to 8:28).
  - **Caption note:** the edition details are muddled as spoken. He calls the agentless version the "last edition" and says the Creator was added "in the third." The point doesn't depend on which edition is which.
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each important action, decide whether the reader should see who did it.
  2. Name the actor (active voice) when agency is the point: responsibility, credit, cause.
  3. Leave the actor out (agentless passive) when the actor is unknown or irrelevant. Also leave it out when naming would force a commitment you don't want to impose on a divided audience.
  4. Judge by the effect on your particular readers, not by a rule (V42 8:28).
- **Symptoms:**
  - Reflexive passives from translation or lab-report habit where agency matters ("It was decided that...").
  - The agency dodge ("a mistake was made"), which he names (V42 7:12).
  - The opposite problem: active-only prose that commits to a claim about who did something that the evidence can't support.
- **When it does NOT apply:** The decision must never be outsourced to a rule like "always use the active voice" (V42 8:28).
- **Example (mine):**
  - Passive fits: in a lab method, "Samples were frozen at -80 °C." Who froze them doesn't matter.
  - Active needed: in an incident review, "The load test was skipped" hides the decision the reader needs to see. "Our team skipped the load test to hit the launch date" puts agency where it belongs.
- **Timestamps:** V42 4:27 to 9:28.

#### 3.4 The deliberate-register test (replaces "jargon = showing off or hiding emptiness")
- **What it is:** Language is social, not just a way of delivering information. Your words show who you are and who you are talking to (V42 12:12). Jargon can work as a membership card: "stochastic" rather than "random," "non-trivial" rather than "hard" (V42 12:42 to 13:46). The real test has two parts: does the writer know who they are talking to, and are the choices deliberate? (V42 13:46 to 14:20)
- **Why it works:**
  - Everyone code-switches. His example: South and North Korean speakers understand each other, but word choice instantly shows who comes from where (V42 11:29 to 12:12).
  - Using a community's word signals you sit at their table; the everyday word can signal you don't.
  - Mocking academic prose written for a community you don't belong to is unfair: you probably weren't the audience (V42 14:20).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Name the reading community.
  2. Learn its code words and use them deliberately.
  3. For a broader audience, switch register. Skilled writers address specialists, then write a popular book on the same ideas (V42 14:20).
  4. Keep Feynman's check: if you can't explain it clearly, you may not understand it. Obscurity can hide empty thinking, and that is a different thing from deliberate register (V42 13:46).
- **Symptoms:**
  - Specialist terms used with outsiders.
  - Everyday words that mark you as an outsider to specialists.
  - Dense language that collapses when you try to say it simply.
- **When it does NOT apply:** It is not a licence for empty obscurity (V42 13:46). Nor should "plain English is good English" be used as a universal standard.
- **Example (mine):**
  - To machine-learning engineers: "The outputs are stochastic, so we evaluate distributions, not single runs."
  - To a general newsletter: "Ask the same question twice and you'll get two different answers, so one test proves little."
- **Timestamps:** V42 9:28 to 14:20.

#### 3.5 Begin as an artist, revise as an architect (replaces "outline first")
- **What it is:** Writing has two distinct phases, and collapsing them causes the trouble.
  - The **artist** phase (his term): timed, undirected writing that follows hunches, for no reader.
  - The **architect** phase (his term): step back and find the structure that is native to what emerged, then strengthen it (V42 16:31 to 20:01).
- **Why it works:**
  - Outlining first assumes you have finished thinking before you write. But writing is how thinking becomes visible to you. He hedges: maybe the thinking happens in the shower or during sleep, but writing is where you discover it and make it legible to yourself (V42 15:45 to 16:02).
  - For him, outline-first reliably led to block (V42 15:25 to 15:45).
  - Ant colonies build strange, efficient structures that fit their environment with no blueprint and no planner. The structure emerges from the activity (V42 16:31).
  - Organisation still matters. He cites studies showing that readers navigate a text with the same brain systems they use for physical space, looking for a hierarchy as in a map or a maze (V42 19:27).
- **How to apply (artist):**
  1. Set a timer, write without direction and follow hunches. Nobody will read this (V42 16:31 to 17:29).
  2. Optional **tether** (his image): write a question at the top of the page and go back to it when you lose your way (V42 17:29).
  3. Watch for signs of judgment. Inner commentary ("that's not very good") or reaching for backspace means other people's judgment has crept in, so remove it (V42 17:29).
  4. The phase should feel free and energised, or calm and absorbed (V42 17:29).
  5. Trust that the subconscious already knows the shape of ideas it has been working on for a while. Your job is to dig them out (V42 18:15).
- **How to apply (architect):**
  1. When you have dug up enough, step back and look for macro patterns native to the ideas: the major and minor **through lines** (his term) (V42 18:34 to 18:55).
  2. Strengthen those through lines in revision. Don't force the material into a template (V42 18:55).
  3. If you need an outline at all, make it after the first draft. It is already in there, waiting to be uncovered (V42 20:01).
- **Symptoms:**
  - Outline slots you can't fill.
  - Block right after outlining.
  - A draft that reads like filled-in blanks.
  - A "three points" template imported from somewhere else.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He is explicit that he is not against organisation (V42 19:27). (Inferred: artist output alone isn't ready for readers; the architect's structure is required.)
- **Example (mine):**
  - Tether question: "Why do our best engineers stop writing design docs after their second year?"
  - Three 20-minute sessions produce 3,000 messy words. Reading them back, "status" and "an audience of one" come up on nearly every page.
  - Those become the two through lines. The architect rebuilds the piece around them instead of the "History / Problems / Solutions" outline planned in advance.
- **Timestamps:** V42 14:20 to 20:01.

#### 3.6 The reader-change test and the four reader questions (replaces "write for yourself")
- **What it is:** Nonfiction exists to change the reader's ideas, not to express yours (V42 21:48 to 22:14). The only question to ask of any language choice is whether it moves your reader from where they are to where you want them (V42 23:06). When you move from artist to architect, answer four questions about the reader (V42 23:44).
- **Why it works:**
  - Clear, organised or persuasive writing that is useless is still useless. No one admires the organisation of ideas that bore them (V42 21:23 to 21:48).
  - Ideas exist only in a reader's mind. He uses Thich Nhat Hanh's plaques for the memorial his disciples insisted on building: "I'm not here," then "I'm not out there either" (V42 22:14 to 23:06).
  - A text nobody reads is the tree falling in an empty forest (V42 23:06).
  - "Write for yourself" rests on the view that writing is self-expression. He calls it a **bad export** from fiction (his phrase) (V42 20:50 to 21:23).
- **How to apply:**
  1. After freewriting, switch hats.
  2. Answer:
     - What do they currently believe about this topic?
     - Where does their knowledge stop?
     - What problems are they trying to solve?
     - What do I need them to think differently after reading? (V42 23:44)
  3. Replace "Have I expressed myself clearly?", the question most writers ask, with the reader-change question (V42 24:08).
  4. Judge each language choice by whether it moves this reader.
- **Symptoms:**

## V43: "Revealing a New Yorker Writer's Personal Essay Strategy" (2026-05-21)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it addresses

It is for personal essayists, especially independent writers on Substack. Their essays are earnest and carefully written but fall flat, because they are built from private symbols and experiences that readers can't get inside (V43 0:21 to 1:11). It is also for anyone who fears that a set structure would cramp their thinking (V43 8:50).

### 2. Core argument

A personal essay is like telling someone about last night's dream. It is vivid to you and means nothing to them, because every symbol is packed with your private associations. As he tells it, Zadie Smith failed at Cambridge writing personal, chaotic essays. She found her way through three working-class Cambridge writers, whose example taught her the impersonal essay. It is still passionate, but it aims the passion outward at the world. Essay readers aren't mainly interested in you; they want to use your essay to understand the world. The writer becomes a guide at a window, and the reader sits beside the writer instead of being asked to identify with them. He pairs this with Smith's six-arrow structure:
- Put the title in the centre.
- Write the first and last lines first.
- Fill the four arrows between them.

The structure only moves forward, which frees your thinking because the only decision left is the next one.

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 The dream test (diagnostic for private symbols)
- **What it is:** Ask whether your essay is like describing a dream: images that feel meaningful to you because of private memories, but mean nothing to the reader (V43 0:21).
- **Why it works:** Each private symbol is crammed with the writer's own associations (his image: a clown car). Readers get the surface but can't get inside (V43 0:21 to 1:11).
- **How to apply:**
  1. List the key images and events in your draft.
  2. For each, ask whether its meaning depends on memories only you have.
  3. If it does, give readers its significance in the world, or swap it for something they can see for themselves.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Vividness that doesn't transfer.
  - Early readers who politely nod or say "nice" and nothing else.
  - An essay whose stakes amount to "this mattered to me."
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say. (Inferred: readers who come to a memoir for the person may be an exception, but he says essay readers aren't primarily interested in you (V43 3:08).)
- **Example (mine):**
  - Before: "My grandfather's green thermos, dented the winter the mill closed, still sits on my shelf."
  - After: "Mill towns didn't lose their jobs in one day. They lost them in the dents: the thermos never replaced, the repair put off, as households quietly stopped planning years ahead."
  - The thermos stays, but now it is a window onto something the reader can see.
- **Timestamps:** V43 0:21 to 1:11.

#### 3.2 The impersonal turn: from "what does it mean to me?" to "what does it reveal about the world?"
- **What it is:** Change the question you put to your subject. When Smith wrote about a 17th-century poem, she stopped asking what it meant to her. She asked what it revealed about English attitudes to land, capital and who gets to own what (V43 3:08).
- **Why it works:**
  - Readers want to use an essay to understand the world (V43 3:08).
  - The point is not to remove passion but to redirect it (**sublimate**, his word) into an object the reader can see, touch and stand beside (V43 4:05).
  - Her convictions come through as arguments, not confessions (V43 10:09).
  - It lets you be passionate without being self-indulgent (V43 9:46).
  - The writer becomes a guide who directs the reader's attention outward, through a window (V43 3:08).
  - Her three models wrote about class, power and the systems they lived inside, with anger, precision and wit. Their subject was a living concern, not cocktail-party talk (V43 2:43 to 3:08).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find the object your passion attaches to: a text, event, practice or system.
  2. Ask what it reveals about the world (class, power, systems, how things work), not about you.
  3. Turn each feeling into a claim. Rewrite each confession as an argument about the object.
  4. Keep the heat, but aim it outward.
- **Symptoms:**
  - "I" is the subject of most sentences.
  - Feelings are reported rather than claims argued.
  - The piece only has value if the reader already cares about the writer.
- **When it does NOT apply:** It is not a call for bland objectivity. Nobody ever called Smith detached or dull, and her convictions are everywhere, just below the surface (V43 9:46).
- **Example (mine):**
  - Personal: "Quitting my job made me feel free for the first time."
  - Impersonal: "The two-week notice period quietly shows who owns an employee's time. The company can end the arrangement today; the worker must give back fourteen days."
- **Timestamps:** V43 2:43 to 4:05, 9:46 to 10:09.

#### 3.3 "Be in relation," not "relate" (the park bench)
- **What it is:** A personal essay asks readers to relate to the writer, to come over to the writer's private world. An impersonal essay asks them to *be in relation* with the writer: side by side on a park bench, looking out at the world together (V43 4:05, 8:50 to 9:46).
- **Why it works:**
  - A personal essay connects only with people who share enough of your experience to follow you in. Ideas and the world are open to anyone willing to think and look, whatever their background (V43 8:50).
  - Thinking alongside someone feels more intimate, not less, than watching them think (V43 9:46).
  - Smith, paraphrased: essaying tries to build a shared space open to anyone, without checking the reader's identity papers in the first paragraph (V43 4:05 to 4:56).
- **How to apply:** Put yourself and the reader in front of the same object. Point at it, and think about it aloud together.
- **Symptoms:** openings that establish the writer's identity or credentials before showing anything the reader can look at.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.
- **Example (mine):**
  - Instead of: "As a first-generation college student, I've always felt out of place..."
  - Open with the object: "The financial aid form asks for a parent's tax return on page one."
- **Timestamps:** V43 4:05 to 4:56, 8:50 to 9:46.

#### 3.4 The value question: the writer or the world?
- **What it is:** Ask whether the reader will use this essay to understand the writer or to understand the world (V43 10:09).
- **How to apply:** Before revising, write one sentence on what the reader will understand about the world afterward. If you can't, revise toward 3.2.
- **Symptoms:** you can't finish the sentence "After reading, they'll see that..." without using "me."
- **Timestamps:** V43 10:09.

#### 3.5 Zadie Smith's six arrows (her framework, as he presents it)
- **What it is:** Picture the essay as a rectangle with an arrow at each corner and one halfway along the top and bottom: six arrows running clockwise, with the title in the centre (V43 4:56 to 5:43).
- **Why it works:**
  - The title in the centre anchors you to the question whenever you drift (V43 5:43).
  - Writing the opening and closing lines first reduces the rest to four arrows (V43 5:43).
  - The arrows only go forward, so you never double back or lose the plot. This frees your thinking by removing every decision except the next one (V43 7:55 to 8:50).
  - The reprise gives a sense of inevitability. Arriving where arrow one promised gives the satisfaction of a closed loop, and makes the essay feel whole (V43 7:55).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Put the title, which anchors your question, in the middle.
  2. Write the opening line and the closing line first (arrows 1 and 6) (V43 5:43).
  3. **Arrow 1, introduction:** state your position and the promise the essay will keep: where you are going to take the reader. Commit and don't waffle (V43 5:43 to 6:17).
  4. **Arrow 2, development:** the first layer of the argument, meaning the evidence and reasoning that start to deliver on the promise. Don't be afraid of metaphor. Smith's view is that a metaphor reaches a general reader better than technical exposition; the captions garble this sentence and mention Piketty (V43 6:17 to 6:49).
  5. **Arrow 3, devil's advocate:** stress-test the argument. Either push it deeper with a second layer of nuance, or complicate it: steelman the other side, bring in the strongest counter-argument, or admit where the claim runs out. This is what makes an essay different from a speech. A speech asserts, while to "essay" is to try, so the reader should feel you are trying out the idea, not forcing it on them (V43 6:49 to 7:08).
  6. **Arrow 4, wind down:** the finish line is in sight. Add no new threads; weave the existing ones into a whole. The argument stops expanding and starts to resolve (V43 7:08 to 7:55).
  7. **Arrow 5, reprise with variations:** echo earlier material, now changed by everything in between. Familiar ideas return and mean more (V43 7:55).
  8. **Arrow 6, conclusion:** the "home straight." Arrive at the destination announced in arrow 1 (V43 7:55).
- **Symptoms:**
  - Essays that wander or double back.
  - Introductions that hedge.
  - No counter-argument, so the piece reads as a speech.
  - New ideas appearing near the end.
  - An ending unconnected to the opening.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He expects personal essayists to object that the structure cramps their thinking, and rejects the objection (V43 8:50). He doesn't limit the framework to any phase. The earlier summary's note to use it "in the Architect phase only" is not stated in V43; it is an inference from V42 (see "Across these five videos").
- **Example (mine):** Title: "Why Meetings Multiply."
  - Opening line (1): "Meetings multiply because they are the only work nobody can be blamed for."
  - Closing line (6): "Cancel a meeting and you haven't saved an hour; you've made someone accountable."
  - (2) Calendar audits, and how shared decisions spread the blame thin.
  - (3) Devil's advocate: some meetings prevent expensive mistakes, and remote teams need live time. So the claim holds for recurring status meetings, not decision meetings.
  - (4) Wind down: blame-avoidance and real coordination woven together.
  - (5) Reprise: "blame" returns, now seen as the currency meetings trade in.
  - (6) The closing line.
- **Timestamps:** V43 4:56 to 8:50.

### 4. His stances
- **Against:**
  - The flood of private-symbol personal essays on Substack (V43 0:21 to 1:11).
  - Putting the writer at the centre (V43 3:08).
  - Waffling in the introduction (V43 5:43).
  - Essays that are really speeches, asserting without testing (V43 7:08).
  - The belief that your everyday experience isn't interesting enough to write about. He defers this to another video, which the earlier summary maps to V21 (V43 10:50).
- **Warnings:** personal essays carry a "massive risk" that few essayists consider (V43 2:18). As the video unfolds, the risk is private, self-centred writing that reaches only readers who share your experience.
- **Not against passion:** redirect it; don't remove it (V43 4:05).

### 5. People and books named as sources
- **Zadie Smith:** the story, the impersonal essay, the six arrows, and the passages on "being in relation" and a common space (V43 throughout, especially 4:05 to 4:56).
- **University of Cambridge, King's College:** the three working-class King's writers who changed her approach. They are not named (V43 2:43).

- **Piketty:** in a garbled sentence about metaphor versus technical exposition (V43 6:49).
- **Substack:** where the private-symbol essays are piling up (V43 0:21).

## V44: "I coached 25 writers to learn THIS" (2026-07-20)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it addresses

It is for people writing online on their own: newsletters and essays, across time zones and levels of experience. From about 30 one-on-one interviews he sorted them into two groups (V44 4:35 to 6:29):
- **Finishers.** They start pieces but never finish or publish them, and drafts sit in a drive. They want a forcing function and accountability.
- **Voice-seekers.** They already publish, but their prose feels flat. It is stitched together from storytelling and copywriting tricks that don't feel like them, and AI is often wearing their voice down. The problem is "finding your voice."

### 2. Core argument

Block and flat voice are not mainly craft problems; they are relationship problems. You never have writer's block, and never worry about your voice, when you are talking to someone you respect and whose respect you want. Your word choices flow from that relationship (V44 30:39 to 31:59). So the most valuable thing a writing course can give is not content. It is a small community of practice: early readers you trust, sharing a ritualised way of giving feedback. The rituals he used are:
- the letter of response
- reading twice
- listening for sameness and difference
- the silent author

In his two-month sprint both problems improved even though he never taught a lesson on either (V44 32:40 to 34:31). Side lessons: publishing your ideas brings opportunities you couldn't have planned, and the sprint's best use of AI was connecting people, not writing.

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 Sort the writer's problem: finishing or voice (diagnostic)
- **What it is:** The two groups need different things. Finishers need a deadline and accountability. Voice-seekers need deeper craft and a real relationship with readers (V44 4:35 to 6:29).
- **Why it works:** Courses built for one group leave the other unserved (V44 6:53 to 10:18).
  - Ship 30 for 30 gets people publishing but teaches little craft.
  - Write of Passage builds community, but its craft teaching soon runs out for more experienced writers.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Ask: have you published anything? What sits unfinished? Does your published work sound like you?
  2. Match the help: a deadline and a partner for finishers; readers plus craft for voice-seekers.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Finishers: a folder of half-drafts.
  - Voice-seekers: a steady publishing record, but "it doesn't sound like me," and patchwork technique.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.
- **Example (mine):** A writer with nine half-finished essays needs a date and a partner. A writer with forty published posts that "all sound like LinkedIn" needs readers she respects and deeper craft.
- **Timestamps:** V44 4:35 to 10:18.

#### 3.2 The accountability partner (borrowed from Ship 30 for 30)
- **What it is:** Pair each writer with a partner straight away and check in daily: How's the writing going? What did you publish? Let's read it (V44 7:46).
- **Why it works:** Participants named it the best part of that course. It solves the getting-started problem (V44 7:46).
- **How to apply:** Pair people up on day one, schedule a daily check-in, and have partners read each other's published pieces.
- **When it does NOT apply:** On its own it provides no craft (V44 8:01).
- **Timestamps:** V44 6:53 to 8:01.

#### 3.3 The letter of response (feedback protocol)
- **What it is:** A letter of about two pages addressed to the essay's author. It moves through fixed stages (V44 15:30 to 18:55, 21:10).
- **Why it works:**
  - If you misread the essay, the author has no reason to accept your criticism. If you state what they were trying to do so well that they agree, they are ready to hear it (V44 17:02 to 17:54).
  - The "what I'd steal" stage turns attention to craft (V44 18:13).
  - The practice teaches people to read like writers: to see how a text produces its effects on a reader, not just to consume it (V44 24:17 to 24:38).
  - A shared protocol makes peer feedback qualified; without one, people don't know how to talk about a text (V44 29:49 to 30:39).
- **How to apply** (he says there were four or five stages and can't recall the middle ones on camera) (V44 18:13):
  1. State as precisely as you can what the essay is trying to do.
  2. Say what you would steal: a technique you'll try in your own work.
  3. (Middle stage or stages: not recalled in the video.)
  4. Only then, and only then, say what was lacking or missing.
- **Practice sequence:**
  - Rehearse the format for two weeks on professionally published essays before anyone workshops their own (V44 18:55).
  - Build the reading list from the group. Each member submits two essays they consider well written, which creates a shared **culture of taste** (his phrase) (V44 19:31).
  - His sprint ran four sessions a week, eight letters over two weeks, with two writers reading their letters each session (V44 20:23 to 21:35).
- **Symptoms it fixes:**
  - Feedback that misreads the piece.
  - Defensive authors.
  - Generic rule-quoting, such as "vary your sentence length" (V44 30:08).
  - Ritual praise sandwiches.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say. He stresses that it is analytical, not a book club about feelings (V44 16:29).
- **Example (mine):** "Your essay is trying to convince engineering managers that onboarding fails in week five, not week one. I'd steal the way you open each section with a real Slack message; I see the problem before you name it. What I missed: what a manager should actually do on day 35."
- **Timestamps:** V44 15:30 to 18:55, 19:31 to 21:35.

#### 3.4 Two readings: gut first, then a body scan
- **What it is:** Read the piece twice, days apart. The first time, read as an ordinary reader. The second time, read as an analyst who locates where each effect happens (V44 22:00 to 24:38).
- **Why it works:** The first reading captures a real reader's response. The second turns a vague verdict ("boring") into knowledge of how the text works. His meditation analogy: when pain arises during a sit, you don't get up. You look at it: where it is, where it is strongest, where it ends (V44 23:37).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Read normally. On your phone is fine; skim if you want to, and put it down if you're bored (V44 22:48).
  2. Write nothing. Record a voice note of your gut reaction: feelings, whatever comes (V44 22:48).
  3. His workflow: have AI turn that voice note into a bullet list of the main points (V44 22:48 to 23:24).
  4. Put it away for a day or a few days (V44 23:24).
  5. Reread analytically. Take each reaction and find where in the text it lives: which part is most boring, and where the boredom starts and stops (V44 23:37 to 24:17).
  6. Study that spot closely to learn how the effect is produced.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Feedback like "dense," "boring" or "confusing" with no location.
  - Admiring an essay without being able to learn anything from it.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.
- **Example (mine):**
  - Gut note: "Lost interest halfway."
  - Body scan: interest drops at paragraph 7, where the legislative history starts. It bottoms out at the list of dates, and comes back when the named nurse returns in paragraph 11.
  - Lesson: history with no character in it loses me.
- **Timestamps:** V44 22:00 to 24:38.

#### 3.5 Same and different (a reading rule)
- **What it is:** Advice from an English professor he once had: to read well, look for where things are the same and where they are different (V44 21:35 to 22:00).
- **Why it works:** It gives listeners one simple instruction that turns two monologues into a comparison. The hour-long conversation then grows out of the agreements and divergences (V44 22:00).
- **How to apply:**
  1. While a peer reads their letter, listen for where it agrees with yours and where it differs.
  2. Start the discussion from the agreements and dig into them, then take up the differences (V44 22:00).
  3. (Inferred: the same move works inside a single text, noticing echoes and contrasts between parts.)
- **Timestamps:** V44 21:35 to 22:48.

#### 3.6 The silent-author rule
- **What it is:** While a writer's piece is discussed, the writer says nothing. In his online version, authors turned off camera and microphone for the hour, then came back for the last 15 minutes to answer questions (V44 25:15 to 27:14).
- **Why it works:**
  - It mirrors publication: once a piece is out, you can't explain or defend it (V44 26:04).
  - It frees readers to say what they really think, without the feedback sandwich. They talk about the text, not to the author, and almost forget the author is there (V44 26:24 to 26:45).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Set a fixed discussion period with the author muted.
  2. Discuss the text in the third person.
  3. End with a short slot for the author's questions.

- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.

#### 3.8 Early readers and a community of practice ("early readers" is his preferred term)

  - When you talk to someone you respect, you choose words to fit the relationship. You show respect, notice their interests and hold back from rambling. Those social cues come before, and shape, your language choices (V44 31:19 to 31:59).
  - Once you know who you are talking to and want their good opinion, block and voice problems disappear (V44 31:59 to 32:40).

  - Writing for "everyone."

## V45: "A.I. Mistakes Writers Must Stop Making" (2026-07-23)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it addresses

It is for nonfiction writers who bring AI into their process. Some ask AI for content and then restyle it; others dump notes, freewriting or a rambling voice memo into AI to "clean it up." It is also aimed at people in the AI-writing debate who fixate on surface tells such as certain sentence structures, repeated words or the em dash (V45 2:13). The problem is prose that is polished but hollow, which readers immediately sense is inauthentic (V45 7:12, 11:22).

### 2. Core argument

The debate is happening at the wrong level. Writing is communication, a meeting of minds, and every language choice comes from how you show up for the other person. He compares it to a first date: a person who doesn't want to be there can't be rescued by a nice shirt, cologne or an earpiece feeding lines (V45 1:08 to 2:37). Real communication starts from a "vision," something you have seen to be true. With the vision in mind and the person in front of you, the words come without planning (V45 5:09 to 6:14).
- **Workflow 1 (restyling AI output):** there is no vision behind it at all.
- **Workflow 2 (AI cleaning up your notes):** it assumes style is a coat of paint you can apply separately from substance. They are not separable. AI sees only the words you used to point at your thought, never the thought, so it produces fluency without communication and a distorted window on your idea.

Supplying more context doesn't fix this. Context can never be complete, and if you are doing that much work, you should turn away from the AI and toward the reader. Acceptable use: AI as a navigation layer over your notes.

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics

#### 3.1 The first-date test (spirit before surface)
- **What it is:** Before editing surface features, ask how you are showing up for the reader. Do you actually want to communicate with this person? (V45 1:08 to 2:37)
- **Why it works:** Language choices flow from the relationship. Surface polish can't save an absent spirit, and the other person senses it (V45 1:49 to 2:37). In conversation you never plan your sentences; they come out in the course of the exchange (V45 3:04).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Name the person you are writing to.
  2. Say what you want them to see, and why you care that they see it.
  3. Only then look at sentences.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Time spent scrubbing "AI tells" such as em dashes and stock sentence patterns while the reader is still unnamed.
  - Smooth prose with no one on the receiving end.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He concedes that speech and writing differ: a writer is separated from the reader by space and time. He says the underlying intent to relate still applies (V45 3:04 to 3:33).
- **Example (mine):** A founder spends an hour swapping out em dashes in a launch post, and nothing improves. Then she writes to one real customer: "You told me last month the export breaks every Friday. Here's what we changed." Now the post has a voice.
- **Timestamps:** V45 1:08 to 3:33.

#### 3.2 Vision first (have you actually seen something?)
- **What it is:** An idea is like a vision: you have seen something and know it to be true. Hold that vision, face the person, and you don't need to plan sentences; the words come out as you describe it (V45 5:09 to 6:14). He records the video unscripted, with no notes, to demonstrate (V45 5:09 to 5:30).
- **Why it works:** Real communication points at something real inside you. Restyled AI output points at nothing: no thought was had and nothing was seen. It is like sanding the edges of a hollow object (V45 6:52 to 7:12).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Before writing, state in one sentence what you have seen to be true.
  2. Picture your reader.
  3. Describe it to them as you would over coffee.
  4. If you can't state the vision, go back to thinking (freewriting, the artist phase), not to polishing.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Moving sentences around without knowing what they are for.
  - Drafts that started from "give me three points on X."
  - Prose that sounds hollow.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.
- **Example (mine):**
  - Not: "Write three points on remote onboarding."
  - Instead: "I watched four new hires in a row ask the same question in week three, and each time the answer sat in a document nobody had linked." That is a vision; write from it.
- **Timestamps:** V45 5:09 to 7:30.

#### 3.3 The chicken-order test (could you keep going if the reader talked back?)
- **What it is:** New to Korea and before delivery apps, he memorised the sounds of a phone order for fried chicken. He sounded fluent, but whenever the owner asked a clarifying question, he panicked and hung up (V45 9:06 to 9:53). AI-cleaned notes give the same result: **fluency without communication** (his phrase) (V45 10:34).
- **Why it works:** Smooth prose you haven't earned in the substance is hollow. Style and substance are not two independent variables; they aren't even separate levels, and changing one distorts the other (V45 11:41).
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each claim, imagine the reader asking a follow-up: why? compared with what? for example?
  2. If you can't answer beyond what's on the page, you have fluency without understanding. Go back to the substance.
- **Symptoms:**
  - Sentences you couldn't re-explain in your own words.
  - Polished paragraphs that claim more than you know.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.
- **Example (mine):**
  - AI-cleaned sentence: "Asynchronous communication fosters deeper alignment across distributed teams."
  - Reader: "Deeper than what? How would I see it?"
  - If your only answer is a rephrasing, the substance isn't there yet.
- **Timestamps:** V45 9:06 to 11:41.

#### 3.4 A window, not a funhouse mirror
- **What it is:** Good writing is a window onto the world. You have seen something and you frame it so the reader can see it too (V45 14:10). When AI builds the window from your words alone, it distorts. It has never seen what you saw; it has only the words you used to point at the vision (V45 14:35 to 14:53).
- **Why it works:** Ask an image generator to redraw your face from another angle while keeping every feature. The result is plausible but always slightly off, because it has never seen you in three dimensions or over time (V45 12:21 to 13:48). Prose "improved" by AI is distorted the same way (V45 13:26 to 14:10).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Keep the framing of what you saw in your own hands.
  2. Try his image experiment yourself if you need convincing (V45 12:21).
- **Symptoms:** a draft that is plausible but subtly not what you meant.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.
- **Timestamps:** V45 12:21 to 14:53.

#### 3.5 Turn from the AI to the reader (his answer to "just prompt better")
- **What it is:** Some defend AI writing by saying you only need to give it more context about the audience and the vision. He has two replies:
  - You can never give enough context to match the full situation of a text. That would take something like Laplace's demon, who knows the position of every atom (V45 14:53 to 15:50).
  - If you go to that much effort, you are already communicating, just with the wrong party. Turn away from the AI and toward your reader (V45 15:50 to 16:23).
- **Why it works:** Readers aren't looking for flashy prose. They want a meeting of minds: someone with a clear vision of something true, who has taken the time to arrange the text so it makes sense to them (V45 16:23 to 17:18).
- **How to apply:** When you catch yourself writing a long prompt that describes your reader, stop. Use that description to write the first paragraph directly to that reader.
- **Symptoms:** long prompts describing your audience; a lot of effort going to the tool rather than the reader.
- **When it does NOT apply:** He does not say.
- **Example (mine):**
  - Instead of a 400-word prompt ("my audience is mid-career ward managers who distrust scheduling software..."),
  - write: "If you manage a ward, you've probably learned to double-check the scheduling software every Sunday night."
- **Timestamps:** V45 14:53 to 17:18.

#### 3.6 Where AI is acceptable
- **What it is:** No AI in communicative writing at any stage. AI is fine as a navigation layer in a second brain, which is his Obsidian setup, to help sort through notes (V45 17:18 to 18:09).
- **Timestamps:** V45 17:18 to 18:09.

### 4. His stances
- **Against AI at any stage of communicative writing** (V45 0:21, 17:18). "Any stage" is what corrupts the spirit you bring to writing.
- **Workflow 1, restyling AI output:** he calls this the source of much of the inauthenticity readers complain about. His crude image is polishing a turd, and he says it is worse than that, because there is no substance underneath (V45 3:33 to 4:39, 6:52).
- **Workflow 2, AI tidying your notes or voice memo:** equally pernicious. Thinking it is legitimate reveals a misunderstanding of writing: the belief that style is independent of substance (V45 7:52 to 8:47).
- **On AI itself:** it is a pattern-recognition machine that turns words into numbers and back, a "parlor trick," not an idea (V45 4:39).
- **Against the surface-level debate** ("don't use this structure, avoid the em dash") (V45 2:13), and against grumpy rants about corrupted art or superior taste (V45 0:21).
- **Against "better prompts" as the fix** (V45 14:53 to 16:23).
- **Positive claim:** readers want a meeting of minds and a text arranged to make sense to them (V45 16:23 to 17:18).

### 5. People and books named as sources
- **Laplace's demon:** the physics thought experiment (V45 15:14).
- **Obsidian:** his second-brain tool (V45 17:18).
- **His own Korean chicken-order story** (V45 9:06).
- No books named.

## Across these five videos

1. **The relationship comes before the language.** This is the strongest thread in the batch.
   - V44 and V45 make the same claim in almost the same words: word choices flow from how you relate to a specific person (V44 31:19 to 31:59; V45 2:37).
   - V41 has the seed in its two conversations: fluent speech comes when you stop monitoring yourself and care about the topic (V41 28:56 to 31:10).
   - V43 describes the ideal writer–reader relation as sitting side by side, looking at the world (V43 9:46).
   - V42 places ideas only in the reader's mind (V42 23:06).
   - **For a skill:** before any sentence-level work, establish who the reader is and how the writer stands toward them. Many surface problems (flat voice, hollow polish) are symptoms of a missing reader.

2. **His explanation of block moves outward: from the self, to process, to relationship.**
   - V41 (May): the unconscious is blocked by hardened self-constructs. The cure is to get out of the way and freewrite daily.
   - V42 (May): outlining before thinking causes block.
   - V44 (July): block is a sign that you have no one to talk to. The cure is early readers in a community of practice.
   - He doesn't withdraw V41. V44 ends by sending viewers to his "unfiltered advice" video on block (V44 34:31), presumably V41.
   - So the views sit together: a solitary practice brings material up, and a relational practice shapes it for readers.
   - All three reject willpower-based fixes: discipline, "just press publish," word counts.

3. **Three modes of writing, not two.**
   - V41's freewriting explicitly has no goal, is for nobody, and is not the first phase of an essay (V41 22:53).
   - V42's artist phase is also for no reader, but it can carry a question at the top of the page and aims to produce material for a piece (V42 17:29 to 18:34).
   - Then comes the architect phase, for readers.
   - **For a skill:** keep them apart:
     - (a) daily goal-free venting
     - (b) question-tethered exploratory drafting
     - (c) reader-facing revision
   - Judgment words (good/bad), backspacing and self-commentary are symptoms that (c) has leaked into (a) or (b) (V41 24:03; V42 17:29).

4. **Surface features follow substance.** This connects most of his stances in the batch:
   - the Provost meme draws attention to the surface (V42 3:05 to 3:31)
   - the AI debate obsesses over em dashes (V45 2:13)
   - unqualified feedback quotes the same meme (V44 30:08)
   - the biggest improvement is not sentence structure or word choice (V44 0:00)

   V42 turns rules into tools judged by their effect: passive voice as agency, jargon as register. V45 pushes this furthest: style and substance are not even separable, so fixing one distorts the other (V45 11:41).
   - **For a skill:** diagnose upstream (vision, reader, point) before editing downstream (length, voice, word choice). Never offer a surface rule as a fix without naming the effect on this reader.

5. **Separate the experience from the judgment, in time.**
   - V41: let the magma cool before sculpting, and read your freewriting later (V41 27:01 to 27:53).
   - V42: artist first, architect after (V42 20:01).
   - V44: read once as a reader, record your gut, then come back days later to analyse (V44 22:48 to 23:24).
   - The shape is the same each time: generate or experience first, then evaluate on a later pass, and locate effects in the text instead of issuing verdicts ("where does the boredom live?", V44 23:37 to 24:17).

6. **The window image unites the personal and the impersonal.**
   - V43 casts the essayist as a guide pointing through a window onto the world (V43 3:08). V45 says good writing is a window onto something you have seen, and AI turns it into a funhouse mirror (V45 14:10 to 14:53).
   - V41 locates the energy in the most private layer of the self (the Real), while V43 says published essays must aim passion outward.
   - These fit together: the energy is personal, and the published object faces the world. V43's word for this is "sublimate": redirect the passion, don't remove it (V43 4:05).

7. **Open tension: fixed structure versus emergent structure.**
   - V42 rejects imposing a template before you have discovered what you think (V42 19:27).
   - Eleven days later, V43 presents a fixed six-part template and says to write the first and last lines first (V43 5:43).
   - He doesn't reconcile them. My reconciliation (inferred):
     - The six arrows name reader-facing moves (promise, develop, test, gather, echo, arrive), not content slots.
     - Writing the first and last lines first assumes you already know your position, which is the output of the thinking phase.
     - V43's title in the centre plays the same anchoring role as V42's question at the top of the page.
   - **For a skill:** use the six arrows once the writer knows their position (architect phase) or as a revision check, not as a starting outline.

8. **Open tension: AI.**
   - In V44 (20 July) he used AI to match people, to draft introduction emails through Gmail, and to turn a reader's gut-reaction voice note into bullet points (V44 12:30, 22:48 to 23:24). Three days later, V45 forbids AI at any stage of communicative writing and names "voice memo → AI cleans it up" as a harmful workflow (V45 7:52 to 8:47, 17:18).
   - The implicit distinctions:
     - The bullet list is for the reader's own private use, not for an audience.
     - Matching people is logistics.
     - The introduction emails are the hard case: they are messages to people, and he doesn't address this.
   - **For a skill:** follow V45's line. No AI-generated or AI-polished prose meant for readers. AI may help navigate notes, summarise privately, or handle logistics.

9. **What he has changed his mind about in this batch:**
   - discipline for creative work (V41)
   - sentence-length variety, active-only voice, jargon as fraud, outlining first, "write for yourself" (V42)
   - what a writing course is for: community over content, then partly back, since version two adds curriculum (V44)

   He also softens his slogan "writing is thinking" into "writing is at least how thinking becomes visible to you," with the thinking perhaps happening in the shower or asleep (V42 16:02). That fits V41's night shift: the unconscious does the work, and writing brings it to the surface.

10. **One value test, in several forms:**
    - Does it move the reader from where they are to where you want them? (V42 23:06)
    - Will they use it to understand the world or the writer? (V43 10:09)
    - Is there a vision you could keep elaborating if they talked back? (V45 11:22)
    - Did it produce an effect on a reader, and where exactly? (V44 23:37 to 24:38)

    All four put the reader's experience, not the writer's self-expression or the text's polish, at the centre of judgment. Only the freewriting of V41 is exempt, deliberately and temporarily.
