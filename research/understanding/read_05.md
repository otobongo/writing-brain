## V21: "This type of essay turns normal experience into valuable writing"

### 1. Who it's for, and the problem

It's for people who want to write personal or experience-based pieces online, on Substack and similar platforms, but hold back. They believe their life is too dull, or that experts have already covered their professional experience. A second, quieter problem is what happens when they do write: the pieces become diary entries that report what happened and give readers nothing beyond it [V21 0:00, 2:32].

### 2. Core argument

Creative nonfiction sits where real events meet the ambition of fiction. It is anchored in fact, but it aims past the facts at something deeper and more universal. That aim is what gives readers value. An ordinary experience becomes worth reading when you look at it closely enough to pull something out of it that holds beyond your own situation. He shows this with a close, annotated reading of David Gessner's essay "Learning to Surf". A plain story (a new father learns to surf and watches pelicans) becomes a meditation on stability, adaptation and being an animal. It gets there through a seed-like opening image, controlled word families, research folded into the story, and images planted early and called back. "My life is boring" is not a real barrier. The craft is in probing the ordinary, not in having a dramatic life [V21 2:32, 35:33].

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 The creative/nonfiction grid (his framing)
- **What it is.** Two axes, creative to non-creative and fiction to nonfiction, give four quadrants:
  - Creative fiction (literary, avant-garde, sci-fi) aims at innovation, and reaches a universal truth through invented stories.
  - Non-creative fiction (genre romance, detective, YA) reproduces patterns readers already know.
  - Non-creative nonfiction (journalism, academic reports, memos, manuals) is plain, factual, functional information transfer.
  - **Creative nonfiction** is anchored in reality but aims **beyond the facts** [V21 1:08–2:32].
- **Why it works.** Readers read only when they perceive value. A piece that stays at the level of "what happened" offers them nothing past the facts, so it doesn't get read. He calls such pieces **"glorified journal entries"** [V21 2:32–3:09].
- **How to apply.**
  1. Name the genre you're in. If it's creative nonfiction, write down in one sentence the deeper truth the events point to.
  2. Treat your real events as raw material for that truth, not as the subject.
  3. Check that the ending lands somewhere a stranger could use.
- **Symptoms in a draft.** A strict chronology of events. Feelings reported but never examined. An ending that only says how things turned out. A reader's honest reaction would be "OK, and?"
- **When it does NOT apply.** In non-creative nonfiction, such as memos, manuals and reports, the goal is plain information transfer, and aiming beyond the facts would be out of place [V21 1:30].
- **Illustration.**
  - Before: "Last year I switched from nursing to software. The bootcamp was hard, and I got a job in March."
  - After: "In the ICU, the monitors beeped when something was wrong. In my first codebase nothing beeped. I had to learn that the most dangerous errors are the silent ones, and that was the lesson both jobs had been trying to teach me."

#### 3.2 The opening image as seed (the "opening number")
- **What it is.** The first image or scene holds all the characters and all the main themes of the piece. Every later section expands something already in that germ, and nothing brand new appears later. He compares it to the opening number of a musical, where the whole cast is introduced [V21 4:20, 7:53–8:40]. In Gessner's opening, pelicans, surfers, the ocean and his daughter all appear. So do the themes of stability, relaxation, humans as animals, and a hint of evolution ("fitting like puzzle pieces").
- **Why it works.** The opening teaches the reader what to track. Coherence comes from each section developing something the reader has already met.
- **How to apply.**
  1. After drafting, list every character (people, animals, places, forces) and every theme.
  2. Rewrite the opening so each one appears, even as a hint.
  3. Check every later section against the list. If a section introduces something that isn't planted, either plant it or cut it.
- **Symptoms.** A key person or idea shows up for the first time halfway through. The opening is scene-setting that has nothing to do with where the piece ends up.
- **When it does NOT apply.** He presents this as what he admires in this essay and in creative nonfiction, not as a universal rule. For professional expository writing he gives different advice: lead with the point (V24).
- **Illustration.** For an essay about caring for an aging father: "Dad's reading glasses sit on the hall table next to the car keys he's no longer allowed to use. Both are still exactly where he left them." The glasses stand for his mind and the keys for his independence. The hall table, a shared house object, carries the theme of the house as a place of handover.

#### 3.3 Pointing the camera: selection is meaning (his image)
- **What it is.** Even pure reporting shows a theme through *which* features the writer notices and *which concept words* describe them. Gessner's opening simply reports the scene, but the details he picks and the words he uses (unperturbed, casual, supine, an exaggerated posture of relaxation, rising and falling together as the ocean heaves) already tell you the piece is about stability [V21 5:47–7:07].
- **Why it works.** Readers infer what matters to the writer from what the writer attends to. Selection is an argument the reader absorbs without being told.
- **How to apply.**
  1. Decide the theme.
  2. When you describe a real scene, keep only the details that bear on it.
  3. Choose descriptive words from the theme's vocabulary.
  4. Cut inventory details, even true ones.
- **Symptoms.** Descriptions read like lists (there was a table, a lamp, a window). Adjectives are generic ("nice", "big"). A reader could not guess the theme from the opening paragraph.
- **Illustration.** The theme is waiting.
  - Before: "The DMV had plastic chairs, a ticket machine and a TV."
  - After: "At the DMV the ticket machine had printed number 212. The screen said 147. A man near me had already folded his ticket into a tiny paper crane."

#### 3.4 Crossover linking, "the swap" (my label, his move)
- **What it is.** To make readers see two things as kin, describe each in the other's vocabulary. Gessner calls the surfers "strong animals" who feel "animal pleasure", and gives the pelicans "athletic feats" [V21 10:34–11:26].
- **Why it works.** It serves the writer's purpose, which is to get readers to identify humans with nature, without stating it as a claim. The reader makes the link themselves.
- **How to apply.**
  1. Name the two things your piece wants readers to see as related.
  2. Borrow one word or image from A's domain to describe B, and the reverse.
  3. Keep it subtle: a verb or an adjective, not a whole extended simile.
- **Symptoms.** The link between your two subjects is asserted ("like the birds, I too...") rather than enacted in the language.
- **Illustration.** An essay pairing a grandmother's garden with running a small business: "She thinned her carrots with the cold judgment of a CFO. My accountant, meanwhile, spoke of cash flow as if it were rainfall."

#### 3.5 Research inside the narrative: don't orphan facts
- **What it is.** Outside material (a quote, a term, a fact from reading) comes in rarely. Each time it does a specific job, and it always arrives *inside* the story's frame. Gessner watches the birds with his daughter, goes home and reads about them, and his books "confirm" what he saw. He never breaks from the narrative to lecture. He also positions himself as a newcomer getting to know his "neighbors", not as an encyclopedia [V21 9:57–11:26, 16:18, 18:02–19:47]. The examples he points to:
  - Funktionslust ("pleasure in doing what one does best") explains the surfers' obsession.
  - Thoreau's line that joy is the measure of right conduct justifies the father's joy in hard times.
  - The early ornithologists' insults ("grotesque", "lugubrious") set up the writer's defense of the birds.
- **Why it works.** The reader's attention stays on the story. Facts carry meaning because a character meets them at a particular moment. Quotes with a clear job advance the argument and don't interrupt it.
- **How to apply.**
  1. For each quote or fact, write in the margin what it does for the story. If you can't, cut it.
  2. Bring it in through a narrative action: I looked it up, a friend told me, I read that night.
  3. Return to the scene right after.
  4. Speak as someone learning, not someone lecturing.
- **Symptoms.** A paragraph that reads like a Wikipedia entry. Quotes dropped between scenes with no character present. A sudden change of voice from storyteller to expert.
- **When it does NOT apply.** He doesn't state a limit. Inferred: in non-creative nonfiction (reports, explainers) facts can stand on their own.
- **Illustration.**
  - Before: "Tomato plants release volatile compounds when damaged, which can warn neighboring plants."
  - After: "That evening, still annoyed about the hornworms, I read that a bitten tomato plant sends a chemical alarm to the plants beside it. I went back out with a flashlight. The whole row smelled sharp, like a room right after an argument."

#### 3.6 The web of signification: word families and contrast clusters
- **What it is.** Track the families of charged words that express a theme, and set them against an opposing family. In Gessner, stability words (graceful, symphonic, glide, relaxed, glorious) are set against weak-stability words (disorient, fall apart, shaky, unsettled). Keep images in the story's own environment: he's "lured" by a steady paycheck, "on the hook", which is ocean and fishing vocabulary. He calls this web of word choices a kind of web of signification [V21 11:55–12:14, 13:58–15:32].
- **Why it works.** Contrast between word families carries the emotional arc without the writer naming it. An image system from one environment makes the whole piece feel of a piece.
- **How to apply.**
  1. Circle every word in your draft that expresses the theme, in one color, and every word that expresses its opposite, in another.
  2. Check that each section leans toward one side, and that the balance shifts across the piece.
  3. Check where your metaphors come from. Replace any from outside the story's world.
- **Symptoms.** Mixed metaphors from unrelated domains. An emotional turn that is stated ("I felt unsettled") but not built in the diction. One word family appears everywhere at the same intensity.
- **Illustration.** An essay about grief set on a farm. Keep it in farm language, such as "the field I let lie fallow" and "grief going to seed", instead of borrowing "it hit me like a freight train".

#### 3.7 Artistic license, always to a purpose
- **What it is.** Creative nonfiction is not journalism. You may shape a real moment, as when Gessner has a credit agent say he has "weak stability", provided the shaping serves the piece. That phrase sets up a sharp contrast with the stability he admires in the pelicans [V21 12:38–13:58].
- **Why it works.** One shaped phrase can crystallize the tension the whole piece turns on.
- **How to apply.** If you rephrase what someone actually said, the new wording must sharpen a theme already in play. Never invent for decoration.
- **When it does NOT apply.** He grants the license because this "isn't journalism". Inferred: in reporting, or in any genre where readers expect verbatim accuracy, don't. He doesn't discuss where the ethical line falls.
- **Illustration.** The landlord really said the lease "might not be renewed." In an essay about impermanence, the writer renders it as the landlord calling the apartment "a short-term arrangement." Same fact, now tied to the theme.

#### 3.8 Function-labeling the sections (model-text annotation)
- **What it is.** Zoom out and give each section a label for what it does in the arc. His labels for Gessner are: opening scene, Weak Stability, Joy, Taste of Balance, Habituation, novelty fading into intimacy, and the culminating day. Every paragraph should carry a single message, and those messages should add up to a larger pattern [V21 15:32, 29:54–30:30].
- **Why it works.** Close reading shows the sentences. Labels show the movement: tension, relief, first success, retreat, culmination. That movement is what you need to copy.
- **How to apply.**
  1. Read an admired piece and label each section in one to three words.
  2. List the labels and read them as a storyboard.
  3. Do the same to your own draft. Merge sections with the same label, and cut or rework any you can't label.
- **Symptoms.** A section can't be labeled. Two sections do the same job. The labels don't progress.
- **Illustration.** Labels for an essay on learning to swim at 40: "Fear at the edge", "Humiliation in the kiddie lane", "First float", "Plateau", "Swimming with my daughter". The sequence shows a clear arc, with the plateau as the necessary dip.

#### 3.9 Plant and call back
- **What it is.** Introduce an image or concept early, and bring it back later with new meaning. Gessner does this with:
  - the relaxed girl on the surfboard, remembered when he finally rides a wave;
  - Funktionslust, which returns as *his own* pleasure as a writer who links human and animal lives;
  - "lusterless" eggs from the old naturalists, contradicted by the hatching eggs;
  - "rituals" and "habituation", which recur [V21 8:40–9:25, 22:33, 27:01, 33:46–34:19].
- **Why it works.** Callbacks reward attention, tie the thread together, and show change without the writer announcing it.
- **How to apply.**
  1. List everything you planted.
  2. Make sure each returns at least once, transformed.
  3. Cut plants that never pay off.
  4. Add plants for payoffs that currently come from nowhere.
- **Symptoms.** A striking early image that is never mentioned again. An ending that relies on an idea the reader has never met.
- **Illustration.** Opening: "I kept one mug after the divorce." Ending: "Yesterday I bought a second mug. Nobody's coming. I just wanted to be ready."

#### 3.10 Abstraction delivered through a concrete moment
- **What it is.** When the essay reaches a general truth, it doesn't state it in the abstract. "Joy comes from small achievements" arrives through the daughter learning to hold her bottle and his own small success on a wave [V21 27:01–28:33].
- **Why it works.** The reader feels the truth before reading it, so it lands as recognition rather than a lecture.
- **How to apply.** Find every sentence of general wisdom in your draft. Put a specific moment directly in front of it that makes it true. Then consider cutting the abstract sentence altogether.
- **Symptoms.** Lessons stated in the abstract ("I learned that patience matters") with no scene carrying them.
- **Illustration.**
  - Before: "Teaching taught me patience."
  - After: "On the third day, the boy who had refused to speak all week raised his hand to ask where the pencil sharpener was. I found myself grinning like he'd recited Shakespeare."

#### 3.11 The subject as a mirror
- **What it is.** The thing observed (the pelicans) becomes a mirror in which the writer shapes his understanding of himself. The essay seems to be about birds but is really about the writer adapting to a new life [V21 24:26–25:11].
- **How to apply.** Ask what your observed subject shows you about yourself. Let your understanding of the subject and your self-understanding change together.
- **Symptoms.** Long stretches of observation that never loop back to the narrator's situation.
- **Illustration.** A piece on restoring an old bicycle, in which each stripped, rusted part mirrors a habit the writer is trying to drop after burnout.

#### 3.12 Change the setting to mark the culmination
- **What it is.** Every scene in "Learning to Surf" happens on the same coast until the final section, when the writer goes to the pelicans' nesting islands. That first change of setting marks the climax [V21 32:03].
- **How to apply.** If your piece stays in one place, save the move to a new place for the moment everything comes together.
- **Illustration.** An essay set entirely in a hospital ward that ends, for the first time, in the patient's garden.

#### 3.13 Two time scales, and zooming from particular to expansive
- **What it is.** The essay runs on a local scale (ups and downs, birth and death, stability and instability) and an evolutionary scale (a great and epic continuation). It moves from the particulars to the wider view at chosen moments, such as when he begins talking about "rituals" [V21 22:33, 35:10–35:33].
- **How to apply.** Identify a larger frame your story belongs to: history, biology, a profession's long arc. Zoom out to it at a turning point, then come back to the scene.
- **Illustration.** A piece about one family's move to a new country zooms out to thousands of years of human migration, then back to the kitchen where the grandmother refuses to throw away her old pot.

#### 3.14 The response paper (class practice)
- **What it is.** In his seminar, each class focuses on one student's piece, and everyone writes that student a response paper. Students practise first on the Gessner essay [V21 3:09]. The format isn't described in detail here.

### 4. His stances
- **Against** navel-gazing and "glorified journal entries". Writing anchored in reality that doesn't aim beyond the facts gives readers nothing to value [V21 0:27, 2:32–3:09].
- **Against** facts or quotes that are "orphaned" from the unfolding story [V21 19:47].
- **For** artistic license, but only in service of a purpose [V21 13:58].
- **Against** the belief that you must have an interesting life or be a "real writer". You only need to look more closely at your own dramas and draw something transcendent out of them [V21 0:00, 35:33].
- **Against** pure self-expression. Ordinary experience is valuable provided you use writing to help readers see the world differently and solve problems [V21 35:33].
- He praises the narrator as a learner who researches his "neighbors", not an expert delivering facts [V21 18:02].
- Note: in this essay the thesis ("we are animals") is stated near the end [V21 34:19], which is the reverse of his point-first advice for professional nonfiction in V24.

### 5. People and works named
- David Gessner, "Learning to Surf" (the model essay).
- Richard Dawkins, *The Blind Watchmaker* (the association between "puzzle pieces" and evolution) [V21 9:25].
- Thoreau (joy as the measure of right conduct), as quoted inside the essay [V21 16:18].

## V22: "The novelist helping experts write well"

### 1. Who it's for, and the problem

It's for experts with valuable ideas who can't package them into clear writing. The example is W. Brian Arthur, an economist whose big idea (increasing returns) was "unreadable" on paper until he spent four days reworking every sentence with his friend Cormac McCarthy. The problem is that expertise is wasted if readers can't understand it [V22 0:00–1:34].

### 2. Core argument

Good writing is judged by the reader's experience, so the easier a text is to process, the better it is. Arthur and McCarthy's 1996 *Harvard Business Review* article, "Increasing Returns and the New World of Business", shows how to make a complex, abstract idea crystal clear and even fun. He walks through it:
- ruthless minimalism;
- an opening that builds a problem before offering the idea;
- a consistent subject in each paragraph;
- concrete stand-ins for every abstraction;
- one theme plus two or three points threaded through everything;
- one message per paragraph;
- an inviting, side-by-side tone suited to persuasion;
- stories that are summed up into named concepts, which then move through the piece like chess pieces;
- intuition pumps instead of plain examples;
- words chosen to signal value to this particular community.

He says the video offers 12 hacks but doesn't number them. The list below is my reconstruction [V22 0:00, 3:19].

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 McCarthy's question: "Do you need that?" (minimalism for clarity)
- **What it is.** For every punctuation mark, word, sentence, paragraph and section, ask whether you need it, and remove anything you don't [V22 2:24].
- **Why it works.** The reader decides what good writing is. Every extra element costs processing effort, and less effort means better writing. Even complex, abstract material can be carried in spare, sharp prose [V22 3:19].
- **How to apply.**
  1. Work from large to small: sections, then paragraphs, sentences, words, marks.
  2. For each unit, ask whether the theme or one of the key points needs it.
  3. Remove it if not. Rebuild sentences that need several internal marks into simpler ones.
- **Symptoms.** Long sentences with many clauses. Semicolons. Stacked qualifiers. Sections that are interesting but serve no main point.
- **When it does NOT apply.** V22 gives no limit. V23 adds a timing limit: cutting too early in drafting is harmful, so apply this in revision [V23 34:19].
- **Illustration.**
  - Before: "It is important to note that, in many cases, although not all, teams that adopt code review early tend, on the whole, to ship fewer defects."
  - After: "Teams that adopt code review early ship fewer defects."

#### 3.2 The punctuation X-ray (his image of an "X-ray" or "thumbprint")
- **What it is.** Strip a text down to its punctuation marks alone. What's left is a thumbprint of the style. Few marks between periods means simply built sentences. In the Arthur/McCarthy article the X-ray is strikingly spare [V22 1:34–3:19].
- **Why it works.** It makes sentence construction visible in a way that reading the words hides, so "write simply" becomes something you can check.
- **How to apply.**
  1. Delete every letter and digit, keeping spaces or line breaks at the periods. (A find-and-replace of `[A-Za-z0-9]` works.)
  2. Scan for dense clusters of commas, semicolons, dashes and parentheses between periods.
  3. Go back to those sentences and simplify them.
- **Symptoms.** Lines like `, , ; — ( ) , .` repeating across the X-ray.
- **When it does NOT apply.** It's a diagnostic, not a rule. He doesn't say every dense cluster is wrong.
- **Illustration.** The X-ray `, , ; , ( ) — .` versus `. . , . .` The first shows one sentence doing too much. The second shows short, simply built sentences.

#### 3.3 Problem construction: the five pieces (his framework)
- **What it is.** The idea is presented as the solution to a problem, built in five pieces [V22 5:23–11:17]:
  1. **Status quo.** What we currently believe ("our understanding ... was passed down to us").
  2. **Concession.** What that belief got right ("roughly valid" for its era). Don't come "off the top ropes" insulting people who hold it [V22 8:14].
  3. **Destabilizing condition.** The turn ("But steadily and continuously in this century...") that shows what's wrong.
  4. **Consequence.** What the change means for these readers' behavior and strategy.
  5. **Solution, or the new idea** (increasing returns).
- **Why it works.** Readers must perceive information as valuable before they'll read it. A common mistake is to pour out information that isn't aimed at anything readers care about. Framing the idea as the answer to a problem gives it value [V22 5:23]. The concession keeps readers from turning hostile.
- **Conceptual vs practical problems.** A **conceptual problem** tells readers their understanding is wrong or incomplete. Conceptual problems are **upstream** of practical ones: once the model changes, new practices follow downstream [V22 10:19–11:17].
- **How to apply.**
  1. State the belief your idea replaces, fairly and plainly.
  2. Grant what it got right.
  3. Write the "but" sentence: the change or anomaly that breaks it.
  4. Say what that means for *this* reader.
  5. Name your idea as the answer.
- **Symptoms.** The draft opens with background or definitions. The idea arrives with no problem attached. The existing view is mocked. There's no clear "but" moment. The consequences are generic rather than tied to the reader.
- **When it does NOT apply.** He doesn't limit it here. Inferred: narrative creative nonfiction opens with an image instead (V21).
- **Illustration.**
  1. Status quo: "Most companies still run the weekly status meeting, a habit from offices where the only way to learn what a colleague was doing was to ask them."
  2. Concession: "In that world the meeting earned its hour."
  3. Destabilizing condition: "But nearly all of that information now sits in shared trackers anyone can read at any time."
  4. Consequence: "So the meeting mostly repeats what people could have read, and it takes the hour from the people with the least time."
  5. Solution: "The meeting needs a new job: deciding, not reporting."

#### 3.4 Loaded value words in the status quo
- **What it is.** The first sentence of the article frames the old idea as dated without saying so. The words do it: "passed down to us" (like hand-me-down clothes), "more than a century ago", "a handful of European economists", "based squarely upon the assumption" (scientists distrust assumptions), "still thrives in textbooks". He colors these **value words** red [V22 6:18–8:14].
- **Why it works.** Readers, especially this audience, register the framing immediately. The old idea is weakened before the argument even begins.
- **How to apply.** When you describe the view you're replacing, choose words your readers associate with "outdated", "unexamined" or "partial". Keep them accurate and restrained. The concession still has to follow.
- **Symptoms.** The status quo is described in neutral language, so the "but" lands with no build-up. Or the opposite: it's described with open contempt.
- **Illustration.** "Our hiring playbook was inherited from an era of paper résumés and was built on the assumption that credentials predict performance." Here "inherited", "era of paper" and "assumption" do the work.

#### 3.5 Topic string: keep the camera on one subject
- **What it is.** Choose the entity each paragraph is about, and keep it in the subject position sentence after sentence. A synonym is fine if it's the same entity: "our understanding" can become "the theory". In the paragraph that defines increasing returns, every main clause has "increasing returns" (or "they") as its subject. Subordinate clauses add context without moving the camera [V22 11:17–13:04, 16:29–17:32].
- **Why it works.** The subject tells readers where to look. If a paragraph is a scene in a film, each sentence is a shot, and the writer is the director. Varying subjects at random is like watching a tennis ball go back and forth, or a chaotic film whose point you can't find [V22 12:10–13:04].
- **How to apply.**
  1. Underline the subject of each main clause in a paragraph.
  2. Decide what the paragraph is about.
  3. Rewrite so that thing, or a clear synonym for it, is the subject of most sentences.
  4. Move other information into subordinate clauses or objects.
- **Symptoms.** In unedited drafts, subjects drift: "The data... Researchers... It... This trend... Customers..." The reader can't say what the paragraph is about.
- **Illustration.**
  - Before: "Burnout is rising. Managers often miss it. The causes include long hours. Surveys show young staff are hit hardest."
  - After: "Burnout is rising, and it hits young staff hardest. It builds quietly out of long hours, so managers often miss it until someone quits."

#### 3.6 Concrete language: something readers "could stub their toe on" (his phrase)
- **What it is.** Ground every abstraction in something material:
  - "bulk processing" immediately becomes a **"smokestack economy"**;
  - a whole school of economic thought gets a stand-in character, **Alfred Marshall**, who "did this" and "said that";
  - "paradigms" become two **"worlds"** or regions [V22 13:48–15:28, 27:16].
- **Why it works.** His claim is that the brain's vision system is much older than its language system, so readers understand what they can see. The concrete image acts as set design, a backdrop against which abstract points make sense [V22 13:48–14:40, 15:28].
- **How to apply.**
  1. Circle abstract nouns (process, framework, dynamics, paradigm).
  2. For each, add a visible image, a person who embodies it, or a place.
  3. Reuse that concrete stand-in whenever the abstraction returns.
- **Symptoms.** Paragraphs with nothing you could draw. Terms like "dynamics" or "mechanisms" with no picture attached.
- **Illustration.**
  - Before: "Legacy approval processes create organizational friction."
  - After: "A purchase request for a $40 keyboard still passes three desks and waits a week in someone's inbox."

#### 3.7 One theme and two or three points: thread and needlework (McCarthy's advice)
- **What it is.** Decide the theme of the piece and the two or three points every reader must remember. They form the single thread through the piece, and the words, sentences and paragraphs are the needlework that stitches it together [V22 17:32–19:00].
- **Why it works.** Most writing advice focuses on sentences and paragraphs, but those are only tools for stitching a larger pattern. In the Arthur article, nothing could be cut because everything served the theme and its few strategy points.
- **How to apply.**
  1. Before revising, write the theme in a line and list the two or three points.
  2. Check each paragraph: which of these does it serve? Cut or move anything that serves none.
- **Symptoms.** You can't name what a reader should walk away with. The piece contains good tangents. There are more than three "key takeaways".
- **Illustration.** Theme: "Remote teams fail through silence, not distance." Points: (1) make decisions in writing; (2) give quiet people a written channel; (3) replace status meetings with async updates. A paragraph on home-office ergonomics goes, however good it is.

#### 3.8 One paragraph, one message: the label test
- **What it is.** A paragraph is a unit of one idea. In McCarthy's terms, it is limited to one message. You should be able to label every paragraph ("defines X", "mechanism of X", "Marshall's world"), and the list of labels, read alone, should hold together [V22 19:00–20:27].
- **Corollary.** A paragraph can be a single sentence when its job fits in one sentence. In the article, short paragraphs usually set up a transition. But he advises against the Substack habit of putting every sentence on a new line, which makes each sentence look like a new idea. Break paragraphs sparingly [V22 20:27–21:00].
- **How to apply.**
  1. Write a label in the margin for each paragraph.
  2. If a paragraph needs two labels, split it.
  3. If two paragraphs share a label, merge them.
  4. Read the labels alone and check the sequence makes sense.
- **Symptoms.** A paragraph that does two jobs. A run of one-line paragraphs that are really one idea.
- **Illustration.** Labels for an explainer on sleep debt: "what sleep debt is", "how it accumulates", "why weekends don't repay it", "what does". Each label maps to one paragraph.

#### 3.9 Inviting persuasion: questions, "let's look", "notice", "I" and "we"
- **What it is.** Many paragraphs open with a question ("What would happen if...?"). The writer uses first person ("They surprised me when I first perceived them"). He invites the reader to look alongside him ("Let's look at the market for operating systems", "Notice several things"). It echoes Prufrock's "Let us go then, you and I" [V22 20:49–23:32, 26:27, 32:40].
- **Why it works.** The article's job is to persuade readers that an old idea is wrong. The traditional argumentative style puts readers "on their back foot". Invitation puts reader and writer on equal footing, the "same side of the table", looking together at a world that "led us astray". It doesn't badger the reader [V22 22:29–23:32, 26:27].
- **How to apply.**
  1. Where you'd write "X is wrong", write a question the reader might ask, then "let's look".
  2. Use "we" for the shared mistake and "notice" for the evidence.
  3. Let one personal note of surprise show that you once believed it too.
- **Symptoms.** A lecturing or adversarial tone ("Readers must understand..."). Pronouns that point at the reader's error ("you are wrong to think").
- **When it does NOT apply.** He grants that the "no I, no you" rule of formal publications exists for a reason, and that rhetorical questions can be misused. He doesn't list the cases, though [V22 21:41–22:29]. Inferred: questions work when they're the question the reader is already asking.
- **Illustration.**
  - Before: "Managers wrongly believe open-plan offices increase collaboration."
  - After: "Why did we think knocking down walls would get people talking? Let's look at what happened when one firm tried it. Notice where people went first: to the phone booths."

#### 3.10 Story, then principles, then named concepts, then redeploy ("chunking"; concepts as chess pieces)
- **What it is.** A four-step sequence [V22 23:32–28:15]:
  1. Ask a question.
  2. Tell a familiar story, chosen because *these* readers know it (Apple, DOS and IBM for 1996 HBR readers).
  3. Sum up the story's lessons in one sentence each ("not predictable in advance", "the dominant system was not the best").
  4. Package each lesson as a named concept (market instability, multiple potential outcomes, possible predominance of an inferior product).

  After that, the concepts move through the rest of the piece like chess pieces. Every time the name recurs, it links back to the story.
- **Why it works.** Chunking. The named concept compresses the story into something readers can carry. Repeating it gives the piece coherence.
- **How to apply.**
  1. Pick an example your readers already know.
  2. Tell it briefly.
  3. Write one sentence per lesson.
  4. Give each lesson a short, stable name.
  5. Reuse exactly those names later, without swapping in synonyms.
- **Symptoms.** Examples that are told but never summed up. Concepts introduced in the abstract with no anchoring story. Names that drift ("lock-in", then "entrenchment", then "stickiness").
- **Illustration.**
  1. Story: a town's two bakeries, where the one that opened 30 minutes earlier took the commuter crowd, even with worse bread.
  2. Lessons: timing beat quality; habits formed in the first week; the crowd stayed.
  3. Concepts: "first-hour advantage", "habit lock".
  4. Later sections: "Your onboarding email is your first hour", "Where does habit lock form in your app?"

#### 3.11 The intuition pump (his term; better than an example)
- **What it is.** A plain example supplies evidence. An **intuition pump** puts the reader in the detective's seat. It's a scenario or image readers can imagine and reason through for themselves, so they see what the writer wants them to see. In Arthur's article:
  - an aircraft's safe arrival depends on the captain, not the cabin crew, so hierarchy still fits the bulk-processing world;
  - the old world is like a modern factory and the new one like a casino, not poker, where choosing *which game to play* is part of the skill [V22 28:15–30:25].
- **Why it works.** Readers reach the conclusion themselves instead of being told it. It links the abstract point to a situation they can think through.
- **How to apply.**
  1. State the question ("Does X still apply here?").
  2. Find a familiar situation with the same structure.
  3. Describe it vividly enough that readers can run it in their heads.
  4. State the implication for your subject.
  5. Where it helps, extend the metaphor with a contrast ("not A but B").
- **Symptoms.** Abstract assertions where readers can only take your word for it. Examples that illustrate but don't let the reader reason.
- **Illustration.** Question: "Does a three-person startup need a style guide?" Pump: "Picture a family cooking dinner together. Nobody writes down who chops the onions, and the meal still arrives. Now picture a restaurant kitchen serving 200 covers. Without the ticket rail, someone gets raw chicken." Implication: rules pay off at scale, not at the family table.

#### 3.12 Community code words (the anthropologist exercise)
- **What it is.** Some **value signals** are universal ("mistake", and the transition "but"). Every community also has its own in-group code words that make its readers sit up. For HBR readers, "strategy" and "it pays to..." hit, as he puts it, a *ka-ching* in the back of the mind [V22 30:25–32:22].
- **Why it works.** The same idea feels valuable or irrelevant depending on whether it's phrased in the reader's value vocabulary.
- **How to apply.**
  1. Choose four or five pieces that are popular and widely shared among your readers.
  2. Read them like an anthropologist, looking for the words that signal value in this group.
  3. Keep a list.
  4. Phrase your problem and payoff in those words.
- **Symptoms.** Your value claims are in your own field's vocabulary, not the readers'. Your readers' key words never appear.
- **When it does NOT apply.** Inferred: don't import code words you don't understand. The point is to understand your reader.
- **Illustration.** For hospital nurses, "charting burden", "handoff" and "safe staffing" signal value. For indie game developers, "wishlists", "scope creep" and "crunch" do. The same productivity tip should be framed in each group's words.

#### 3.13 Pre-empting "this isn't new"
- **What it is.** Arthur notes that the economist Hicks warned about increasing returns in 1939 and "had it wrong": the new idea doesn't destroy the standard theory, it complements it. This heads off the reader who thinks the idea is old news [V22 26:27–27:16].
- **Why it works.** It shows value (this is a real advance) and a fair stance (it complements, it doesn't demolish).
- **How to apply.** Name the nearest prior idea your readers might know. Say exactly where it fell short. Position your idea as extending it, not wiping it out.
- **Illustration.** "Yes, 'eat the frog' already told us to do hard tasks first. What it missed is that the hardest task isn't always the most important one."

#### 3.14 The intro previews the structure
- **What it is.** The last sentences of the opening paragraph open out into the article's main parts, what increasing returns *are* and the *mechanisms* behind them. The whole structure is visible in miniature in the first paragraph [V22 32:40–34:30].
- **How to apply.** Make the last sentence or two of your introduction name the parts in the order your sections will take them.
- **Illustration.** "...That shift changes both *what* a good hire looks like and *how* you find one." The sections that follow take "what" first, then "how".

### 4. His stances
- Expertise that can't be packaged clearly might as well not exist [V22 0:00].
- **The reader decides** what good writing is. Good writing is a function of the reader's experience [V22 3:19].
- **Against** dumping information that isn't aimed at a problem readers care about [V22 5:23].
- **Against** attacking the existing view head-on. Concede first [V22 8:14].
- **Against** writing advice that focuses only on sentences and paragraphs. The theme is the thing [V22 18:30].
- **Against** the internet habit of putting each sentence on a new line [V22 20:27–21:00].
- **Nuanced** on the formal-register rule against "I" and "you". There's a reason for it, but inviting personal language is very effective for persuasion [V22 21:41–23:32].
- **Rhetorical questions** can be misused. These ones work because they bring the reader into conversation [V22 21:41].
- Writing should be **fun**, and a good piece takes readers on a journey after which they see the world anew. A lot of would-be writers hate writing because of the process, but the process "isn't one process, it's two" (a teaser for another video) [V22 34:30].
- In V22 he frames the Arthur article's ultimate function as **persuasion** [V22 22:52]. V25 contradicts this for essays (see the cross-video section).

## V23: "give me 43 min, I'll DELETE your writer's block"

### 1. Who it's for, and the problem

It's for aspiring and working writers, especially indie and online writers building newsletters, who say they want to write but don't get it done. It covers those who quit after a few months of writing in public, and those who hide in research, revision or "business" tasks. The problem, in his words, is writer's block or "resistance", which he renames **fear** [V23 0:00, 2:36].

### 2. Core argument

Nobody fears emails or product descriptions. Fear only appears around writing that tries to connect, change minds or solve problems, which is exactly the writing worth doing. So the goal is not to eliminate fear but to live with it, build a structure that keeps you writing despite it, and use it as a signal of where the valuable material is. Practically, he says:
- invent the person who writes;
- protect a small, consistent daily habit for about two months;
- aim low and forgive misses;
- have concrete moves for when you're stuck;
- catch virtuous procrastination and perfectionism;
- keep enough fear and uncertainty alive that writing stays a discovery;
- cut late, not early;
- treat revision and research as part of writing.

He insists this is his own professional practice, not training wheels [V23 0:00, 30:00–31:54, 40:48].

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 Name the block as fear
- **What it is.** Rename writer's block or "resistance" as fear. Notice when it shows up: never with emails, texts or copywriting, only with writing meant to make readers think differently, solve a problem or feel less alone [V23 0:00, 1:47–2:36].
- **Why it works.** The whole genre of writing routines and tactics exists to manage one problem, fear. Naming it tells you what you're actually managing, and shows that the fear comes with ambition, not incompetence [V23 0:52–1:47].
- **How to apply.** When you avoid writing, ask whether the piece is one that matters. If it is, the reluctance is fear of judgment, and the moves below apply.
- **Symptoms (in the writer).** Scrolling instead of writing. An inner voice saying nobody cares what you think. Wanting to go back to consuming other people's work [V23 2:36].
- **Illustration.** "I've rewritten the opening of my newsletter six times" is usually not a craft problem. The piece argues something your colleagues might dispute.

#### 3.2 Invent the person who writes the book (from Sheila Heti)
- **What it is.** Stop focusing only on the project. Design the self who can finish it: what kind of person, with what habits, and what tricks they play on themselves [V23 5:13–6:00].
- **Why it works.** Rules like "I'll write when nothing more important comes up" always fail, because something more important always comes up, just as a smoker who'll quit "as long as nothing stressful happens" never quits. The only rule that works is unconditional, and it belongs to an identity [V23 3:26–5:13].
- **How to apply.** Write down the habits of the person who could finish this piece. Treat the next two months as the time you build that person.
- **Symptoms.** Writing that depends on inspiration or leftover time. His Faulkner joke makes the point: "inspired" at 9 a.m. every morning [V23 3:26].

#### 3.3 Protect the seedling: about two months of deliberate discipline
- **What it is.** Plan for at least two months of serious, ruthless self-discipline, roughly how long a habit takes to form. He notes that estimates range from six weeks to three months. Shield the new habit, and the new self, like a seedling against deer and invasive species [V23 6:00–6:52].
- **How to apply.** Set the pattern, put it in your calendar for eight weeks, and decide ahead of time what you'll say no to.

#### 3.4 Write in the morning (his preference)
- **What it is.** Do your writing session early [V23 6:52–7:38].
- **Why it works.** Willpower is like a muscle that tires through the day, so spend the full tank on the most important task. Finished writing also turns the rest of the day into a "victory lap". An unwritten piece hangs over your evening.
- **When it does NOT apply.** He says it's what works for him, and others may have different strategies.

#### 3.5 Session length and pattern: consistency over any specific timing
- **What it is.** Thirty minutes a day is fantastic if you're busy, and an hour on good days is great. In busy teaching periods he writes three 90-minute sessions between classes. Options include the Pomodoro method (25 minutes on, 5 off) and a chess clock that gamifies focus: one side for 4 hours of writing, the other for 4.5 hours of breaks, and the goal is to run down the writing side first [V23 7:38–9:17].
- **Why it works.** The exact timing matters less than having a consistent pattern.
- **When it does NOT apply.** He used the chess clock for a few months over a holiday and isn't using it much now. Pick whatever fits your life.

#### 3.6 Announce the habit, not the project
- **What it is.** When you tell people or post publicly, say "I'm writing 90 minutes a day", not "I'm writing a book" [V23 9:17–10:07].
- **Why it works.** He has heard, and hedges it, that the brain half-confuses announcing a goal with achieving it, which drains motivation. Announcing a grand project also makes it feel bigger than it is. Narrating your *process* to others makes it real, independent of your mood. An accountability partner who checks in helps.
- **When it does NOT apply.** Grand announcements of 90-day goals have fizzled for him, so avoid them.
- **Illustration.** Not "My novel will be done by June!" but "I write from 7:00 to 7:45 every weekday. Ask me how it's going on Fridays."

#### 3.7 Output target: roughly two pages, or just complete the session (the Seinfeld rule)
- **What it is.** He used to obsess over word counts and dropped that. He now aims for about two pages a day, or simply for completing the session. In a Jerry Seinfeld-style rule, during the reserved time you don't have to write, but you can't do anything else. Revising what you've already written counts [V23 10:07–11:49].
- **Why it works.** Undistracted time with nothing else to do eventually produces writing. And writing is recursive, so revision is legitimate work.
- **How to apply.** Set the timer, close everything else, and allow yourself to sit, stare, reread and revise. You can't switch to another app.

#### 3.8 The arithmetic: break the target down
- **What it is.**
  - About 1,000 words, roughly two pages, a day is four 25-minute sessions of 250 words each.
  - That's 10 words a minute, about one sentence a minute.
  - At two pages a day, a book draft takes about four to five months [V23 11:49–12:41].
- **Why it works.** A daunting target becomes one sentence a minute, which feels manageable.

#### 3.9 Know when you're allowed to stop, and write in one place
- **What it is.** A fixed daily target tells you when you're *done*, which ends the guilt of feeling you should always be writing more. A dedicated writing place draws the same boundary. When you're not there, you're free to live [V23 12:41–13:37].

#### 3.10 A non-negotiable container, but not an extreme one
- **What it is.** Victor Hugo had his servants lock him in until he slid the day's pages under the door. The principle is that the goal isn't negotiable. The method is too extreme to copy [V23 13:37].
- **When it does NOT apply.** He warns against self-destructive regimes like bricking your phone or locking yourself in a room [V23 18:04].

#### 3.11 Aim low, and become a goal-meeting person
- **What it is.** Set small, easily met goals: 15 focused minutes, one sentence a day. Your job is not to become a writing machine like Stephen King. It's to become a person who meets goals. In James Clear's gym story, a man went to the gym, changed, and went home, just to become a gym-goer. Small wins compound [V23 13:37–16:20].
- **Why it works.** Meeting goals creates a loop of self-motivation and reward, and unmet goals don't build habits [V23 13:37, 15:18].
- **Symptoms.** Planning 2,000 words a day or four hours straight, then missing and quitting.
- **Illustration.** Week one: write one paragraph a day before opening email. Week two: two paragraphs.

#### 3.12 The missed-goal protocol
- **What it is.** Missing now and then doesn't hurt the habit. Making a big deal of it does [V23 16:20–19:52]. The protocol:
  1. **Forgive yourself immediately.** You don't abandon a diet because of one burger.
  2. **Look forward.** Focus on meeting today's goal.
  3. **Never add today's gap to tomorrow.** If you wrote 300 of 500 words, tomorrow is still 500, not 700. Carried debts spiral into imagined 3,000-word catch-up days.
  4. **Celebrate hits.** Take a day off, or have an untracked "bonus day" of writing for joy. Some of his best work came from those.
  5. **Go easy on yourself.** Drop the "deranged drill sergeant". Treat the part of you that wants to write as someone you care about.
- **Why it works.** For the habit to last it has to be fun, and the point of the habit is to discover the pleasure of writing. Painters and sculptors don't talk about the torment of playing with paint [V23 18:04–19:52].

#### 3.13 Stuck mid-session: the non-stop freewrite in a new document
- **What it is.** Open a new document and type without stopping for 5 to 10 minutes. Start with whatever is in your head, for example "I'm stuck on this piece because I can't figure out how to...". Repeat yourself if you must, but write real sentences, not keyboard-mashing. It doesn't matter what you write, as long as you don't stop [V23 19:52–22:22].
- **Why it works.** The rambling usually drifts into focused writing and leads you back into the real project. It replaces the urge to escape (his is a spoonful of peanut butter in the kitchen). He uses the same drill to build fluency in EFL students who have high knowledge and low fluency, and their fluency rises quickly. He says it breaks him through about 9 times out of 10.
- **How to apply.** When your legs start lifting you out of the chair, stay 5 to 10 more minutes, in a separate file, typing.
- **Illustration.** "I'm stuck because I don't know whether section 3 comes before the case study or after. If it comes after, the reader already knows the result, which is sort of the point... actually the case study *is* the argument, so section 3 is just its setup..."

#### 3.14 Lost faith in the whole project: reread as a reader, then outline what you actually have
- **What it is.** For big-picture doubt ("this is garbage, the premise is flawed"):
  1. Reread everything you've written slowly, line by line, as if meeting it for the first time. Follow the thought process of the person who wrote it.
  2. Then write an outline of what's actually there, not what you planned. Describe the logic you see.
  3. From it you'll see the holes, the misplaced parts, and where it went sideways [V23 22:22–24:09].
- **Why it works.** You've been experiencing the text as a writer. Rereading it as a reader changes your view. Often it's better than you thought, or the fault is plain. Macro problems can't be fixed at the sentence level. You need to see the structure, the larger units of discourse, to fix them [V23 23:19–24:09].
- **Symptoms.** A feeling that the whole thing isn't working, combined with fiddling with sentences.
- **Illustration.** An outline of what you actually have reads: "1. anecdote about client; 2. definition of churn; 3. anecdote about a different client; 4. three tips; 5. back to the definition." The fault is visible at once. The definition is split, and the second anecdote is doing the tips' work.

#### 3.15 Talk it through with one person, and show struggling work
- **What it is.** If both moves above fail, talk it through with someone whose judgment you trust, ideally one-on-one. Writing groups larger than three or four people tend to give feedback that's too polite and vague. Share work *while* it's struggling, not only when you're proud of it. Showing only good work is like going to the doctor only when you're healthy [V23 24:09–25:45].
- **Symptoms (as he sees them in consultations).** A writer starts avoiding certain parts of the feedback because they feel guilty that the work isn't going well [V23 24:09].

#### 3.16 Spot virtuous procrastination (his term)
- **What it is.** Writers have moved past Netflix to "advanced" avoidance that *feels* productive: cleaning the house, organizing the desk, admin, and, for indie writers, marketing, SEO, three hours on an Instagram strategy, "catching up on the news". The sneakiest kind is work that feels important, because it has deadlines and clear outcomes, unlike writing's uncertain toil [V23 25:45–27:37].
- **Why it works.** If writing frightens you, cleaning feels like a vacation. You may even have made the mess unconsciously to avoid the desk.
- **How to apply.** Do a decent job on the business side, but remember what you want to be known for: your ideas and your voice. Those only come from writing.
- **Test.** Is this task moving the current piece forward, or making me feel busy?

#### 3.17 The perfectionism loop: "it's not your magnum opus, it's your next work"
- **What it is.** The opposite failure: endless research and revision to avoid judgment. While more research is possible, the piece stays in "perfect potential". His advice is to let it go. Being evaluated teaches more than another month of polishing [V23 27:37–29:19]. More extensions from university are, in his image, like giving a drunk a drink.
- **How to apply.** Set a release date. Treat publication as the next lesson, not a verdict.
- **Catch yourself sooner.** He still procrastinates, by reading too much and letting admin eat his time. The difference is that he notices faster. Say your excuses out loud to a colleague ("I can't publish until I redesign my website"), because out loud they sound ridiculous [V23 28:26–30:00].

#### 3.18 Fear as a metal detector: "What am I most afraid of here?"
- **What it is.** Following a Buddhist nun (Pema Chödrön), treat fear as a sign that you're getting closer to the truth. When stuck, ask what you're most afraid of, what scares you about this paragraph, and why you're stuck. Then write about that fear and follow its thread. The fear is the "beep beep" of a beachcomber's metal detector [V23 30:00–32:41].
- **Why it works.** Writing that aims at greatness, which he defines as producing new knowledge, a surprising discovery for the writer delivered as a gift to the reader, is always frightening. Fearless writing is complacent writing, and supremely confident writers have nothing to teach [V23 30:00–31:54].
- **How to apply.** In a freewrite, answer the three questions honestly. The paragraph you've been avoiding usually hides the most interesting problem in the piece.
- **Illustration.** "I'm afraid this paragraph admits that the method I've taught for five years failed with my best client." That admission is probably the heart of the essay.

#### 3.19 Keep enough fear alive: don't know your ending, and don't hoard your best idea
- **What it is.** If writing is thinking, then writing when you already know your conclusion involves no discovery, and the idea is "dead on arrival". When a great ending idea turns up mid-draft, write it down right there instead of saving it for the finish. That forces you to find something even better for the ending. He compares it to Wile E. Coyote building the bridge just ahead of his feet [V23 32:41–34:19].
- **Why it works.** Keeps the writing open to learning. And your best idea shouldn't sit buried in the middle, so its placement pushes you to think further.
- **When it does NOT apply.** He calls it perhaps just a personal preference [V23 32:41]. Inferred: this is a drafting (thinking) move. At the editing stage, V24's advice to put the main point at the end of the introduction governs where the point ends up.

#### 3.20 Concision comes later ("writing anorexia", his phrase)
- **What it is.** "Cut ruthlessly" isn't wrong as a point of style, but you can't worry about concision when you've just started. You can only afford to cut when you're rich. Cutting 10 pages from a 20-page draft is a disaster, and cutting 50 from 200 is nothing. Pruning early slows you down and may throw away the wordy paragraph that holds your next big idea. Done too early, it becomes self-punishment disguised as virtue, a kind of **writing anorexia** [V23 34:19–35:22].
- **Symptoms (in process).** Deleting sentences as you type. A thin draft with nothing to choose from.

#### 3.21 The artist and the architect (his framing)
- **What it is.** Shift between two modes. The **artist** is a channel for ideas: give them life and space, and stay out of their way. The **architect** steps back to see how everything hangs together and which pages don't fit the final shape [V23 35:22–36:18].
- **Why it works.** You have to write without knowing exactly how things will turn out. Once the architect arrives there's enough material to be generous with cuts.

#### 3.22 Revision is writing: the recursive loop
- **What it is.** He calls the advice to separate writing from revision (write first, fix later) "completely backwards" [V23 36:18]. His loop:
  1. Pause after each section or paragraph and reread it.
  2. See what you've actually done, and start mapping the shape of the whole.
  3. Hunt for the strongest thoughts. His drafts are uncensored, so they hold several ideas, some connected and some orphaned.
  4. Build the structure around the strongest ideas, and cut or save the weaker ones.
  5. The emerging structure shows where the next sections need to go [V23 36:18–38:09].
- **Why it works.** Revision creates the text as much as first sentences do. School teaches revision as fixing marked errors on a static, finished text at a separate moment. Real revision is dynamic: it's where you discover what you're trying to say [V23 37:13–38:09].
- **Symptoms.** Treating revision as grammar cleanup. Refusing to reread until a full draft exists.
- **Illustration.** After drafting three paragraphs on "why meetings fail", you reread them and notice that the orphan sentence "people bring status, not questions" is the real idea. Sections 2 and 3 now reorganize around questions versus status.

#### 3.23 Research counts as writing, if it serves the piece
- **What it is.** He used to separate research days from writing days and now finds that artificial. Browsing, reading and note-taking can be moments of discovery, so count them toward your writing goals. But research is also a classic virtuous procrastination [V23 38:09–39:56].
- **Test.** Reading to understand something specific the piece needs is writing. Reading to avoid the feelings of the blank page is avoidance.
- **Why it works.** Writing, research and revision are all part of one process of generating ideas.

#### 3.24 Start with one thing for a week
- **What it is.** Most people nod and try nothing. His advice is to pick one practice, commit to it for a week, then add another. It gets easier, not easy [V23 40:48–42:48].

### 4. His stances

## V24: "I'm an Editor, these are the 7 First-Draft Mistakes I Fix All the Time"

### 1. Who it's for, and the problem

It's for nonfiction writers (essays, op-eds, professional writing) who want to edit their own first drafts instead of paying for a consultation. It suits especially those who wrote something they were proud of that underperformed. The problem is that writers can't see their own draft's failures by rereading it, and the failures that matter are rarely grammatical [V24 0:00, 1:40].

### 2. Core argument

Rereading your own draft fails because your words trigger your memory of the idea. That is association, not communication. To get ideas off the page and into a reader's mind, you need to know *consciously* what readers expect *intuitively*. He gives a seven-step, top-down diagnostic, the one he uses with clients:
1. know the reader;
2. lead with the point;
3. frame it as the solution to the reader's problem;
4. build coherence with key terms;
5. put the characters readers care about in the subject position;
6. make logical connections explicit, with actions as verbs;
7. only then, fix mechanics.

Most professional writing fails because it serves the writer instead of the reader [V24 0:52–1:40, 22:56].

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.0 The coffee stain problem (his term): why self-editing by feel fails
- **What it is.** Picture yourself knocking coffee onto your draft. From then on the stain reminds *you* of that moment, but it means nothing to a reader. Your sentences work the same way. You wrote them to express an idea already in your head, so rereading them brings the idea back to you. That's association, not communication [V24 0:52–1:40].
- **Why it matters.** You can't use the "does it read well to me?" test on your own text. You need explicit knowledge of reader expectations. That's the job of the checklist.
- **How to apply.** When editing, don't ask "does this feel clear?" Run each of the seven checks as an explicit test.
- **Illustration.** You wrote "the Q3 problem" and it feels perfectly clear, because you remember the whole incident. Your reader has never heard of it.

#### 3.0b You can't judge a sentence without its reader
- **What it is.** A sentence can't be judged good or bad by its content alone. His example is someone telling a friend whose pet just died that they can always get another pet: the words aren't wrong, the situation is. Language is always social, but writing is done alone, which is a strange, asocial use of a social tool [V24 1:40–3:01].
- **Why it matters.** It explains why step 1 comes first.

#### Step 1. Know your reader, not just your audience
- **What it is.** Don't stop at "who is my audience?" Ask what they want and need, using his four questions [V24 3:01–3:22]:
  1. What motivated them to click the headline or pick up the piece?
  2. What problem are they dealing with?
  3. What answer are they looking for?
  4. What decision are they trying to make?
- **Why it works.** You can't decide whether a text succeeds without knowing what it responds to. He won't even look at a client's text until these questions are answered [V24 3:22].
- **How to apply.** Write the four answers at the top of the draft before editing anything, then judge every later choice against them.
- **Symptoms.** You can name a demographic ("marketers", "parents") but not a decision they're facing. The draft could be addressed to anyone.
- **Illustration.** Audience: "HR managers". Reader: an HR manager at a 50-person firm who clicked because a key hire just quit. Their problem is replacing her without overpaying. They want to know whether to use a recruiter. The decision is due this week.

#### Step 2. Lead with the main point: writing patterns vs reading patterns (his terms)
- **What it is.** Writers use a text to *solve a problem*. They start with a question, work through the evidence and stop when they reach resolution, so the best idea comes at the end. Readers use **reading patterns**: they need the destination first so they can decide whether to invest. The mismatch causes **interference**. Readers have to dig for the ideas, or they quit. His image is giving a friend directions to a restaurant by describing *your* route from *your* apartment [V24 4:18–7:28].
- **Why it works.** Reading is an investment of time. Knowing the point up front sets the right expectations. Even skimmers and speed-readers get the message [V24 5:17, 7:28].
- **How to apply.**
  1. Find the one sentence you need readers to **accept, act on, or use**. It's usually at the end of the draft, where you discovered it, or buried mid-paragraph.
  2. Move it to the **end of the introduction**, not the conclusion.
  3. At smaller scales, open paragraphs with the claim and follow with the reasons.
- **Symptoms.** Paragraphs that open with "After reviewing...", "Having considered...", "In examining...". The recommendation appears in the last sentence. A reader who stops after the introduction can't say what you think.
- **When it does NOT apply.** He frames this for professional writing [V24 7:28]. Inferred from V21: narrative creative nonfiction may let the thesis emerge at the end.
- **Illustration.**
  - Before: "We surveyed 40 customers, reviewed support tickets, compared churn by plan, and interviewed the sales team. Based on this, we think the free tier should be capped at 3 projects."
  - After: "Cap the free tier at 3 projects. Customers who hit a limit upgrade at twice the rate of those who don't, the heaviest free users generate most support tickets, and sales says the cap won't hurt new sign-ups."

#### Step 3. Frame the idea as the solution to the reader's problem (motivation)
- **What it is.** Readers don't care about your ideas, they care about their problems. Giving information they didn't ask for makes them think "why are you telling me this?" [V24 8:15]. His squirrel op-ed shows three openings:
  - one that ignores the reader's resistance;
  - one that adds information that doesn't touch the resistance;
  - one that names the specific belief blocking the proposal and dismantles it. Only this one works [V24 8:15–10:27].
- **How to apply.**
  1. **Start with a problem readers recognize, not with background.**
  2. Diagnose which of three reader types you face [V24 10:50–12:20]:
     - **Uninformed readers** don't know about the problem. Give enough background for them to see it and its stakes.
     - **Indifferent readers** know but don't care. Establish the consequences of not solving it, made directly relevant to them.
     - **Resistant readers** know the problem and dislike your solution. Speak their language: acknowledge their position, then show that it's less stable than they think, so your idea looks like what makes their position more secure.
  3. For resistant readers, find the *belief* that stands between them and your solution. Your "however" sentence must target that belief, not a side fact.
- **Symptoms.** Opening with background or history. A "however" followed by a fact that doesn't contradict what the reader believes. A reader who could say "so what?" and still reject you.
- **Illustration.** A proposal to switch a team from Slack to email for decisions, aimed at resistant readers who love Slack because it's fast.
  - Weak: "Everyone loves Slack. However, we're moving decisions to email."
  - Also weak: "Everyone loves Slack. However, we send 4,000 messages a week." (So what? Fast is still fast.)
  - Better: "We use Slack because it makes decisions fast. But last quarter, three decisions were made twice because nobody could find the first thread."

#### Step 4. Coherence through key terms, not variation
- **What it is.** Ignore the advice to avoid repetition and vary your words. Variation confuses readers, either by bringing too many things on stage or by giving one concept several names. Think of a scene in a play or musical. It has one clear main character, and secondary characters come on to draw out aspects of that main character through comparison and contrast. Too many characters and you can follow each line but can't say what the scene is about [V24 12:20–14:39].
- **Why it works.** Most professional writing fails because it's about too many things at once [V24 14:39].
- **How to apply.**
  1. Pick a section or paragraph.
  2. Identify the major key terms readers need to accept.
  3. Track them from beginning to end, using the same words or closely related synonyms [V24 14:39–15:13].
- **Symptoms.** One concept under three names ("users", "customers", "clients", "members"). Many new nouns arriving in one paragraph. A reader can understand each sentence but can't say what the passage is about.
- **Illustration.**
  - Before: "Our retention strategy... keeping subscribers... loyalty initiatives... reducing attrition... customer stickiness."
  - After: "Retention" throughout, with "churn" introduced once as its explicit opposite.

#### Step 5. Characters in the subject position: the spotlight
- **What it is.** Readers describe unclear writing as dull, passive, unclear, indirect or abstract. Those words report how readers *feel*, not what's wrong. The underlying cause is what sits in the subject position. The subject is the **spotlight** that picks out the main character of the scene. Neither the active nor the passive version of a sentence is better in the abstract. It depends on what you and your reader care about. Put the people, companies, concepts or ideas your readers care about in the subject position, and keep it consistent across sentences so it becomes a point of view [V24 15:13–18:11].
- **Why it works.** When the subject stays consistent, the reader has one steady place to look. When it keeps changing, the spotlight wanders. Grounding subjects in characters is what readers experience as vigorous, direct and concrete writing [V24 15:50, 17:33].
- **How to apply.**
  1. Underline each sentence's grammatical subject.
  2. Ask who or what the reader cares about here.
  3. Rewrite so that character is the subject, using the passive where that's what it takes.
  4. Hold it steady as long as the passage needs.
- **Symptoms.** Subjects that are abstractions nobody cares about ("consideration", "implementation"). Subjects that change every sentence.
- **When it does NOT apply.** He rejects "active is always better". The passive is correct when the thing acted on is what the reader cares about [V24 16:49].
- **Illustration.** A piece about patients: "Patients were discharged too early" keeps the spotlight on patients, which is right here. "The hospital discharged patients too early" is better in a piece about hospital policy.

#### Step 6. Make logical connections explicit, with actions as verbs (Grice's maxims)
- **What it is.** When you put two items side by side, readers *assume* a logical relationship between them. His illustration is a Jerry Stiller joke whose punchline depends on the listener inventing a link between two unrelated purchases. In linguistics this is Grice's maxims. If you don't state the relationship, readers won't shrug and assume there isn't one. They'll guess [V24 18:11–20:59]. Two fixes:
  1. **Logical bridges.** Connectors like because, therefore, however and although mark cause and effect, condition, contrast and comparison.
  2. **Actions as verbs, not nouns** [V24 20:59–21:04].

  Steps 5 and 6 together give you **cores**: a clear character as subject plus a clear action as verb makes a **core clause**, and logical bridges join the cores [V24 21:04].
- **Why it works.** If smart people feel stupid reading your work, the problem is the writing, not their intelligence. Readers give up when working out connections is too taxing [V24 18:11–19:05].
- **How to apply.**
  1. For each pair of adjacent sentences, ask what the relationship is, and add the connector that states it.
  2. Find nouns hiding actions (-tion, -ment, -ance) and turn them back into verbs, with the actor as subject.
- **Symptoms.** Sentences simply lined up with no connectors. Sentences built on "occurred", "took place" or "resulted in" with the real action in a noun.
- **Illustration.**
  - Before: "Implementation of the new policy occurred in May. Complaints increased."
  - After: "We implemented the new policy in May, and because it removed the phone option, complaints rose."

#### Step 7. Mechanics last
- **What it is.**
  1. Proofread for grammar, punctuation, spelling and formatting.
  2. Check house style when submitting.
  3. Match the unwritten conventions of your platform, such as Substack norms, so the piece looks at home in its genre.
  4. Handle presentation: web markup, or print spacing and margins [V24 21:04–22:56].
- **Why it works.** Typos break a serious reader's trust, like wearing a sharp suit with your fly down. But it's the **last** step. Most people obsess over grammar while the writing serves no clear purpose for readers [V24 21:36–21:58].
- **When it does NOT apply.** Don't start here. Online tools are fine, but he thinks nothing replaces a writer's or editor's keen eye [V24 21:58].

**Ordering principle.** The seven steps run top-down: reader, point, problem, coherence, subjects, connections, mechanics. Purpose and structure get fixed before surface [V24 22:56].

### 4. His stances
- **Against** the "old way of editing" (rereading by feel) [V24 0:52].
- **For** knowing the reader, not the audience. He won't read a text until the four questions are answered [V24 3:22].
- **Against** judging writing by grammar-checker standards. A text can score perfectly in Grammarly and still be useless to its reader [V24 5:17].
- **Against** burying the point, and against opening with background [V24 6:30, 10:50].
- Readers care about their problems, not your ideas [V24 8:15].
- **Against** the "avoid repetition, use variation" advice, which he calls grade-school stuff [V24 12:49].
- **Against** teachers who say the active version is always better. It depends on the reader's focus [V24 16:49].
- Readers' labels (dull, passive, unclear) describe feelings, not causes, so don't act on them literally [V24 15:50].
- If smart people feel stupid, it's the writing [V24 18:11].
- Mechanics last. Most professional writing fails because it serves the writer, not the reader [V24 21:58, 22:56].
- **Warning** that this kind of writing is a "dying art" in the age of AI (closing plug) [V24 22:56].

## V25: "I wish I knew this before I started writing online"

### 1. Who it's for, and the problem

It's for people starting to write essays online who were told to "write about whatever you're interested in" (or "write for yourself"), and who believe original ideas are automatically valuable. The problem is that this is a writer's definition of the essay, and it produces pieces readers can't use [V25 0:00–0:45].

### 2. Core argument

He offers a **reader's definition** of an essay, short enough for a sticky note: *an essay helps readers understand something better that they want to understand.* Each part does work:
- **Specific readers.** Essays deal in judgments, not facts, and judgments exist only by consensus within a group, so the reader shapes everything.
- **Understanding, not persuasion.** That is the essay's genre function.
- **Something they want to understand.** Originality isn't value, and an idea becomes knowledge only when readers accept it.
- **Readers decide what counts as "better".** You must know their starting point.
- **Helping requires methods, not intentions.** That method is argument: claim, evidence, warrant.

[V25 0:45–11:00]

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 The reader's definition of an essay (his framework)
- **What it is.** "An essay helps readers understand something better that they want to understand" [V25 0:45]. He sets it against the writer's definition, "write about whatever interests you".
- **Why it works.** Writers use writing to think, so the solution appears at the end. If you ship that, readers can't use it. The writer's definition ignores the reader [V25 0:00–0:45].
- **How to apply.** Keep it on a sticky note and check a draft against its five parts:
  1. Which specific readers?
  2. Does it aim at understanding, with any action second?
  3. Is it something *they* want to understand?
  4. Better by *their* measure?
  5. What method (argument) delivers it?
- **Symptoms.** "I don't know my reader yet, I'm just getting my ideas down." "It doesn't matter who reads it." A topic chosen because it interests you, with no reader problem attached.
- **Illustration.**
  - Writer's definition: "I'm fascinated by medieval guilds, so I'll write about them."
  - Reader's definition: "Freelance designers want to understand why they keep undercutting each other's rates. Medieval guilds show how a trade sets a floor price without a boss."

#### 3.2 Arguments of fact vs arguments of judgment (why the essay needs specific readers)
- **What it is.** Questions of fact (who committed the crime?) are settled by examining evidence and making a case. They belong in reports and briefings. Essays deal with **arguments of judgment**: good, bad, right, wrong, better, worse, what art means, which course of action to take. That knowledge isn't objective. It exists only by consensus within a particular group [V25 0:45–2:24].
- **Why it works.** If the essay's questions are socially constructed, the reader is not an afterthought, the "icing on the cake". They're the **batter**. Structure, conventions, word choice and references all flow from the reading community. So ask: **valuable to whom?** [V25 2:24]
- **How to apply.**
  1. Decide whether your question is one of fact (write a report) or of judgment (write an essay).
  2. For an essay, name the community whose consensus you're entering, and take conventions, vocabulary and references from it.
- **Multiple audiences.** Writing across two or more audiences is fine, but it's like playing several games of chess at once. You still have to think about each game's specifics [V25 2:24].
- **Symptoms.** Treating the reader as something to figure out after drafting. An essay that argues a fact (better as a report) or states a judgment with no community in view.
- **Illustration.** "Did remote work lower our team's output?" is a question of fact, so write a report. "Should our team stay remote?" is a judgment, so write an essay whose reasons and evidence are ones *this* team's leaders already value.

#### 3.3 The essay's function is understanding, not persuasion (genre as function)
- **What it is.** Every genre exists to help its users get something done, so every genre has a function. A white paper's function is to persuade stakeholders to take a position and act. An essay's function is to help readers understand. Most writing manuals say the essay aims to persuade, and he says they're wrong. Some pieces do both, but action comes second. If you chase the rabbit of action, you risk failing the essay's primary function [V25 2:24–3:18].
- **How to apply.** State in one line what the reader will understand afterward that they didn't before. If your line is "they will do X", you may be writing a different genre, such as a proposal or white paper, or you need to put the understanding first.
- **Symptoms.** Calls to action crowding out explanation. Pushing the reader toward a verdict before they understand the situation.
- **Illustration.**
  - Persuasion-first: "You must switch to a four-day week."
  - Understanding-first: "Why do four-day-week pilots succeed in some firms and quietly fail in others?" The reader can then judge for themselves.

#### 3.4 Write about what readers want to understand: original isn't valuable
- **What it is.** Writers want to write about what *they* want to think about. His joke about quitting to invent a coaster that doubles as a camera lens shows how he views that. The romantic image of the stubborn genius whom readers eventually find ("if you build it, they will come") drives the chase for **original** research. But treating originality as value imagines knowledge as a fixed puzzle with missing pieces. That's true only for arguments of fact, where new evidence is automatically valuable. For judgment, your idea is an **invention taken to market**: readers adopt it if they understand it and it solves a problem they care about. Otherwise it stays on the shelf. An idea only becomes knowledge when readers accept it [V25 3:18–7:01].
- **Why it works.** Reading rates are falling and attention is scarce. Readers don't want to spend time on whatever interests you. Essays mostly succeed by advancing understanding of something readers **already** care about [V25 7:01].
- **When it does NOT apply.** This isn't **audience capture**. There are ways to shift what readers care about, but it's rare, hard and risky, so treat it as the exception [V25 4:50, 7:01].
- **How to apply.**
  1. List what your readers are already trying to understand. Where are they stuck, and what do they argue about?
  2. Find where your idea advances one of those.
  3. If it advances none, either find the connection or pick a different topic.
- **Symptoms.** A pitch built on "nobody has written about this before". A topic that matters only to you.
- **Illustration.** "Nobody has written about the history of the paperclip" isn't a reason. "Office managers keep buying tools that nobody uses. The paperclip shows what makes a simple tool stick" might be.

#### 3.5 Readers decide what counts as "better understanding": know their starting position
- **What it is.** An essay is like a set of directions. To take someone further, you must know where they're standing now. So ask: what do they already understand? What do they want to understand? Where are the gaps? You can't be agnostic about your reader and write well, because they decide whether the piece helped [V25 7:59–8:27].
- **How to apply.** Before drafting the reader-facing version, write three lines: what they believe now, what they want to know, where their knowledge stops. Start the piece from the first line, not from your own starting point.
- **Symptoms.** Explaining basics your readers already know, or skipping steps they need. Starting from where your own research began.
- **Illustration.** For experienced project managers new to AI tools, don't explain what a project plan is. Start from what they already do (breaking work into tasks) and show where the tool changes that step.

#### 3.6 Ability, not intention: argument as the method (claim, evidence, warrant)
- **What it is.** From a Buddhist monk he studied with: love is the *ability* to help someone suffer less. Intending to help without the ability can do harm. In writing, the skill set is **argument**, which has three parts [V25 8:27–11:00]:
  - **Claim.** A falsifiable statement about how things are or should be. Someone must be able to disagree. "Water is wet" is only a definition. "Water beats Gatorade for hydration" is a claim.
  - **Evidence.** Whatever *this* reader will accept as credible. Some want citations from authorities or primary sources. Others are convinced by a well-chosen anecdote.
  - **Warrant.** The logical bridge connecting evidence to claim, a bit like a reason. Evidence doesn't speak for itself. In climate science, researchers who accept the same rising temperatures offer different explanations to support different claims.
- **How to apply.**
  1. Write the claim and check that someone could deny it.
  2. List the evidence and ask whether *these* readers find that kind of evidence credible.
  3. Write out the warrant: "this evidence supports this claim because...".
  4. If a rival could read the same evidence the other way, strengthen the warrant.
- **Symptoms.** Claims nobody could dispute. Evidence of a kind your readers discount (anecdotes for a data-driven audience, or citations for a practitioner audience that wants cases). Evidence placed next to a claim with no stated link.
- **Illustration.**
  - Claim: "Short daily standups hurt senior engineers more than juniors."
  - Evidence: "Seniors in our team log their longest focus blocks in the morning."
  - Warrant: "A 9:30 standup cuts the morning block in two, and seniors depend on long blocks for design work, so they lose more."

#### 3.7 Prefer reader psychology to rule lists
- **What it is.** Writers usually fall back on lists of rules. He prefers understanding what happens in the reader's mind as they move through your sentences, so you can adapt your prose to how they process it [V25 11:00]. (He points to another video for the four principles.)

### 4. His stances
- **Against** "write about whatever you're interested in" as the writer's definition. It's the worst advice, in his framing [V25 0:00].
- **Against** "I don't know my reader yet" and "it doesn't matter who reads it" [V25 0:45].
- **Against** writing manuals that say an essay's goal is persuasion [V25 2:24–3:18].
- **Against** self-centeredness and the "if you build it, they will come" myth [V25 4:50–5:42].

- V21 shows the narrative version: lessons carried by a concrete moment, abstractions like "weak stability" given a scene.
- V24 links clarity to grounding: putting real characters in the subject position is what readers feel as "concrete".

**5. Writing to think vs writing to be read: this is where the set shifts, and where a skill must be careful.**
- V24 and V25 both *open* from the same diagnosis. Writers use writing as an extension of thinking, so the solution comes last. Shipped that way, the piece gives readers "your route" [V24 4:18–7:28; V25 0:00]. So the point must move to the end of the introduction (V24).
- V23 says, emphatically, that separating writing from revision is "completely backwards". Revision *is* writing, recursive and exploratory. He also says to put your best idea wherever it occurs, rather than holding it for a planned ending, and to cut late [V23 33:31, 36:18–38:09].
- V22 closes by promising that the writing process "isn't one process, it's two" [V22 34:30].

These fit together once you separate *time* from *function*. What V23 attacks is revision as a later clean-up of a finished, static text. What V22 and V24 insist on is two different *orientations*: the writer's thinking pattern and the reader's reading pattern. A skill can treat them as layers:
- While drafting (V23's artist/architect loop), explore, reread often, and don't prune or relocate for readers yet.
- When shaping for readers (V24's checklist), move the point up front, frame the problem, fix terms and subjects, then do mechanics.

Two tensions follow from this:
- **Concision.** V22 praises McCarthy's ruthless "do you need that?". V23 warns that early concision is "writing anorexia". The resolution is timing: cut when you're rich in material.
- **Point placement.** V21's model essay withholds its thesis until the end and opens with an image. V24 wants the point at the end of the introduction for professional writing. The resolution is genre: narrative creative nonfiction vs expository or professional writing.

**6. Persuasion vs understanding: a real shift within five weeks.**
- In V22 he says the Arthur/McCarthy article's ultimate function is persuasion. He praises its inviting "let's look" tone as ideal for "what's essentially persuasion", because traditional argument puts readers on their back foot [V22 22:52–23:32].
- In V25 he says writing manuals are *wrong* that the essay's goal is persuasion. An essay's function is understanding, persuasion to act belongs to genres like the white paper, and action comes second [V25 2:24–3:18].

You could reconcile them by genre (an HBR strategy article isn't quite an essay), or by the fact that Arthur's piece attacks a *conceptual* problem, which is changing understanding. Either way, the later framing is sharper. For a skill: default to "help the reader understand", treat action as a consequence, and use V22's side-by-side, inviting tone because it produces understanding without defensiveness.

**7. Argument grows more explicit across the set.**
- V22 shows argument implicitly: concession, heading off "Hicks already said this", intuition pumps that let readers reach the conclusion themselves.
- V24 makes it tactical: dismantle the resistant reader's specific belief, and make logical links explicit because readers *will* invent links (Grice).
- V25 names the structure: a claim that must be falsifiable, evidence credible to *this* reader, and a warrant, because evidence doesn't speak for itself.

The warrant (V25) and the logical bridge (V24) are the same idea at two scales, whole argument and sentence pair.
