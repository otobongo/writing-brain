# What we understand: the Writer Science method, for building a writing skill

This is the synthesis. It draws on the ten deep-read reports (`read_01.md` to `read_10.md`, all 50 videos), the six analyses (`catalog.md`, `architecture.md`, `current.md`, `tensions.md`, `skill_mapping.md`, `applicability.md`), and spot checks of the load-bearing passages in `readable/`. It is written for the person who gathered the material, to give enough understanding of William Fitzpatrick's method to decide how a skill built on it should work.

**How to read it.** Section 1 is the whole argument in about a page. Sections 2 to 5 explain the method: its core ideas, the path a piece takes through it, which version of each framework is current, and how to settle his apparent contradictions. Sections 6 and 7 turn that into consequences for a skill and name the method's limits. Section 8 lists the decisions only you can make. If you have ten minutes, read 1, 6 and 8.

**Conventions.**
- Citations are V-number plus m:ss, following the Source index in `writer-science-system.md` (V1 = 2024-02-29, V50 = 2026-09-13).
- A number in square brackets, such as [3.15], points to the full entry in `catalog.md`. Each entry there has the technique's steps, symptoms, limits, an example and every source. Every catalog entry appears somewhere below; section 3 carries most of them.
- Everything is paraphrase. Direct quotes are rare and under 15 words. All examples are my own, written in the spirit of his.
- "(inferred)" marks a step that goes beyond what he says.

## 1. His method in plain language

Fitzpatrick's teaching rests on one claim: a piece of writing is not good or bad in itself, only good or bad for a particular reader. Meaning, clarity and even coherence are not stored in the text. They arise when a specific reader works through it (V2 0:21; V33 8:53; V49 8:21 to 10:14). So there are no universal writing rules, because there are no universal readers (V12 7:30). The passive voice, jargon, repetition, "I", long sentences and the em dash are all tools, right or wrong only by what they do for the person reading. His test for any choice is whether it moves this reader from where they are to where you want them (V42 23:06).

What readers want from nonfiction is value, and he defines value narrowly: help with a problem the reader already has. The problem may be practical (what to do) or conceptual (what to believe). Readers owe the writer nothing. They spend attention only when they can see what it buys them, and text that is clear, organised and useless is still useless (V19 9:25; V31 3:35). So value comes before clarity, and the introduction, where readers decide whether the piece is worth their time, is the single most important thing to get right. He calls it the 80/20 of good writing (V46 19:48).

The difficulty is that experts can't produce valuable writing directly. Anything worth writing involves thinking too tangled to finish in the head, so the expert has to think on the page (V35 0:50; V47 0:59). Writing to think produces text in the writer's order: the question at the top and the answer at the bottom, background first, findings in the order they were discovered, private shorthand throughout. Readers need the opposite: the point early, a start from what they already know, a route that begins where they stand. He calls this clash **interference** and names it the main problem of expert writing (V47 0:00). The writer can't see it, because rereading your own words is remembering, not reading. Each sentence reminds you of the idea you already hold (V24 0:52; V49 1:49 to 3:40; V50 2:00 to 4:24).

The method answers both problems with two movements.
1. **Write to find out what you think**, with readers kept out of the room, until you can state your point.
2. **Build a separate text for a named reader.** Put what that reader needs in the places readers look: the title, the end of the introduction, the opening of every section and paragraph, the start and end of every sentence, the key terms and the conclusion. Then check those places deliberately instead of trusting how the draft feels (V50 4:24).

Work top-down: reader and value, then the point, the introduction and the order of sections, then paragraphs, sentences, compression and certainty, and mechanics last (V24 22:56). One shape repeats at every scale: a short opening that states the point and names the key terms, then a longer discussion that delivers on it (V27 37:51; V46 8:20).

Two further ideas complete the picture.
- **Language is social.** Word choices signal who you are and whom you are addressing. So the writer uses the reading community's own terms, enters its conversation politely, and sounds like "a partner and not a preacher" (V18 35:20; V12 14:37 to 18:09; V42 12:12).
- **The value of a piece lies in the writer's own seeing:** what they found by thinking, what they witnessed, what they have at stake. That is why, by mid-2026, he rules out AI at any stage of writing meant for readers, including "cleaning up" your own notes, while allowing it as a navigation layer over your own material (V45 0:21, 17:18; V40 23:23).

The method is built for **pointed** writing: text that answers a question or solves a problem. He explicitly excludes fiction, memoir and poetry (V50 0:48).

## 2. The load-bearing ideas, and why each matters

Eight ideas carry the method. The first is the root. The next three describe the reader. Two describe the writer's handicaps. The last two are the structural rule and the boundary that follow. Almost every technique in the catalog derives from one or two of them.

```
                ROOT: meaning is made in a specific reader  (tools, not rules)
                                   |
      +----------------------------+----------------------------+
 reader with a problem       reader reading in a line      reader in a community
      VALUE                        POSITION                   RELATIONSHIP
      +----------------------------+----------------------------+
                                   |
              ...but the writer faces two handicaps:
      thinking runs the other way           you can't read your own text
      (interference -> two phases)          (coffee stain -> check locations)
                                   |
      ONE SHAPE AT EVERY SCALE               THE VALUE IS THE WRITER'S OWN SEEING
      (index, then discussion)               (the boundary for any AI helper)
```

### 2.1 Meaning is made in a specific reader [0.1, 0.2]

**The idea.** Writing is a function, not a thing: meaning is what a reader does with a text (V33 8:53). Coherence emerges between writer, reader and text. His own master's thesis was clear to him when he wrote it and is partly opaque to him ten years later, because the reader changed (V49 7:34 to 10:14). From his second video on, he judges sentences by their effect on a reader's mind, in his words "good and bad rather than right and wrong" (V2 0:21).

**Why it matters.** This is the source of every "it depends" in his teaching, and of his lifelong fight with rulebooks (Strunk and White in V14; grammar checkers in V14 7:44; school rules in V31). He rejects rules because rules assume one reader, and because they train writers to stop thinking about readers at all (V33 12:31 to 14:11). Drop this idea and the method collapses into a style guide, the thing he spent the channel dismantling.

**For the skill.** Make no reader-dependent judgment without a named reader. He won't look at a client's text until the reader questions are answered (V24 3:22). Phrase every flag as an effect on this reader, with a choice, never as a rule.

### 2.2 Value comes before clarity [0.3]

**The idea.** Readers trade attention for value, and value is a solution to a problem they already care about, which they have to be able to see (V18 9:14; V31 3:35 to 4:34; V46 8:20). None of these count as value: novelty (V27 26:10), the writer's effort or conviction (V28 1:59), originality or personal interest (V25 5:42), or the fact that something is true or interesting (V19 1:06:47). Problems come in two kinds. Practical problems are solved by changing what the reader does. Conceptual problems are solved by changing what they understand, and they sit upstream of practical ones (V18 4:21; V22 10:19).

**Why it matters.** It sets the order of work. Motivated readers even judge prose clearer and will push through clotted sentences, but incoherence and irrelevance defeat everyone (V27 27:52, 32:42). So sentence work cannot rescue a piece with no problem, while solving the problem softens many sentence faults. This asymmetry is why the introduction comes first.

**For the skill.** The first review question is "what will this reader understand or be able to do afterwards?", not "is this clear?" If the writer can't answer it, sentence feedback is premature.

### 2.3 Readers read in a line and look in fixed places

**The idea.** Readers take in text one word at a time, hold only three or four chunks in working memory, and carry a compressed "mental briefcase" from sentence to sentence: the last words, the topic, sensory impressions and any open expectation (V26 1:32 to 4:55). Because the order is fixed, readers expect certain things in certain positions:
- They wait for the verb, which he pictures as holding your breath through a tunnel (V8 0:42).
- They take whatever comes first as what the sentence is about, the **topic position** (V9 1:55; V33 1:40).
- They give the end of a sentence the most weight, the **stress position** (V2 9:38; V14 5:51).
- They look to the opening of any unit to learn what it is about, whether or not you meant it as a guide (V46 9:53 to 10:50).

**Why it matters.** Most clarity turns out to be placement, not vocabulary or grammar. He says roughly 80% of clarity comes from how information is organised within sentences and paragraphs (V19 42:56; V37 0:19; V48 3:00). It also gives the method checkable locations, which the next two ideas depend on.

**For the skill.** This is where almost all the mechanical checks live: subject length, words before the verb, the topic list, the last words of each sentence, the opening of each paragraph.

### 2.4 Language is social

**The idea.** Words carry information and also signal membership. "I have a prior" and "I have an assumption" say the same thing to different communities (V12 3:46 to 5:04). Essays deal in judgments, and judgments exist only by consensus within a group, so the reader is, in his image, the batter, not the icing (V25 0:45 to 2:24). Writing means joining a table of people already working on a problem (V12 14:37 to 18:09). The ideal stance is side by side with one reader, looking at the same thing, which he calls the classic scene of joint attention (V30 2:44; V43 4:05 to 4:56).

**Why it matters.** It explains positions that otherwise look inconsistent. He rejects "write at a fifth-grade level" (V12 3:46; V47 13:39) yet also attacks words chosen to impress (V29 13:07): both are about fitting this community. He wants courtesy before disagreement (V22 8:14; V28 4:20). By 2026 he treats flat voice and writer's block as relationship problems that fade when you write to specific people you respect (V44 30:39 to 32:40). And what counts as evidence, and how much hedging is normal, depends on the community (V25 9:25; V19 1:02:55).

**For the skill.** Register decisions need the reader's community and its code words. The skill can't supply the relationship itself; it can only point the writer toward it.

### 2.5 Thinking runs the other way: interference

**The idea.** Valuable expertise is like tangled Christmas lights: you can't untangle it in your head, only by writing (V35 6:15; V47 0:59). But thinking on the page leaves a text organised by the writer's process: uncertainty to certainty, order of discovery, background first, loops and private shorthand (V19 25:11 to 27:22; V47 2:51, 12:59). Readers want certainty up front and a journey from a familiar, stable world to the discovery that something is wrong with it (V47 3:55, 7:16 to 8:19). His image is giving a friend directions to a restaurant by describing your own drive from your own house (V24 4:18; V35 9:41; V47 10:18).

**Why it matters.** It explains the two phases [1.1]: think first, then build for readers. It explains why line editing can't rescue a thinking draft, since the problem is the route, not the words (V31 8:47; V35 12:09; V47 17:30). And it explains why readers' complaints ("unclear", "dense", "disorganised") describe friction, not causes (V47 6:18 to 7:16). Bring readers into the thinking phase and you freeze or pander (V20 1:05 to 5:22; V38 18:28); leave them out of the second and nobody uses the result (V38 19:34).

**For the skill.** Before doing anything, find out which draft this is: a thinking draft or a reader draft.

### 2.6 You can't read your own text

**The idea.** Rereading your draft is association: the words trigger the idea you already hold, like a coffee stain that means something only to the person who spilled it (V24 0:52 to 1:40; V49 1:49 to 3:40). He cites a Carnegie Mellon study in which readers briefed on a topic caught fewer structural errors than cold readers, because they filled the gaps from what they knew. The writer is the most briefed reader there is, so resting a draft and rereading it "as a stranger" doesn't work (V50 2:00 to 4:24). He drops the phrase "curse of knowledge" and speaks instead of expert **compression**: the private shorthand you don't notice you use (V47 12:59 to 16:21).

**Why it matters.** It justifies his mechanical diagnostics (underline sentence openings, read only the paragraph openings, compare the point in the introduction with the point in the conclusion) and his insistence on real readers. He calls readers poor critics of a draft (V47 4:44), yet his cohort protocol has them pin down exactly where a reaction starts and stops (V44 23:37). The reading that reconciles the two, readers as sensors rather than diagnosticians, is an inference, though a well-supported one.

**For the skill.** Check locations, not impressions. The skill has a double position here: it is a cold reader of the writer's intentions, so it can spot missing links, but it is "briefed" on many subjects, so it will silently fill technical gaps a real lay reader cannot. Compression checks must test against the reader brief, not against the model's own understanding (skill_mapping §5.6).

### 2.7 One shape at every scale

**The idea.** The reader draft repeats one pattern, which he calls **fractal**: a short, easily grasped **index** that states the point and names the key terms, then a longer **discussion** that delivers it (V18 23:07; V27 37:51 to 43:21; V46 8:20 to 12:43). A sentence has a short subject and verb, then the predicate. A paragraph has one to three opening sentences, then development. A section has an opening segment, then its paragraphs. The document has its introduction, then the body.

**Why it matters.** It reconciles two rules that look opposed: "put the point first" (units) and "put the emphasis last" (sentences). Both hold because the point sits at **the end of the beginning**, his phrase for the thesis at the close of the introduction (V50 8:23). He applies the same logic to the governing idea at the end of a paragraph's first sentence (V37 7:25). The same "start where the reader already is" logic runs through old-before-new in the sentence (V15 12:00), common ground before the problem (V28 4:20), and stability before instability (V47 7:16). It also gives one diagnostic that works at every scale: read only the beginnings, and see whether they tell the story (V46 12:12).

**For the skill.** A small set of checks applies at every level. The skim view (openings only) is probably the single most useful artefact a script can produce.

### 2.8 The value is the writer's own seeing [0.5]

**The idea.** Good writing starts from a vision, something you have seen to be true. With the vision in mind and the person in front of you, the words come without planning (V45 5:09 to 6:14). Voice emerges from lived experience (V36 1:10 to 5:55). Style is a by-product of honestly working through your own inquiry (V38 10:50 to 16:38). A narrative piece needs judgment calls, or it is an encyclopaedia entry (V39 3:20 to 4:58). Style and substance can't be separated, so changing one distorts the other (V45 11:41).

**Why it matters.** This is the ground of his AI position, and it separates his method from style polishing. If the value lies in thinking the writer has not yet done, a tool that supplies fluent prose supplies the one thing that isn't worth anything: "fluency without communication" (V45 10:34). He tells a story of memorising a phone order for fried chicken in Korea and hanging up in panic at the first clarifying question (V45 9:06 to 9:53).

**For the skill.** This is the boundary. The skill works as a coach and diagnostic editor that asks, locates and explains. It does not ghostwrite or polish the writer's thinking into prose. Section 6.5 spells this out, and section 8 asks you to confirm it.

## 3. How a piece moves through the method

The earlier system summary described two phases. The transcripts support a finer picture: three modes of writing kept apart, a bridge where the reader enters, then passes on the reader draft from top to bottom. Block, practice and genre modules sit alongside.

```
ongoing   0  CAPTURE AND VENTING ....... no reader, no goal
          1  THINKING DRAFT (artist) ... no reader; a question at the top of the page
                exit: "the click": you can state what you now think
          2  BRIDGE .................... reverse outline, reader brief, point sentence
                the reader enters here
          3  BONES (architect) ......... value, point, introduction, sections, argument, ending, title
          4  FLOW ...................... paragraphs, sentences, presence
          5  FINISH .................... compression, certainty, emphasis, mechanics
          6  READERS ................... real readers; feedback tied to locations
any time     BLOCK ..................... diagnose the kind, then one move
away         PRACTICE .................. drills on other writers' sentences
by genre     MODULES ................... add or switch off defaults
```

Two rules govern the whole path. **Revise top-down, one tool at a time** [0.4]: fix scale by scale, because each lower decision is defined by a higher one, and because applying many tools at once freezes writers (V12 51:02; V19 1:14:43; V23 40:48). And **scale the method to the stakes**: nobody fears writing emails (V23 30:53), forcing functions suit routine writing (V41 13:46), and the two-document process is for writing that makes new knowledge (V47 16:21). A routine memo needs little more than point-first and the reader's questions. A grant, an essay for cold readers or a book gets the whole path.

**Running example (mine; all figures invented).** An analytics lead at a 60-store grocery chain thinks the weekly store ranking misleads the regional managers who use it to set bonuses.

### Stage 0. Capture and venting (ongoing, optional)

**Purpose.** Keep raw material flowing and give the unconscious room to work. **Reader:** nobody, not even you as a judge.

**Moves.**
1. **Daily goal-free freewriting.** He calls it opening a vent for "magma": not a first draft, not material for a newsletter, and not to be judged good or bad. Let it cool, then reread it cold for the small part, perhaps a tenth, that has "shine" [1.9] (V41 22:17 to 31:10).
2. **Capture continuously.** Keep fleeting notes with timestamp IDs, a weekly log of teachable moments from work, and scraps that later cluster into ideas [1.22] (V12 29:53 to 31:16; V17 6:04; V40 5:53 to 7:26).
3. **Literature notes as a dialogue.** Copy the line that provoked you, then write your reply, asking where it is the same as and different from what you think. Don't store highlights or summaries [1.23] (V40 7:26 to 10:37).
4. **Permanent notes.** One idea in your own words, linked to its source and to an existing note, so nothing is orphaned [1.24] (V40 10:37 to 15:54).
5. **The night shift.** Work a problem until progress stops, then rest. Capture the images and hunches that come back [1.18] (V41 16:41 to 20:09).

**Why it sits outside the piece.** It feeds pieces without being one. A skill can support it, but it should never evaluate or edit a vent.

**Example.** In a Monday freewrite the lead grumbles about the ranking email. A week later one line has shine: "we rank stores on sales they never had a chance to make."

### Stage 1. The thinking draft (the artist)

**Purpose.** Produce the idea by writing. **Reader:** absent. Bringing an imagined, demanding reader in too early is what he calls the **expert's curse**, the start-and-delete cycle (V20 1:05 to 5:22).

**Moves, roughly in order.**
1. **Decide the mode.** This session is for producing, not perfecting [1.1] (V11 2:03 to 5:13).
2. **Start from a question, not a topic.** Write it at the top of the page, say "I don't know; what evidence would I need?", and notice if you have already picked a side [1.3] (V38 2:14 to 5:42). If you have no question, ask "why?" of a recurring frustration until you reach a systemic one [1.4] (V17 5:34 to 6:04).
3. **Write in timed, question-tethered sessions.** No deleting; put placeholders in brackets; refuse the urge to research first [1.6] (V11 3:01 to 4:27; V20 6:12 to 7:40; V42 17:29).
4. **Let the claim come out of evidence plus your own reasoning.** He calls starting from a conclusion and hunting for support "writing in reverse" [1.5] (V38 6:40 to 9:31; V17 6:42 to 7:38).
5. **Keep moving with small moves.**
   - Write to yourself as "you"; when a sentence feels wrong, write why; start with a placeholder opener [1.7] (V11 8:33 to 10:00).
   - Alternate free generation with naming the ideas that surfaced, so they "ferment" together [1.8] (V11 11:16 to 15:32).
   - Talk it out and work from the transcript as raw material [1.10] (V11 10:00; V20 7:40).
   - Treat the draft as a lab and a play space [1.11] (V35 6:40 to 8:11; V36 19:29 to 25:12).
6. **Read when the writing gets hungry**, for a named gap, not before starting [1.14] (V35 0:50 to 3:38).
7. **Spend your best idea where it appears** [1.12]. **Reread for shape between bursts**: he calls separating writing from revision "completely backwards", meaning revision as a later clean-up of a static text [1.13] (V23 32:41 to 38:09). But don't polish or prune yet; early cutting is "writing anorexia" (V23 34:19).

**Exit.** "The click": you can say what you now think, and it is usually not where you started [1.2] (V11 6:14 to 6:54; V47 12:03). **If skipped:** you write only what you already knew, which AI can replace (V35 8:11), or you give readers what they want, which is audience capture (V38 19:34).

**Example.** At the top of the page: "Why do the stores nearest the warehouse always rank last?" Three sessions turn up the logs: same-day delivery covers a 10 km radius; eleven stores sit inside it; their walk-in shoppers now order part of the weekly shop online, and those sales are credited to no store. The click: "The ranking measures how far a store is from the warehouse, not how well it is run."

### When the writing won't come (any stage)

His account of block moves over time, from fear (2025), to a hardened self-image (May 2026), to having no one you respect to write to (July 2026). He never withdraws the earlier accounts, and all of them reject willpower as the fix. So the first move is to **diagnose which kind of block it is** [1.15]:
- **Fear** appears only around writing that matters (V23 0:00 to 2:36). Ask what you are most afraid of in this paragraph, and write about that; he treats fear as a metal detector pointing at the valuable part [1.16] (V23 30:00 to 32:41).
- **The reader has arrived too early.** Switch back to the thinking draft (V20 5:22).
- **An outgrown project.** You log hours, produce nothing and resent the sessions. The project may belong to an earlier self-image; consider putting it away and following what keeps interrupting (V41 0:00 to 16:41).
- **No one to write to.** Find a few early readers whose respect you want (V44 30:39 to 32:40).
- **Resistance that survives every method** deserves a hearing (V11 10:41 to 11:16).

Other moves:
- Stuck mid-session: stay five to ten minutes and type non-stop in a new document [1.17] (V23 19:52 to 22:22).
- Rest and let the night shift work [1.18].
- Catch "virtuous procrastination": research, formatting and admin that feel diligent [1.19] (V23 25:45 to 27:37).
- Break the perfectionism loop with a release date: this is your next work, not your magnum opus [1.20] (V23 27:37 to 30:00).
- A small, forgiving habit works for routine writing and for getting started, but he abandons discipline as the frame for a long, self-chosen creative project [1.21] (V23 3:26 to 19:52; V41 13:10 to 13:46).
- Sort **finishers** (who need a deadline and a partner) from **voice-seekers** (who need readers and deeper craft) [7.10] (V44 4:35 to 10:18).

### Stage 2. The bridge

**Purpose.** Turn the thinking draft into the materials for a reader draft. **Reader:** enters here. He won't read a client's draft before the reader questions are answered (V24 3:22). Three things happen, in this order.

**First, see what you actually have.**
1. **Reverse outline** [2.1]. Label what each part *does*, then read the labels alone. A part you can't label is fluff or still tangled (V35 8:11 to 9:41; V47 18:15 to 22:07).
2. **Find the through lines.** Look for the two or three ideas that keep recurring and build on those, not on a template [2.2] (V42 16:31 to 20:01).
3. **Diagnose interference** [2.3]: the answer at the end, background first, order of discovery, narrated thinking, private shorthand (V19 25:11 to 27:22; V47 2:51 to 3:55).

**Second, write the reader brief.**
4. **The purpose chain**: your goal, then who must read it for that goal to come true, then their pain, then the piece as a bridge, then a filter for every element [2.5] (V18 9:52 to 15:26).
5. **The reader questions** [2.6]. What brought them to this, what problem they face, what answer or decision they are after (V24 3:01). What they believe now, where their knowledge stops, and what must change (V42 23:44).
6. **The reader's stance** [2.7]: uninformed (give enough background to see the problem), indifferent (show consequences that touch them), or resistant (name the belief that blocks your idea) (V24 10:50 to 12:20). Awareness levels add nuance (V27 27:00).
7. **For high-stakes genres, the rhetorical planning wheel** [2.8]: purpose, your role, audience, context, and "what else must they believe for X to be believed?" (V30 4:46 to 11:13).
8. **A private problem statement** at the top of the page: I'm writing about this topic to answer this question so the reader can gain this or avoid that. Add a vision check: what have you actually seen to be true? [2.9] (V12 35:03 to 37:20; V45 5:09 to 7:30).
9. **Frame the value.**
   - For each idea, the benefit gained or cost avoided, in the reader's terms [2.10] (V20 15:41; V19 54:13 to 57:08).
   - Classify the problem as practical or conceptual [2.11].
   - Think of the text as directions from point A to point B, not a garden hose for your ideas [2.12] (V19 6:26 to 8:34).
   - Replace "nobody has studied this" with the error in the reader's current model and what it costs them [2.13] (V31 14:34 to 18:37).
10. **Language for this reader.** Hunt the community's code words and value words "like an anthropologist" in four or five texts they share [2.14] (V19 16:59 to 18:46; V22 30:25 to 32:22; V35 13:27 to 18:54). Unpack your own compressed shorthand where this reader doesn't share it [2.15] (V47 12:59 to 16:21). Plan the reader's likely replies as a decision tree [2.16] (V20 21:38 to 24:58).

**Third, fix the point sentence.**
11. **Compare the point in the introduction with the point in the conclusion**, and keep or synthesise the sharper one; the conclusion usually holds the best version [3.4] (V50 4:24 to 8:23).
12. **Check that it is arguable** (someone could disagree) [3.23] and that it works as "point, *because* reason" [3.24] (V50 17:24 to 18:24).

**Then build the reader draft as a new route** [2.4]. Start from where the reader stands and pull the points out of the thinking draft only as each serves a turn (V47 9:18 to 12:03; V50 0:00, 15:06).

**Example.**
- **Labels:** complaint about the Monday email / how the ranking is computed / delivery map / three managers' comments / basket data / the click (last).
- **Brief:** regional managers who set quarterly bonuses from the ranking. They are resistant: the ranking is simple and tied to money. Their knowledge stops at the overlap between the delivery radius and store catchments. Their decision: Q3 bonus targets. Their words: like-for-like, footfall, basket size.
- **Point:** "Rank stores on like-for-like walk-in sales adjusted for the delivery zone, because the current ranking penalises the eleven stores inside the same-day radius for sales the warehouse took from them."

### Stage 3. The bones (the architect, part one)

**Purpose.** Put value, point, structure and argument where readers look for them. V50 is his fullest procedure for this stage, and V46 lists the locations. **Reader:** fully present. If you fix only one thing, fix the introduction (V46 19:48).

**Moves, in order.**
1. **Choose the shape from function, not a template.** A genre is a sequence of functions that serve a goal writer and reader share. His example is ordering coffee: the wording varies from café to café, the functions don't (V20 17:08 to 21:38) [3.1]. Replace **private** structure (an order only the writer can explain) with **contextual** structure (what the genre's readers expect) or **textual** structure (a pattern the text makes visible) [3.2] (V49 10:14 to 14:02).
2. **Place the point at the end of the introduction.** "Point last" is an advanced option that needs its key terms previewed early [3.3] (V50 8:23 to 11:36).
3. **Build the introduction as a problem** [3.5]:
   - **common ground** the reader already accepts [3.6];
   - a **concession** of what that view gets right, instead of coming off the top ropes [3.7];
   - the turn, with *but* or *however*, then **condition plus consequence**, tested with "so what?" in this reader's voice [3.8];
   - a **solution preview**: the promise and direction, not the details [3.9] (V28; V27 25:12 to 31:36; V46 7:04 to 8:20).

   Three tests: which of two market vendors does the opening sound like, the one boasting of effort or the one naming the shopper's problem [3.10] (V28 1:15 to 4:20)? Does it enter through something the reader already cares about [3.11] (V12 41:39; V39 16:37 to 19:04)? Is there a real turn, or only additive connectors like "furthermore" [3.12] (V31 4:34)?
4. **Order the sections** by the questions the point raises in the reader, in the order they would arise [3.16] (V50 13:22 to 13:57; V19 33:26 to 35:19).
   - Open each unit with an **index**: one to three sentences stating a point or problem, not a topic, and naming the key terms [3.15] (V46 8:20 to 12:43).
   - Join sections with transitions that round up and push off; a real reader question can serve as a palate cleanser [3.17] (V50 15:50 to 16:32).
   - Make coherence visible with headings built from key concepts and a point sentence at the end of each opening segment [3.19] (V27 31:36 to 37:51).
   - Keep one theme and two or three points [3.20] (V22 17:32 to 19:00).
   - Hold key terms stable [3.21] (V46 13:26 to 15:51; V48 20:31).
   - Turn a familiar story into short named concepts and redeploy them [3.22] (V22 23:32 to 28:15).
5. **Check the argument.**
   - Each claim is arguable [3.23].
   - Each has a reason, evidence this reader accepts, and a written **bridge** (warrant) from evidence to reason [3.24] (V25 8:27 to 11:00; V50 17:24 to 21:04).
   - Make the judgment call at contested points [3.25] (V39 3:20 to 4:58).
   - Answer the three standard objections (other causes, counterexamples, different definitions) where they arise [3.26] (V19 57:59 to 1:00:28).
   - Use research to create tension, not to display reading [3.27] (V19 40:04 to 42:56).
   - Analyse rather than describe [3.28] (V3 2:32; V12 46:41).
6. **Develop each unit with the uneven U** [4.3]. On his five-level scale of specificity: open with an arguable point (level 4), explain it, prepare the evidence, give it (level 1), interpret it, return to the point, then push off to the larger question (level 5). Never leave a quote or statistic undiscussed at the end of a paragraph, the "hit-and-run quotation" (V50 21:38 to 30:18).
7. **Hold it together.**
   - Filter every element by its value to this reader [3.29] (V19 1:05:58 to 1:07:30).
   - Look for orphans that link neither back nor forward [3.30] (V18 15:26 to 20:19).
   - Plant images early and call them back [3.31] (V39 13:36 to 16:37; V42 2:12).
8. **End by pushing off higher.** Return to the problem, show it solved, and open onto a broader question. Don't summarise [3.32] (V46 16:18 to 19:48).
9. **Write the title and headings last among the bones.** Titles use one of four curiosity triggers (a question, others know something, a violated expectation, an open sequence), often followed by three key-term nouns, and never an inside joke [3.13] (V6 2:43 to 5:27; V46 1:04 to 2:55). Headings and chapter titles state a promise [3.14] (V18 2:38 to 7:12; V27 34:45).

**Checks for this stage.** The **skim test**: read only the indexes and see whether they tell the story [3.18] (V46 12:12; V47 19:13). Value words at the top of each paragraph [4.2] (V19 49:20 to 54:13). A key-term check [3.21].

**Example.**
- **Introduction:** "Every Monday the ranking gives us one comparable number for 60 stores, and it has helped us spot stores in trouble. But since same-day delivery launched, the eleven stores inside its radius lose part of every weekly shop to the warehouse, and the ranking credits those sales to no one. So in Q3 we are about to set bonuses that punish some of our best-run stores. Ranking on like-for-like walk-in sales, adjusted for the delivery zone, fixes that without adding work."
- **Sections, in the order a regional manager would ask:** Are these stores really being penalised, or are they just weaker? How big is the effect? Won't an adjusted number be gamed? What would the Q3 targets look like?
- **Conclusion:** pushes off to which other measures still assume customers shop in only one channel.
- **Title:** "Why Our Best-Run Stores Rank Last: Delivery Radius, Like-for-Like Sales and Q3 Bonuses".

### Stage 4. Flow (the architect, part two)

**Purpose.** Once the bones hold, make paragraphs cohere and sentences easy to process.

**Paragraphs.**
- One paragraph, one job: label it, underline its point, promote the point to the top [4.1] (V18 22:07; V22 19:00 to 21:00).
- Keep a small cast of two or three characters in the topic position, repeated rather than varied [4.4] (V9 1:55 to 6:01; V26 5:48 to 9:19; V32).
- Check it mechanically: underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence, ask who is on the "movie poster", draw lines between mentions of the same thing [4.5] (V27 18:19; V32 0:23 to 5:46; V37 4:53).
- End each sentence on what the next picks up, the relay race [4.6] (V15 7:58 to 12:29; V27 11:43 to 15:07).
- Remember that handoffs make cohesion, not coherence: a smooth chain can still be about nothing [4.7] (V27 15:42 to 17:46).
- Choose one of four topic patterns by purpose, each with its own failure [4.8] (V48):
  - constant topic, to characterise one thing (risk: stagnation);
  - linking, for processes and causal chains (risk: running off the rails);
  - whole then parts, for breakdowns (risk: the missing umbrella);
  - theme preview, to weave strands (risk: synonyms for the previewed terms).
- Signal a zoom into an example; use enumerators only for heavy lists that point back to an index [4.9] (V32 17:01; V37 11:33).
- State logical links; readers assume one and will guess [4.10] (V18 18:31; V24 18:11 to 21:04).
- Give every sentence a nameable job [4.11] (V19 18:46 to 21:25).
- Order examples same, same, then different [4.12] (V12 45:38).
- Prefer an intuition pump the reader can run in their head to a bare example [4.13] (V22 28:15 to 30:25).

**Sentences.**
- **What is the sentence for?** Judge it by its effect in context, not by rules [5.1] (V10 2:27 to 6:56).
- **Who does what:**
  - characters as subjects, actions as verbs [5.2] (V6 6:03 to 12:04; V27 0:41 to 5:34);
  - zombie nouns and empty verbs, with his three-step fix [5.3] (V8 2:52 to 8:06);
  - keep nominalisations that link back, replace "the fact that", sit as objects, or name concepts these readers know [5.4] (V27 5:34 to 11:43);
  - a short subject and an early verb [5.5] (V27 21:11 to 25:12; V37 16:40);
  - move long opening clauses; don't wedge anything between a verb and its object [5.6] (V12 48:28 to 51:02).
- **Old, new and emphasis:**
  - every sentence needs something old and something new, in that order [5.7] (V15);
  - the stress position: keep weak words out of the end [5.8] (V14 13:50 to 16:03; V37 16:03);
  - choose the subject by what this reader cares about, using the question they would ask [5.9] (V33);
  - active or passive by position and agency [5.10] (V14 3:03 to 7:04; V15 13:05 to 17:20; V42 4:27 to 9:28), after checking it really is passive [5.11] (V14 7:44 to 10:59);
  - grammar that matches importance (", which", *-ing* phrases, *since*) [5.12] (V32; V37 9:04 to 16:03);
  - give caused changes a cause instead of "became" or "emerged" [5.13] (V37 10:48);
  - repack sprawling phrases into noun-phrase "suitcases" [5.14] (V32 12:05);
  - choose context-first or reveal deliberately [5.15] (V26 1:50 to 3:37);
  - open an expectation, then pay it off [5.16] (V26 11:54 to 13:21).
- **Concrete and memorable:**
  - language readers "could stub their toe on" [5.17] (V22 13:48 to 15:28; V9 9:17 to 13:32);
  - move up and down the ladder of abstraction [5.18] (V12 10:56 to 12:09);
  - characters, not labels [5.19] (V39 20:04 to 22:57);
  - metaphors from the body that pass his "portal" test [5.20] (V36 5:55 to 9:58);
  - replace clichés with a fact or a fresh image [5.21] (V12 37:40);
  - classical figures for the few lines that must stick [5.22] (V12);
  - break rules on purpose, once you know them [5.23] (V36 9:58 to 13:48);
  - let sentence length follow what the sentence carries, not a rhythm quota [5.24] (V42 0:00 to 4:27).

**Presence.**
- Voice comes from lived experience, not surface rules [6.12] (V36 1:10 to 5:55; V38 10:50 to 16:38).
- Put a person on the page where it helps: personal front matter, impersonal methods [6.13] (V4 8:45 to 14:02; V5 5:53 to 9:53).
- Be a partner, not a preacher; take habitual "you" out of the subject position [6.14] (V18 25:41 to 35:20).
- Replace metadiscourse ("this section discusses") with the moves of a conversation [6.15] (V9 13:32 to 16:45).
- Anchor the text in the classic scene: one reader beside you, looking at the same thing [6.16] (V30 11:13 to 15:41).
- Understate the serious [6.17] (V12 38:43 to 40:20).

**When principles collide**, his demonstrations favour the larger unit and the reader. Old-before-new beat a human subject in his own live revision (V37 13:39 to 14:29). Keeping the topic string beats "use the active voice" (V9 6:41 to 9:17). The reader's code word beats generic plainness (V42 13:46).

**Example.** Before: "A reduction in basket size at the eleven stores was the result of the introduction of same-day delivery." The cast this reader cares about is the stores, so: "The eleven stores inside the delivery radius lost about a sixth of their weekly baskets once same-day delivery launched. Those baskets did not disappear; they moved to the warehouse." The second sentence picks up where the first ends.

### Stage 5. Finish

**Purpose.** Remove wasted effort and set the strength of each claim.

**Moves.**
1. **Compress late, and for cognitive load, not length** [6.1] (V29 2:15 to 2:36; V23 34:19).
   - Ask "do you need that?" from sections down to punctuation, and the harder you want to keep a word, the harder you test it [6.2] (V22 2:24; V6 1:08 to 2:43).
   - Strip a passage to its punctuation, the X-ray, to see which sentences do too much [6.3] (V22 1:34 to 3:19).
   - Cut fillers [6.4], doublets [6.5] and redundancy [6.6]; replace a phrase with the exact word [6.7]; write in the affirmative unless the warning is the point [6.8] (V29).
   - Thin out chains of "of the" [6.9] (V5 4:43).
   - Package simply without dumbing down [6.10] (V5 2:05 to 4:43).
2. **Calibrate certainty** on a scale from hedged through calibrated to strident. Fix the verb slot with one precise verb and the subject slot with a narrower noun; read "obviously" as a sign of a gap in the argument; match the community's baseline [6.11] (V50 30:18 to 35:11; V19 1:02:00 to 1:05:09).
3. **Economise emphasis.** *However*, dashes and signposts lose force with use [6.18] (V34 8:51 to 9:42). Use em dashes for emphasised examples, asides and mitigations, and ", which" for definitions [6.19] (V34).
4. **Mechanics last** [6.20] (V24 21:04 to 22:56; V2 for the few real rules).

**Example.** Strident: "The ranking is meaningless." Hedged: "It might perhaps be argued that the ranking could be somewhat misleading for certain stores." Calibrated: "For the eleven stores inside the delivery radius, the ranking measures distance from the warehouse, not how well the store is run."

### Stage 6. Real readers

**Purpose.** Find where friction is, since the writer can't feel it.

**Moves.**
1. Accept that you can't edit by feel [7.1] (V49; V50 2:00 to 4:24), and treat complaints such as "dense" as symptoms at a location, not instructions [7.2] (V47 4:44 to 8:19; V32 3:24).
2. Test with real people [7.3] (V19 1:11:07; V16 5:28 to 7:47), ideally **early readers** you respect and want respect from, in a community of practice [7.4] (V44 28:00 to 34:31).
3. Use a protocol:
   - the **letter of response**: first what the piece is trying to do, then what you would steal, and only then what is missing [7.5] (V44 15:30 to 21:35);
   - two readings: a gut reaction first, then an analytical reread days later that locates each effect [7.6] (V44 22:00 to 24:38);
   - listen for where readers' responses are the same and where they differ [7.7] (V44 21:35);
   - the author stays silent while the piece is discussed [7.8] (V44 24:38 to 27:14).
4. After publication, mine the sympathetic middle-rated reviews [7.9] (V18 1:47 to 2:38).
5. Write with stakes: under your name, for people whose judgment you care about [7.11] (V36 13:48 to 19:29).

**Example.** A store manager's letter of response begins: "This is trying to get us to cut delivery." That misreading is the finding. The solution preview never said the delivery service stays. The fix goes into the introduction, not into the section the manager complained about.

### Away from any draft: practice

These build skill over months and are not steps in producing a piece. Practice is not performance (V10 10:46).
- Copy admired passages from memory [1.25] (V12 27:40 to 29:53; V14 12:35 to 13:50).
- Collect striking sentences [1.26] and rework their shapes in Great Sentences Mad Libs [1.27] (V10).
- Label a model text's sections by function [1.28] (V21 15:32).
- Write a précis that summarises an argument and then argues against it [1.29] (V36 13:48).
- Give each paragraph its own page while revising [1.30] (V10 10:08).
- Do the joint-attention drill with a partner [1.31] (V30 2:44 to 5:36).

### Genre modules

These apply the same principles to particular forms. Some switch off core defaults, such as point-first.

- **Essays** [8.1 to 8.3]:
  - his reader's definition: an essay helps specific readers understand something they want to understand (V25 0:45);
  - the dream test for private symbols, and the "impersonal turn" that aims the writer's passion at the world (V43 0:21 to 4:05);
  - Zadie Smith's six arrows, used once the position is known (V43 4:56 to 8:50).
- **Creative nonfiction** [8.4 to 8.10] (V21):
  - aim beyond the facts;
  - the opening image as seed;
  - selection is meaning;
  - webs of related words;
  - no exposition breaks;
  - artistic licence only to a purpose;
  - arc devices.
- **Narrative idea and science writing** [8.11 to 8.15] (V39; V12 33:24):
  - foreground what you saw;
  - mark every time shift;
  - the one-word hyperlink;
  - interview the person, not the source;
  - the engine of the story.
- **Dialogue and scenes** [8.16 to 8.19] (V13):
  - no "author-splaining";
  - ask why the text switches to dialogue;
  - charged ambiguity;
  - silent dialogue (ellipsis) at moments of shame.
- **Academic writing** [8.20 to 8.22] (V1, V4, V5):
  - scholar before researcher;
  - literature review as story with a cast of studies;
  - personal front matter, impersonal methods, present tense for your own findings.
- **Expert books** [8.23, 8.24] (V16, V17):
  - six book types chosen by goal;
  - Vow, Victim, Villain positioning.

## 5. Where his advice seems to conflict, and the resolution the skill should adopt

Almost every apparent contradiction dissolves under one of six lenses (tensions.md §1):
1. **Stage:** venting, exploring, shaping or finishing.
2. **Genre and function:** essay, proposal, peer-reviewed paper, narrative, book, spoken script.
3. **Audience:** insider or outsider, cold or committed, uninformed, indifferent or resistant.
4. **Scale:** title, introduction, section, paragraph, sentence, word.
5. **Level:** parsing and logic (be explicit) versus feeling and meaning (leave room); correctness versus effect.
6. **Era:** an explicit reversal means the later view wins.

A seventh, smaller lens is **packaging versus substance**: titles like "4 Writing Hacks" or "DELETE your writer's block" are YouTube packaging, which he admits (V38 16:38).

| Tension | Resolution the skill should adopt |
|---|---|

1. Stage?
2. Genre and function?
3. Reader?
4. Scale?
5. Level?
6. Did he explicitly reverse himself?
7. If both still apply, present the choice with the reader effect of each, as he does himself (V37 13:11; V33 8:08).

## 6. What this means for a skill

### 6.1 The shape of the work

`skill_mapping.md` maps 291 technique uses. 178 need a judgment question from the model, 115 need a step from the writer (an answer or an exercise), 82 have any mechanical component, and 35 are coaching or design only. The consequence: **the parts of the method that create value (the reader, the problem, the point, the evidence, the judgment) can only be asked about, not computed.** The mechanical layer clusters at the sentence and finishing stages. And scripts are most useful when they produce **views** that let the writer see a pattern (the skim view, the topic list, the X-ray, introduction and conclusion side by side), which is also how he diagnoses (V27 18:19; V46 12:12; V19 54:13).

### 6.2 What can be checked mechanically

Every hit is **a location to look at, never a verdict.** The model sorts hits against the reader brief and drops known false alarms before the writer sees them. No check runs on a thinking draft or a vent. The IDs refer to the full catalogue in skill_mapping §4.

| Stage | Checks a script can run | Main false alarms to filter |
|---|---|---|
| Bones | Skim view of indexes and flags on openings that start with a date, statistic or quote (M22); opening patterns such as background, "in this essay I will" or gap claims (M25); introduction and conclusion side by side, "In summary" openers, narrated-process markers (M26); evidence at paragraph edges, a possible hit-and-run (M27); connector balance, with no turn before the point and "however" everywhere (M19); key-term drift and title nouns that never recur (M23, M24); headings that share no words with their sections (M49); value-word circling (M34); enumerators with no index (M46); writer-centred phrases, the two-vendor test (M47); runs of citations as subjects (M43); runs of one-sentence paragraphs (M28) | Genres that require background or a gap statement; creative nonfiction; point-last sections |
| Flow | Topic list per paragraph, with scatter and concreteness (M12); sentence-to-sentence handoffs (M13); words before the verb and subject length (M8); long opening clauses (M9); verb-object splits (M10); passive **identification** only (M11); nominalisations weighted to subject position, and empty verbs (M6, M7); weak words in the stress position (M14); metadiscourse density (M15); "you" as subject (M16); dummy subjects (M17); undefined shorthand at first use (M29); "became" or "emerged" with no cause (M30); labels on people (M36); function-word chains (M38); drifting names for one thing (M41); attributions at sentence end (M44) | The whole-to-parts pattern; the four legitimate nominalisations; passives that link; long subjects that are old information; genres that require metadiscourse; instructions addressed to "you" |
| Finish | Fillers (M1); doublets (M2); redundant modifiers and categories (M3); wordy phrases (M4); stacked negatives (M5); stacked hedges or absolutes, "obviously" (M18); dash economy and dashes around definitions (M20); punctuation X-ray (M21); clichés as questions for the portal test (M35); mechanics flags (M51) | Warnings where the negative is the point; deliberate litotes; discipline norms for hedging; legal terms of art; deliberate rule-breaks |

**Anti-checks the skill must never run**, because he spent the channel dismantling them (skill_mapping §4.3):
- varying sentence length;
- flagging the passive as a fault;
- flagging repetition or suggesting varied openings;
- readability grade targets;
- "shorter is better";
- hunting "AI tells" such as em dashes;
- school rules (no opening "But", split infinitives, final prepositions);
- "never use I";
- topic-sentence or five-paragraph templates;
- "omit needless words" as a standing order;
- word count as quality;
- rewarding hooks;
- any check on a thinking draft.

His numbers are heuristics to tune, not quotas: three or four items in working memory (V26 3:37); the first seven or eight words (V27 18:19); momentum over nine or ten words (V27 21:35); a one-to-three-sentence index (V46 12:43); about 5% passive (V6 11:47).

### 6.3 What needs judgment

These are questions the model asks of the draft, using the reader brief:
- **Value:** is this a problem this reader cares about, and could a busy stranger see it (V18 9:14)?
- **The point:** is it arguable, and does "because" actually connect (V50 17:54 to 21:04)?
- **Evidence:** is it the kind this reader trusts (V25 9:25; V50 18:24)?
- **Order:** does the section order follow the reader's questions?
- **Cast:** who belongs in the topic position, and which topic pattern is the paragraph using (V33; V48)?
- **Voice:** does a passive keep the flow or hide an agent? Is a nominalisation a hidden action or a field term? Does the persona partner or preach? Is a rule-break deliberate?
- **Openings and endings:** does an opening device pay off? Does the conclusion end higher than it began?
- **Certainty:** is each claim calibrated to its evidence and the community's norms?
- **Metaphor:** does it pass the portal test, showing the unfamiliar thing through the familiar one (V36 9:00)?

The model is well placed to be a cold reader. When it can't reconstruct why sentence B follows A, or what a section's point is, that failure is the finding (skill_mapping §5.6; V44 17:02). But it is not the reader. Every reader-dependent judgment is provisional and should end by sending the writer to real readers (V19 1:11:07; V49 9:16).

### 6.4 What needs the writer

Some things only the writer can supply, and the method depends on them:
- **Who the reader is:** what they believe, where their knowledge stops, their decision, their community's words (V24 3:01; V42 23:44).
- **The question and the point** (V38 3:03; V50 4:24).
- **Evidence, stories, reporting and experience:** what they saw, logged, measured or lived (V17 6:04; V36; V39 8:57 to 11:29).
- **The judgment calls** at contested points (V39 3:20).
- **The reverse-outline labels, first**, because the labels are the writer's own account of what they did (V35 8:57).
- **Reader-facing text:** titles, openings, metaphors and the rewrite itself (V45; V36 6:22).
- **Real readers and relationships:** early readers, stakes, a community of practice (V44 28:53; V36 17:19).
- **The vision:** something they have seen to be true (V45 5:09).

### 6.5 What his AI stance implies

**His position, in its final form.** No AI at any stage of communicative writing (V45 0:21, 17:18). Restyling AI output is hollow because no vision stands behind it. Handing AI your notes or voice memo to "clean up" is just as bad, because AI sees only the words you used to point at a thought, never the thought, so its version is fluent but subtly distorted (V45 7:52 to 14:53). More context won't fix this: context can never be complete, and if you are working that hard, turn to your reader instead (V45 14:53 to 16:23). He does allow AI as a read-only navigation layer over notes you wrote yourself (V40 15:54 to 23:23), for logistics (V44 11:19 to 13:07), and to find a precise verb (V50 34:15).

**The paradox, and his own way out.** The skill is itself AI. His principle, AI works *on* your thinking rather than *doing* it (V40 15:54), maps onto roles he plays for writers that a skill can also play:
- the editor who won't read until the reader questions are answered (V24 3:22);
- the editor who helps you name your goal and works backwards from the reader, not from taste (V18 14:32 to 15:26);
- the diagnostician who checks locations instead of feel (V50);
- the "guide by the side" (V44 15:00).

The role he rejects is the ghostwriter and polisher. So the skill is a **coach and diagnostic editor that does not write the writer's prose**.

**The ladder of help.** Stop at the lowest rung that works (skill_mapping §5.7):
1. **Locate.** Quote a few of the writer's words and say where.
2. **Name the effect** on this reader, as a hypothesis.
3. **Ask** the question that brings out what the writer meant.
4. **Describe the move** in the writer's own terms, for example "your next sentence opens with 'the committee'; try ending this one there".
5. **Demonstrate** on an invented parallel sentence on a different subject. This is how he teaches: on model passages, never on clients' text (V37; V50).
6. The rung the method rules out: writing the finished sentence for them.

**Must not:**
- draft from notes or prompts;
- "clean up", polish or restyle;
- rewrite the writer's sentences;
- supply claims, examples, metaphors, evidence, titles or openings;
- summarise sources in place of reading;
- scrub AI tells;
- critique a thinking draft;
- pose as the target reader;
- feed its own output back as the writer's thinking.

**May:**
- ask questions;
- locate and diagnose;
- explain on parallel examples;
- navigate the writer's own material read-only;
- lay out the writer's own sentences as views;
- handle timers and templates;
- offer word candidates for a slot the writer has named;
- write a clearly labelled cold-reading letter of response on request.

**Gray zones where his statements pull both ways** (skill_mapping §5.8):
- tidying a voice memo (V20, V40, V44 vs V45);
- proposing reverse-outline labels or a paragraph's point;
- showing a fix on the writer's own sentence;
- drafting messages to real people (V44 12:30 vs V45);
- titles.

Section 8 asks you to settle these.

### 6.6 The modes this suggests

**Routing takes two questions.** Is this draft for you, to work out what you think, or for readers? And who is the reader, and what should the text do? Four gates come before any feedback: which draft, which reader, top-down order, and at most three issues per pass.

| Mode | Purpose | Enter when | Leave when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Brief** | Fix the reader, goal, genre and code words before any judgment | Any reader draft with no brief; a new genre | The brief is written in the writer's words |
| **Think** | Help the writer find what they think; reader kept out | "I don't know what I think yet"; a topic but no question | "The click": they can state the point |
| **Unstick** | Diagnose the block and offer one move | Stuck, avoiding, perfectionism, flat voice | Writing again, or a missing reader identified |
| **Bridge** | Reverse outline, interference, point sentence, reader questions | A thinking draft plus the click | The writer has a point and an ordered list of reader questions |
| **Architect** | Bones: introduction, point, sections, argument, conclusion, title | A reader draft plus a brief | The skim test tells the story |
| **Flow** | Paragraph topic strings, sentences, presence | The bones hold | Local friction resolved |
| **Finish** | Compression, calibration, emphasis, mechanics | Near-final text | Ready for readers |
| **Readers** | Plan early readers, protocols, and translate feedback into located causes | Feedback in hand, or "is it ready?" | Back to Architect or Flow with located friction |
| **Gym** | Practice drills on the writer's chosen models | No live draft; wants to improve | n/a |
| **Plan** | Book or project positioning: type, purpose chain, 3Vs, chapter promises | A book or large project | A plan in the writer's words |
| **Notes** | Read-only navigation of the writer's own notes | "Have I written about this before?" (needs access to the notes) | n/a |

Genre overlays switch defaults on or off. Creative nonfiction turns off point-first and the opening-pattern flags. Academic writing turns on the voice-by-section and tense checks and relaxes the hedging thresholds. Dialogue turns on the speech-verb adverb check. Books route to Plan.

A leaner first version could be Brief, Bridge, Architect, Flow and Finish (the revision core), with Think and Unstick added next, since the method's value is mostly upstream. That is a choice for you (section 8, question 5).

## 8. Questions only you can answer

These are decisions that change what gets built. For each: why it matters, and the options. Where the corpus leans one way, I say so, but the choice is yours.

### 1. Who is the skill for, and what writing does it cover?
**Why it matters.** It sets the genre modules, the defaults, and how often the skill must say "this is outside the method". His method is scoped to pointed nonfiction (V50 0:48). Applied to fiction or social posts, much of it doesn't transfer.
**Options.**
- (a) Your own writing only, tuned to the genres you write.
- (b) Expert nonfiction for anyone, as he scopes it.
- (c) All writing: the core for nonfiction, transferable parts for fiction and social, and an explicit "outside the method" label beyond that.

### 2. How strictly should it follow his AI line?
**Why it matters.** This is the biggest design fork. His final position (V45) rules out AI polishing or rewriting anything a reader will see. Many users will expect exactly that. The line decides whether the skill feels like a coach or an editor, and how faithful it is to the method.
**Options.**
- (a) **Strict coach.** Never rewrites the writer's sentences; demonstrates only on invented parallel examples. Closest to V45 and to how he teaches.
- (b) **Illustrative.** On request, rewrites one of the writer's own sentences as a labelled demonstration of a move; the writer does the rest.
- (c) **Editor.** Proposes concrete rewrites with reasons, and the writer accepts or rejects each. This drifts toward what V45 calls a coat of paint.
- (d) **Full assistant**, which drafts and polishes. This contradicts the method.

### 3. Which gray-zone help is allowed?
**Why it matters.** Each item sits where his own statements conflict (skill_mapping §5.8), so the corpus can't decide them for you.
**Options (for each item: allowed, on request only, or never):**
- verbatim transcription of a voice memo;
- tidying or bulleting a private note;
- word candidates for a slot the writer names (V50 34:15);
- proposing reverse-outline labels or "this paragraph's point" as a cold reader;
- suggesting titles;
- drafting messages to real people (V44 vs V45).

### 4. How hard is the reader gate?
**Why it matters.** He won't read without the reader questions answered (V24 3:22), and most reader-facing checks mean nothing without a brief. But many people will paste a draft and want feedback immediately.
**Options.**
- (a) **Hard gate:** no review until a brief exists.
- (b) **Soft gate:** ask once; if declined, infer a provisional reader, state it, and label reader-dependent judgments "provisional".
- (c) **No gate:** review against a generic reader, which contradicts idea 2.1.

### 5. Which stages to build first?
**Why it matters.** His value is mostly upstream (the question, the point, the reader), but the demand is usually "fix my draft". A revision-only tool risks line-editing thinking drafts, which he says can't work (V47 17:30).
**Options.**
- (a) **Revision core:** Brief, Bridge, Architect, Flow, Finish.
- (b) **Full pipeline:** add Think, Unstick and Readers.
- (c) **Everything,** including Gym, Plan and Notes.
- (d) **Thinking-first:** Think, Unstick and Bridge, leaving revision to later.

### 6. Real scripts, model judgment, or both?
**Why it matters.** Scripts are consistent and cheap, but they need a parser, word lists and somewhere to run. Model-only judgment is portable but less consistent. The "views" (skim view, topic list, X-ray, introduction beside conclusion) are the highest-value scripts and the easiest to build.
**Options.**
- (a) Model judgment only.
- (b) A few view-producing scripts, with the model judging.
- (c) The fuller check catalogue (M1 to M51) with a filtering layer.

### 7. How much per pass, and how interactive?
**Why it matters.** He warns that many tools at once freeze writers (V12 51:02; V19 1:14:43). But some users want a full report.
**Options.**
- (a) One issue at a time, conversationally.
- (b) Up to three ranked issues per pass, top-down (skill_mapping's default).
- (c) A full audit on request, still ordered top-down.

### 8. Whose voice and vocabulary does the skill use?
**Why it matters.** His terms (index, uneven U, interference, stress position) are precise but unfamiliar. Naming him ties the skill to a real person; presenting his method as the skill's own risks misattribution. His teaching habits (one vivid image per idea, commit the fault and then fix it, "which version is easier?") could shape how the skill explains things.
**Options.**
- (a) His terms, with the skill described as based on Writer Science.
- (b) Plain descriptive terms, crediting him in the skill's documentation only.
- (c) His terms, no attribution. This is the weakest on honesty.

In every option the skill should not speak as him.

### 9. How to treat his empirical claims?
**Why it matters.** Several claims rest on unnamed studies (section 7.3). Stated as fact, they could mislead.
**Options.**
- (a) Present them as his reasoning, without numbers.
- (b) Omit them and give only the reader-effect explanation.
- (c) Verify the underlying research and cite it properly.

### 10. Where his advice is open, decide or ask?
**Why it matters.** Section 5 lists seven unresolved tensions, including the subject-slot priority, the six arrows versus no templates, the 5% passive and the AI edge cases. He models presenting such choices with their effects (V37 13:11).
**Options.**

### 11. What happens outside the method?
**Why it matters.** Requests will come for things he never covers: slides, technical documentation, grant budgets, plot, data presentation, proofreading.

### 12. What should persist, and does the skill see your notes?

### 13. How far into human processes should it go?
**Why it matters.** His late view locates voice and block in relationships: early readers, stakes, a community of practice (V44). Software can't be those people, but it can scaffold them.
**Options.**
- (a) Explain only.
