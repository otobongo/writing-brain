## V16: The EXACT Writing System to Make Readers Recommend Your Book (2025-07-04)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it solves

It is for experts writing a nonfiction book, especially a prescriptive or business-supporting one, who want readers to recommend it without a big marketing effort. Their problem is that school trained them to be "nuanced" and "objective." As a result the book's promise is fuzzy, its audience is everyone, and it takes no stand. Nobody can describe it quickly, recommend it easily, or identify with it (0:22).

### 2. Core argument

Word of mouth works socially, not logically. People recommend books to say something about themselves, sometimes without reading them. His friend recommended *Bad Therapy* without having read it; she was signalling that she questions the status quo (1:07–2:43). So the nonfiction author is in the business of persuasion, which needs ethos (credibility) and pathos (emotion) as well as logos (argument). Experts lean almost entirely on logos. The **3V framework** supplies the other two through positioning:

- a **Vow**: a promise clear enough that the wrong readers leave;
- a **Victim**: one specific person in pain, described so precisely that they feel seen;
- a **Villain**: a shared enemy that gives readers belonging and shows what you stand for.

A clear promise, the right reader and a delivered promise add up to five-star reviews and recommendations.

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 The book as an identity signal *(my label; a principle, not a procedure)*
- **What it is:** A recommendation is partly a statement about the person making it. A book spreads when its title and stance are bold enough that carrying it says something about you.
- **Why it works:** On social media the bold, emotional take gets shared more than the balanced one. This is not because it is more thoughtful; it is because it is more sharable (2:03). A reader can get the gist from the cover, and that is the point (1:35).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Ask what a reader would be saying about themselves by recommending the book.
  2. Check that the title and subtitle carry a position a person could repeat in one breath.
  3. If the book makes sense only after page 50, the cover is not doing any social work. Rework the title and subtitle.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** The title names a topic rather than a position. You cannot picture anyone bringing it up over coffee. Your pitch needs caveats.
- **When it does not apply:** He aims this at books whose goal is to be read and shared. He admits the mechanism is "not logical" (1:35). It is not a licence to be inaccurate; V19 (1:04:23) insists that claims rest on evidence.
- **Example:** Before: *Communication Practices in Hybrid Teams*. After: *Kill the Status Meeting: Why Your Team Talks More and Knows Less*.
- **Timestamp:** 1:35–2:43.

#### 3.2 Rebalancing ethos, pathos and logos
- **What it is:** Ethos is why the reader should trust you. Pathos is how the message feels. Logos is the argument itself (3:01–3:21).
- **Why it works:** Experts default to logic and nuance, but identity and trust run on emotion (14:23). A book that is pure logos can be correct and still inert.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Audit the pitch and the introduction. Where is your credibility visible?
  2. Where does the reader feel something at stake?
  3. Use the 3Vs to supply whatever is missing: the Vow and Victim for stakes, the Villain for stance.
- **Symptoms:** The pitch is all mechanism and no stakes. The author is absent. The reader is never addressed as someone with something to lose.
- **When it does not apply:** He gives no limit here. V19 (1:02:55) adds that peer-review communities reward caution.
- **Example:** Logos only: "Spaced practice improves retention." With pathos and ethos: "I watched my strongest students forget half the course by June. Spaced practice is why the other half didn't."
- **Timestamp:** 3:01, 14:23.

#### 3.3 The 3V framework *(his term)*
This is a positioning system with three parts, the Vow, the Victim and the Villain, set out in 3.4 to 3.6. He calls it both a framework and a system (3:21, 15:14).

#### 3.4 Vow *(his term)*: the promise
- **What it is:** A promise so clear that anyone who is not the right reader can opt out before buying.
- **Why it works:** "readers who are confused do not leave five-star reviews" (3:21). Vague promises attract the wrong crowd, and that crowd punishes you for wasting its time (3:44). A specific promise does two things: it draws in the right people and lets the wrong ones excuse themselves (5:28). The readers who stay know what to expect, and when you deliver they review the book and tell friends.
  - His evidence is two books on designing offers. The one with a fuzzy promise (*Value Proposition Design*, 2014) has about 2,300 reviews. Hormozi's *$100M Offers* (2021) has over 26,000. Its cover promise is bold, concrete and irresistible (3:44–4:34).
  - He uses the same filter on his channel: "nonfiction" goes in the title or the opening so fiction writers leave. Losing the wrong viewers beats collecting downvotes and salty comments (4:34).
- **How to apply (his steps, 5:28–7:47):**
  1. **Name the change.** For prescriptive nonfiction, answer: what will be different in the reader's life after the last page? "Learning" means behaviour changed. His example is the hot stove: the pain is information, and if you touch the stove again you have not learned anything (5:28–6:54).
  2. **Say it out loud.** Pitch the book in one tight sentence to a real person, then stop talking.
  3. **Watch their face and listen.** Sit through their confusion. Every puzzled look marks something to sharpen. Keep adjusting until a new person gets it in one or two sentences.
  4. **Lock it in.** When strangers get it on the first try, freeze that line. It becomes the subtitle, the back-cover line and the product-page line.
- **Symptoms:** You can describe the book only by its topic. The promise uses abstract nouns ("insights," "a framework," "a mindset") instead of a changed behaviour. People nod politely and ask what it is actually about. Reviews say it was not what they expected.
- **When it does not apply:** "Name the change" is framed for prescriptive books (5:28). For books of ideas, V18 (5:10) supplies the equivalent: the change is swapping an old idea for a new one.
- **Example:** Before: "A book about productivity for knowledge workers." After: "Finish your most important project every week in the first two hours of Monday."
- **Timestamp:** 3:21–7:47.

#### 3.5 Victim *(his term)*: the one reader in pain
- **What it is:** The specific person who is in pain and looking for relief. Write for the person whose hair is on fire, who thinks they cannot keep doing it this way (12:05–12:21).
- **Why it works:**
  - Early drafts often pack in tips for beginners and experts, solopreneurs and Fortune 500 executives. That feels like it widens the market, but it works only if the goal is a mass-market bestseller (7:47–8:36).
  - *Atomic Habits* managed it by presenting what is really a book about elite performance and deliberate practice as a book about a near-universal pain, not sticking to habits (8:36–8:58).
  - If the book is meant to attract clients, build authority or solve a specific problem, a broad audience weakens it.
  - Naming someone's pain precisely makes them stop scrolling and lean in, because you come across as someone who "gets it" rather than one more voice with advice (11:02).
  - A Hormozi clip makes the same point: if you describe prospects' pain in their own language and experiences, they infer that you can fix it. How specific you are about the pain becomes the pitch (11:02–12:05).
- **His own case (8:58–10:24):** He launched the channel as "I help writers." His inbox filled with fiction writers, fantasy world-builders and memoirists he could not really serve, some of whom mostly debated him. He realised his people are *experts* who need to turn knowledge into specific value for specific readers. He rewrote the homepage, the offer, the tone and the voice. He lost subscribers, but his leads improved, and sales calls started to feel like talking with people who were already halfway sold.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Decide what the book is for (clients, authority, bestseller). Unless it is aimed at the mass market, narrow the audience.
  2. Name one person and the moment they are stuck.
  3. Describe the pain in their words and situations, not yours.
  4. Remove chapters and tips written for other readers.
  5. Re-voice the book and your marketing for that one person.
- **Symptoms:** A chapter on mindset sits next to one on advanced analytics. The audience is phrased as "anyone who wants to…". The pain is described in category terms ("communication challenges") rather than lived detail. People you cannot help keep reaching out.
- **When it does not apply:** If the goal is a mass-market bestseller, broad pain positioning is the right strategy (8:36–8:58). V17's Booking Agent is deliberately broad for the same reason.
- **Example:** Before: "For leaders at every level." After: "For the engineer promoted to manager three months ago who still reviews code at midnight because delegating feels slower."
- **Timestamp:** 7:47–12:21.

#### 3.6 Villain: picking a fight / naming a shared enemy *(he calls it "the villain" at 13:51)*
- **What it is:** Name what your reader is against.
- **Why it works:**
  - People crave belonging, and a shared enemy is one of the quickest ways to create it. That makes a fight the fastest way to get readers on your side (12:21).
  - When you call out what readers are against, they trust you to lead them toward what they want. It shows what you stand for as well as what you know (12:48).
  - In the 1980s Harley-Davidson could not beat Honda and Yamaha on performance, so it sold identity instead: loud pipes, leather, American steel. It sold rebellion, not motorcycles (12:48–13:10). Yeti versus flimsy disposable gear and Patagonia versus fast fashion are the same move.
  - His channel's villain is polished AI fluff that ignores readers, which he calls "writing slop." Calling it out makes the channel a beacon for writers who care about readers (13:10–13:51). *Bad Therapy* takes swings at the therapy industry, the drug companies and soft parenting (13:51).
  - A villain taps pathos and ethos at once (14:23). It attracts the right people and repels the wrong ones.
- **How to apply (his steps, 14:42–15:14):**
  1. Think about your specific reader and what they already hate but may not have named: a broken system, a lazy trend, or a shiny shortcut that does real harm.
  2. Call it out plainly and make the villain visible. Copywriters call this "throwing stones at their enemies." You are standing up on the reader's behalf, and they will cheer you on.
  3. Show how the book gives readers the tools to fight back and win. They will then use your arguments in conversation, as his friend did, and carry the book "like a badge of honor" (15:14).
- **Symptoms:** The book argues *for* something but never *against* anything. The reader has no one to disagree with. It is unclear what the author stands for.
- **When it does not apply:** He does not qualify it in this video. V19 (35:19–40:04) tempers it: if you need a community of peers to accept you, concede before you attack. His villains are always systems, trends or shortcuts, never individual people.
- **Example:** Before: "Better onboarding improves retention." After: "The 40-slide welcome deck is where new hires' enthusiasm goes to die. Here's what week one should look like instead."
- **Timestamp:** 12:21–15:14.

### 4. His stances in V16
- **Argues against:**
  - School advice for books meant to be shared: be nuanced, be objective. He says readers hate it (0:22), and that authors are in the business of persuasion, not logic and nuance (2:43–3:01).
  - Vague promises (3:44).
  - Writing to everyone, unless you are aiming at the mass market (8:14–8:58).
- **Changed his mind:** He repositioned his own channel from "writers" to "expert writers," accepting the lost subscribers (8:58–10:24).
- **Warnings:**
  - The wrong readers punish you in reviews.
  - Broad, beginner-to-expert content is a bestseller strategy, and following it can make your book fail when your goal is different (7:47).

## V17: 6 Types of Nonfiction Books to Start Writing Today (2025-07-13)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it solves

It is for busy experts and professionals who feel they "have a book in them" but are stuck. He says they need a model, not a ghostwriter: a book type that has worked for others with the same goal (0:00).

### 2. Core argument

Choose a proven book type that matches your goal. The type then tells you:

- who the reader is;
- what shape and length the book takes;
- what stance to take;
- which moves to make.

He names six types: **Personal Brand Builder**, **New Authority**, **Lead Getter**, **Booking Agent**, **Legacy Book** and **Gift**. Three beliefs run under all six:

1. Writing is how you *become* an expert: thinking on paper creates knowledge.
2. Evidence and stories should come before claims.
3. A book written without the reader in mind is a diary.

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 Choosing the book type by goal *(the video's overall framework)*

| Type *(his terms)* | Your goal | Reader | Shape and stance | His model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Personal Brand Builder (4:46) | Pivot a mid-career identity into thought leadership | A community you want to lead | Driven by questions; language bent to that community | Ana Lorena Fábrega, *The Learning Game* |
| New Authority (10:53) | Be the go-to voice for commentary | Anyone invested in the status quo | An argument that flips the status quo; no hedging | Aaron Stupple, *The Sovereign Child* |
| Lead Getter (18:20) | Qualified consulting leads | Prospects facing the problem just before yours | Short, skimmable, practical, one urgent problem | April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* |
| Booking Agent (24:55) | Podcast and stage invitations | Mass market | One transmissible idea; broadly useful; never pitches | Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, *Never Split the Difference* |
| Legacy Book (30:59) | Pass on hard-won wisdom | One person (child, mentee) | Idiosyncratic; a story behind every lesson | Anthony Pompliano, *How to Live an Extraordinary Life* |
| Gift (36:43) | Show a truth others have not yet seen | Readers living through the same moment | Narrative; calm authority; about the reader's transformation | Paul Millard, *The Pathless Path* |

- **How to apply:**
  1. State your definition of success for the book.
  2. Match it to a type.
  3. Adopt that type's reader, shape and stance.
  4. Study the model book.
- **Symptoms of a mismatch:** An ideas book sold as a practical guide, which is exactly V18's diagnosis of Dan Koe. A lead book that publishers try to stretch into mass-market self-help (17:04). A booking-agent book that reads like a brochure.

#### 3.2 Personal Brand Builder *(his term)*
- **What it is:** A book that turns a burned-out, mid-career professional into a thought leader in their own field, on their own terms. It starts with questions, not answers.
- **Who it is for:** Someone about ten years into a career that is tied to their identity (teacher, lawyer, healthcare worker), with no agency, who does not want to retrain (0:00–0:52).
  - His model is Ana Lorena Fábrega ("Miss Fab"), a teacher who was disheartened by what schools made her teach. Her book *The Learning Game* led to a role as ambassador for Synthesis, an AI maths tutor (1:41–2:28).
  - Her book began as a list of questions and ended as her message (2:28–2:43).
- **Why it works:**
  - "Writing is the process through which you become an expert" (2:43). You become a teacher by teaching and an authority by being an author (3:13).
  - Writing that transcribes ideas already fully formed is the boring kind: memos, emails, the worst nonfiction (3:34).
  - Expert writing extends thinking. You lay your ideas out in all their mess, fill the gaps, cut and refine, and that process generates new knowledge (4:07).
  - Pressing questions that everyone half-asks but nobody has answered are a sign you are writing something valuable (3:34).
  - You need no one's permission; you write because you have questions, not because you have arrived (4:46).
- **How to apply (5:34–9:13):**
  1. **Ask "why?" five times** about a bad day at work until you reach a systemic question. His chain ends at "why are we teaching this?" He says: "Bingo." (5:34–6:04)
  2. **Capture stories weekly or daily.** He keeps a weekly log of "teachable moments" from class (6:04–6:42). Write the story first and extract the lesson afterwards (see 3.3).
  3. **Define your audience and bend your language around it.** Fábrega's book leans on references her readers (tech founders and CEOs) already share: Taleb, Munger, Ryan Holiday, Tim Ferriss, Naval (7:38–8:34). Picking an ideal reader is not enough if you then write the book *you* want.
  4. **Don't wait until you're ready.** Writing is a learnable skill; she hired a coach (8:34–9:13).
- **Symptoms:** Waiting for credentials before starting. Starting from a thesis. General references that signal no particular community.
- **When it does not apply:** He gives no limit. Unlike the Lead Getter, this type does not need a proven method, because it is built on questions.
- **Example:** Asking why five times: "The client meeting went badly." → "I didn't have the numbers." → "The dashboard was stale." → "Nobody owns the dashboard." → "Why does every team assume data is somebody else's job?" That last question could carry a book.
- **Timestamp:** 0:00–9:13.

#### 3.3 Story first, lesson second: letting claims come out of evidence *(my label; his underlying rule)*
- **What it is:** An idea has three parts: a claim, the evidence, and an explanation that links the evidence to the claim. Start with the evidence, analyse it, and let the claim emerge (6:42–7:38).
- **Why it works:** Writers who start from the claim go hunting for evidence and end up with a quote or data point that only seems related. It is a square peg in a round hole. Or they make up flimsy stories to prove the point (7:38).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Keep a log of real experiences.
  2. Reread it and write out what each story actually shows.
  3. Draft the claim that the stories support.
  4. Write the explanation that links each story to that claim.
- **Symptoms:** An anecdote that illustrates a neighbouring point, not this one. A quotation that "sort of" fits. A story that feels manufactured.
- **When it does not apply:** He does not say.
- **Example:** Claim first: "Remote teams need more rituals," then hunting for a story. Evidence first: three logged incidents of dropped handoffs between time zones, from which the claim emerges: "Handoffs fail at the time-zone boundary, not in meetings."
- **Timestamp:** 6:42–7:38.

#### 3.4 New Authority *(his term)*
- **What it is:** A book of ideas that overturns the accepted view. It offers an argument rather than advice.
- **Why it works:**
  - Most nonfiction is useful but forgettable (9:40). To establish authority, a book needs an argument, because books that challenge the status quo get talked about (9:55).
  - It is not about being edgy for its own sake. It makes you someone people turn to for insight and commentary (9:55).
  - *The Sovereign Child* argues that children deserve full, unconditional freedom, and it answers the objections that come to mind the moment you hear the idea (10:53).
  - It spread by word of mouth and got its author onto Tim Ferriss's podcast. Readers buy extra copies to hand to friends so they can argue about it, not because they agree with every word (10:53–11:39).
  - He goes as far as saying that if your book doesn't make someone mad, it probably isn't worth writing (9:13).
- **How to apply (11:39–14:08):**
  1. **Start with the idea that bothers you,** the contrarian take that keeps coming out of your mouth. Ask Peter Thiel's job-interview question: what important truth do very few people agree with you on? (11:39–12:13)
  2. **Don't hold back.** In formal academic writing nuance and restraint are essential. In a conversation-starting book, avoid hedging: make your boldest case and let readers add their own qualifications. Naval accepted about 90% of *The Sovereign Child* but said he would be a tyrant about maths and reading (12:13–12:33).
  3. **Build the argument in layers.** Persuasion is a bridge from where readers are to where you want them. Each story, example, analogy and answer to an objection is one stone in that bridge (12:33–12:58).
  4. **Welcome disagreement.** An idea is only an idea if someone else could argue against it; nobody needs a book proving that water is wet. Truth claims need evidence and reasoning (13:18–13:36). Test the idea: what is it, and who would disagree? Its author sharing his negative reviews shows how disagreement signals engagement (13:36).
  5. **Make it a conversation starter.** Write lines that readers highlight and share ("bangers"). He refers to his separate video on 31 rhetorical techniques (14:08).
- **Symptoms:** A list of advice with no thesis. A central idea nobody could dispute. Claims hedged until they say nothing.
- **When it does not apply:** Academic writing, which needs restraint (12:13). Practical lead books, which he calls "not an idea book" (17:27).
- **Example:** Before: "Five tips for better meetings." After, an arguable thesis: "Most meetings exist to spread blame, not to make decisions. Cancel any meeting that has no named decision-maker."
- **Timestamp:** 9:13–14:44.

#### 3.5 Lead Getter *(his term)*
- **What it is:** A short, practical, targeted book that solves one urgent problem for prospective clients. He calls it part lead magnet and part sales rep (17:27).
- **Why it works:**
  - Consultants get tired of explaining the basics again and again to each new client (14:44).
  - Blog posts are too shallow, and workshops or webinars take too much effort and do not scale (15:55–16:08).
  - April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome* teaches her positioning method once, for good, and filters leads: if you like the book, you will like working with her (16:08–16:33).
  - Publishers wanted her to write mass-market self-help ("position your life"). She refused. She then doubled her rates, was booked months in advance, and every new client had already read the book (16:33–17:27).
  - Readers get immersed in your approach and your language, so the ones who reach out arrive halfway sold (17:27).
- **How to apply (18:20–20:56):**
  1. **Choose one specific, urgent problem.** Don't throw in the kitchen sink: it is "a problem solver, not an encyclopedia" (18:51).
     - Ideally, pick the problem that comes just *before* the one your consultancy solves. A firm that helps startups scale might write about finding product–market fit. A leadership-development firm might write about the move from individual contributor to manager.
     - Alternatively, give your full method as a do-it-yourself guide. That serves readers who will never pay much while warming up the ones who will pay for a done-for-you service (18:51–19:40).
  2. **Test the method in the real world first.** A book cannot stand over the reader's shoulder to correct misunderstandings.
     - He admits he is not ready to write his own method book, because he is still refining the method for authors outside academia (20:01).
     - Teach it one-to-one, in workshops and through content until it is clear, effective and irrefutable.
  3. **Build the book like a product:** short, skimmable, delivering a quick win.
     - Dunford's book is under 30,000 words, with colour-coded case studies.
     - His rule of thumb: could a client finish it on a short flight? Books that feel good to finish get recommended (20:35–20:56).
- **Symptoms:** Trying to impress prospects with sheer volume. Exercises that feel like homework. A framework nobody has tested. Pressure to broaden the appeal.
- **When it does not apply:** When the method is not yet proven (20:01). When the goal is a bestseller or a big idea.
- **Example:** A bookkeeper's book. Not "Everything About Small-Business Finance," but "Close Your Books in One Afternoon a Month," the problem just before "we run your finance function."
- **Timestamp:** 14:44–20:56.

#### 3.6 Booking Agent *(his term)*
- **What it is:** A broadly useful, story-driven book built around one bold idea, designed to get you invited onto podcasts and stages. It is designed "not just to teach, but for reach" (24:55).
- **Why it works:**
  - Podcasts invite authors, not businesses (22:05).
  - Chris Voss wrote *Never Split the Difference* with the ghostwriter Tahl Raz. Nothing in it is a sales pitch, and it barely mentions his firm, Black Swan Group, until the final third (22:31–23:16).
  - Voss was introduced everywhere as the author of the book, not as the founder of the firm. The media pointed to the book, and the book quietly pointed to the consultancy (23:39).
  - Hosts will not share a sales pitch with their audience, because it cheapens their own brand (24:14).
- **How to apply (25:25–28:04):**
  1. **Make it useful to the mass market.** Keep the core message but aim the stories, tactics and applications broadly. The book has to stand on its own and be useful whether or not the reader ever hires you (25:25–26:24).
  2. **Reduce your message to one powerful idea** built to spread like a virus: easy to catch on first exposure and easy to pass on. Voss's title carries the counterintuitive core idea, that you don't settle for half, and the reader thinks "wait, I don't have to compromise?" (26:24–26:55)
  3. **Lead with the book, and the business will follow.** Do not turn it into a brochure; for now, forget you have an offer. Salesy writing erodes trust.
     - He read Voss's book two or three times before he noticed the consultancy behind it.
     - Weave in your philosophy and method, but never pitch. Let the most motivated readers connect the dots (26:55–28:04).
- **Symptoms:** Calls to action inside chapters. The firm's name recurring. A core idea that takes a paragraph to state.
- **When it does not apply:** This is the deliberate exception to the Victim principle (V16 3.5): here broad appeal is the point, because the goal is reach.
- **Example:** A title that carries one idea, such as "Meetings Are Debt," a counterintuitive line a podcast host can repeat, versus "A Practical Guide to Organisational Meeting Efficiency."
- **Timestamp:** 20:56–28:04.

#### 3.7 Legacy Book *(his term)*
- **What it is:** A book that captures hard-won wisdom for family, friends or a mentee. It is not driven by the market, so it is free to be quirky.
- **Why it works:**
  - Experience cannot be inherited; you cannot put it in your will (29:57). You can leave an intellectual legacy as well as a material one (30:18).
  - Anthony Pompliano's *How to Live an Extraordinary Life* grew out of private letters to his children. It has 65 short letters with one idea each. A typical chapter gives a lesson, the story and the mistake behind it, and advice for applying it (29:04–29:57).
  - There is no template. He tries to help writers find their "singularity," the thing that makes them weird and unique. Voice, stories, format and organisation together make a book as strange and singular as its author (30:38–30:59).
  - The aim is not to go viral but to avoid dying with your best lessons still in your head (30:59).
- **How to apply (31:55–32:47):**
  1. **Write for one person:** a child, a grandchild, a mentee. Even if others will read it, writing to one person gives the message specificity and earnestness. Ta-Nehisi Coates's *Between the World and Me* was written as a message to his son (31:55–32:11).
  2. **No lesson without a story.** This is not the place for grand theory; pair every point with the experience that taught it (32:11–32:29).
  3. **Let go of publishing.** Treat the book as a gift or as notes to yourself, like Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* (32:29–32:47).
- **Symptoms:** Abstract maxims with no story behind them. Writing to "readers" instead of to someone. Imitating a template.
- **When it does not apply:** When the goal is leads or virality.
- **Example:** Before: "Always save for hard times." After: "The winter the mill closed, your grandfather and I had eleven weeks of rent in a coffee tin. That tin is why you grew up in this house. Keep one."
- **Timestamp:** 28:04–32:47.

#### 3.8 Gift *(his term)*
- **What it is:** A book by someone who has lived through something important and seen something true that others have not yet recognised. It opens a window onto the world (33:30).
- **Why it works:**
  - Paul Millard began *The Pathless Path* by writing to make sense of how lost he felt after leaving his job, feelings many people shared during the pandemic (33:30–34:36).
  - Over five years he held nearly 400 "curiosity conversations," free one-to-one calls with readers, and heard the same themes again and again: hidden dissatisfaction, fear and unspoken dreams. His writing gave readers words for something they already knew (34:36–35:19).
  - Books like this present ideas with clarity and confidence, as if they were obvious truths: no "maybe," no "it depends." The author speaks with calm authority and never stops to doubt. It reads as effortless even though tough choices sit behind it (35:19–36:43).
- **How to apply (36:43–38:16):**
  1. **Write the book only you can write.** An economist or a philosopher might have the same ideas, but your own journey makes them irrefutable (37:32).
  2. **Focus on the reader's transformation.** A book without the reader in mind is a diary. Even when the story is about you, it is really about the reader: if it works, the reader imagines walking in your footsteps (37:54–38:16).
  3. **Apply the section test:** what should the reader understand, feel or do differently after this section? If you cannot answer, cut the section (38:16).
- **Symptoms:** Memoir-like passages with no payoff for the reader. Hedging. Theory that any expert could have written.
- **When it does not apply:** He does not say.
- **Example of the section test:** A section about your year abroad stays if it makes readers see their own stalled careers differently. The paragraphs about visa paperwork go.
- **Timestamp:** 32:47–38:16.

#### 3.9 Curiosity conversations *(Millard's term, which he endorses; see also V19 1:11:07)*
- **What it is:** Unstructured one-to-one calls with readers to learn what they care about, in their own words.
- **Why it works:** The recurring themes tell you what readers are living through and give you their language. They also confirm that your experience is shared (34:36–35:19).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Offer short calls to people who read your work.
  2. Listen without an agenda.
  3. Log recurring themes and phrases.
  4. Use the log to choose topics and wording.
- **Symptoms that you need it:** You are guessing at what readers feel, and your pieces get polite silence.
- **Example:** After twenty calls with new managers you notice that twelve use the phrase "I'm still doing my old job." That phrase becomes the title of a chapter.
- **Timestamp:** 34:36.

#### 3.10 Sharpen the axe *(his closing idea)*
- **What it is:** If you want to write a book, hold off writing it until you have a blueprint. He points to his next video on his method for starting a book (38:16).
- **Note:** This sits in tension with "don't wait until you're ready" (8:34). I read it as: start writing to think now, but plan the *book* before you draft the *book*.

### 4. His stances in V17
- **Argues against:**
  - Writing as the transcription of ideas already fully formed (3:34).
  - Starting with the claim and then hunting for evidence (7:38).
  - Waiting for authority or permission (4:46, 8:34).
  - Hedging in idea books (12:13) and gift books (35:19), while granting that academic writing needs restraint.
  - The mass-market advice publishers give when your goal is leads (17:04).
  - Salesy books (26:55).
  - Writing a method book before the method is proven. He applies this to himself (20:01).
  - Encyclopaedic, show-everything-you-know books (18:51, 20:35).
- **Warnings:**
  - Most nonfiction is forgettable (9:40).
  - A book with no reader in mind is a diary (37:54).
- **Minor inconsistency:** He opens by saying you don't need a ghostwriter, you need a model (0:00), yet the Voss model used a ghostwriter (22:31).

- The authors Fábrega references: Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Lindy, skin in the game, antifragility), Charlie Munger (mental models), Ryan Holiday (stoicism), Tim Ferriss (anti-goals), Naval Ravikant.

## V18: What Went Wrong with Dan Koe's Book (2025-07-22)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it solves

It is for creators with one-person businesses and big social followings, and for first-time nonfiction authors, who are moving from social-media writing (tweets, scripts, sales pages) to a book. Their problem is that a book is a different genre with different conventions and language choices. A book written like a stream of posts disappoints even loyal fans (1:47).

### 2. Core argument

Dan Koe's self-published *The Art of Focus* damaged his personal brand. The book may hold value, but readers could not *perceive* it (9:14). He read dozens of reviews, set aside the one-star rants, and kept the two- and three-star ones from fans who wanted to like the book (1:47–2:38). Four failures recur:

1. **The promise does not match the contents.** The title says "practical"; the chapter titles are riddles.
2. **Purpose.** The content does not match what his audience comes to him for.
3. **Structure.** The ideas do not cohere, leaving orphaned ideas.
4. **Voice.** The author on the page sounds like a preachy guru and says "you" constantly.

The root cause is genre. Koe wanted to write an ideas book but sold it as a practical, problem-solving book (35:20).

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 Mining the sympathetic reviews *(my label)*
- **What it is:** To diagnose a book, read the two- and three-star reviews, which come from readers who wanted to like it. Ignore the one-star rants and look for complaints that recur (1:47–2:38).
- **Why it works:** Fans who are let down report real friction rather than hostility, and a complaint that repeats points to a structural cause.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Collect the middle-rated reactions: reviews, or beta-reader comments.
  2. Group them into recurring themes.
  3. Trace each theme to a cause (promise, purpose, structure, voice).
- **Symptoms it catches:** Reviews saying "hard to explain what it's about," "disjointed," "needed an editor."
- **When it does not apply:** He does not say. V19 (1:11:07) adds that you should test with real readers *before* publishing.
- **Example:** Beta comments for a draft: "lost me in chapter 3," "felt like separate blog posts," "not sure what to do with this." Three different phrasings of one cause: no through-line.
- **Timestamp:** 1:47–2:38.

#### 3.2 Chapter titles as promises *(his framing: "name your chapters as the promise of that chapter")*
- **What it is:** Each chapter title states the takeaway: what the reader will be able to do, or which new idea will replace which old one (3:24–4:21).
- **Why it works:**
  - The book's subtitle says "practical," which promises help the reader can put into practice. Koe's chapters are called "The New Rich," "The Self," "The Game" and "The Lens" (2:38–3:24).
  - None of them tells readers what they will learn or be able to do. Because the promise and the contents do not line up, readers lose trust before page one. He likens it to clickbait: a video called "How to Write a Bestselling Business Book" that turns into a deep dive on breathwork (2:38).
  - "A chapter makes a promise to the reader" (7:12): pay attention to this instead of 500 other things, and here is what you will get. Readers who want that value will happily trade their attention for it.
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each chapter, find its central idea. He found Koe's in the last paragraph of "The New Rich."
  2. Ask: by the end, what will the reader be able to *do*, or what new idea will they hold, and which old idea will it replace? (4:21)
  3. Rewrite the title as that takeaway. His version: "The New Rich" becomes "Design your life around stillness, not status" (6:05).
  4. Put it both in the table of contents and at the top of the chapter.
- **Symptoms:** Vague noun phrases. Inside references that make sense only after reading the chapter. A table of contents that says nothing about what the reader gains.
- **When it does not apply:** He frames this for prescriptive, practical nonfiction (5:10). His closing diagnosis implies a true ideas book is a different genre, but even then the reader's "action" is swapping an old idea for a new one.
- **Example:** Before: "Chapter 4: The Inbox." After: "Chapter 4: Answer email twice a day without missing what's urgent."
- **Timestamp:** 2:38–7:12.

#### 3.3 Practical problems and conceptual problems
- **What it is:**
  - A practical problem is solved by changing what you do.
  - A conceptual problem is solved by changing what you understand.
  - Conceptual problems sit upstream of behaviour: a better understanding of, say, climate change ultimately tells us how to act. So even an ideas book asks the reader to do something, namely replace an old idea with a new one (4:21–5:10).
- **Why it works:** It gives every chapter an outcome to promise, even when the chapter is about ideas.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Classify the problem each chapter solves.
  2. If it is practical, promise the actions.
  3. If it is conceptual, name the old idea and the new idea that replaces it.
- **Symptoms:** Chapters that "explore" a theme without changing either behaviour or belief.
- **Example:** Conceptual: "Motivation is not a feeling you wait for; it's a result of starting." Practical: "Start the task for five minutes before deciding whether you feel like it."
- **Timestamp:** 4:21–5:10 (and again in V19 7:44 and 10:39).

#### 3.4 Readers don't owe you attention; value must be perceived
- **What it is:** Readers owe the writer nothing, so a writer has to earn attention by giving clear value on every page (7:47–8:24).
- **Why it works:**
  - School hides this, because teachers are paid to read your essays (8:24). The belief "I worked so hard on this that they will have to read it" is left over from school.
  - Value has to be *perceivable*. Koe may well deliver value, but the way the book is written and its ideas are positioned stops readers from seeing it (9:14).
- **How to apply:** On each page, ask whether a busy stranger could see what is in it for them. See 3.5 for the method.
- **Symptoms:** Reviews like "a book on focus that couldn't hold my focus" or "added nothing though I wanted it to" (7:47–9:14). Readers who finish and still regret the time.
- **Example:** A chapter opening with a paragraph of musings on attention in ancient philosophy, when the reader came to learn how to protect a working morning.
- **Timestamp:** 7:47–9:52.

#### 3.5 The purpose chain *(my label for his book-planning sequence)*
- **What it is:** Before touching ideas or prose, set out a chain: the writer's goal → the reader who must read the book for that goal to come true → that reader's main pain → the table of contents as a bridge → a filter for every element (10:47–13:56).
- **Why it works:**
  - When a book's ideas do not match the purpose the reader brought to it, trust breaks and the book goes in the bin (9:52). Koe's audience comes to him for business and personal-branding advice; his spiritual reflections have nothing to do with that (9:52).
  - The chain becomes the razor that decides what goes in the book (13:56).
  - It also keeps editing from being about personal taste. Editing means helping the writer name their goals, then working backwards from the needs and interests of the reader who will help achieve them, not applying the editor's pet preferences or arbitrary rules (14:32–15:26).
  - "Books are tools." Without a reader you have a diary, and useful books are the ones that get finished, shared and recommended (15:26).
- **How to apply (his steps):**
  1. **Step away** from the book, the prose, the ideas, even the reader. Ask: what is your goal, your definition of success? A personal brand, clients, a legacy, sharing an idea. Write it down (10:47–11:35).
  2. **Who needs to read this for that goal to come true?** For example, a leadership consultant needs newly promoted managers. What do you know about them? They are short on time, will not sit through a 300-page armchair book, and might prefer audio (11:35–12:30).
  3. **Pin down their main problem or pain.** It organises the ideas and the promise in the title (12:30).
  4. **Design the table of contents as a bridge** from their situation to the solution. For a practical problem, the chapters are actions. For a mindset problem, the chapters move them from current beliefs to replacement beliefs (12:30–13:23).
  5. **Filter.** Test every story, idea and example: does it help this reader solve this problem? If not, cut it, however good your spiritual-journey story is (13:23–14:32).
- **Symptoms:** Reviews saying "What does this have to do with focus, persuasive writing or entrepreneurship?" (9:52). Readers calling the book "unfocused" or saying it "meanders." Side roads that interested the author but are irrelevant to the reader (14:32).
- **When it does not apply:** He suggests fiction may run on personal taste; this chain is for practical, prescriptive nonfiction (14:32).
- **Example:** Goal: bookings for a nutrition practice. Reader: parents of children newly diagnosed with food allergies. Pain: fear at every meal. Table of contents: "Clear your kitchen in one weekend," then "Read any label in ten seconds," and so on. Filter: cut the chapter on your own marathon training.
- **Timestamp:** 9:52–15:26.

#### 3.6 The 3D cube puzzle: coherence at every level *(his classroom metaphor)*
- **What it is:** Writing is like a 3D cube puzzle. Each small piece must fit its neighbours for the whole cube to hold its shape. Sentences make paragraphs, paragraphs make sections, sections make chapters, chapters make the book (15:57–16:48). He calls this coherence and cohesion.
- **Why it works:** A sentence can be fine on its own. But if it does not link back to what came before and lean forward into what comes next, the reader gets lost. The result is gaps and "orphaned ideas" (16:48–18:31).
- **How to apply:**
  1. At each level (sentence, paragraph, section, chapter), check that the unit links backwards and sets up what comes next.
  2. Mark any unit that could be moved elsewhere without anyone noticing. That is an orphan.
- **Symptoms:** Any single paragraph is "fine," but the reader cannot say how it relates to the book's promise. Reviews like "no structure, no thread; I kept forgetting what I'd read" (15:57).
- **When it does not apply:** Unplanned, exploratory "pantser" writing (Stephen King's distinction, as he tells it, between pantsers and planners) is fine, even nice, for developing a practice and exploring ideas. When you *compile* those ideas into a book, you need to think in structures (16:48–17:41).
- **Example:** An orphan: a vivid paragraph about a monk's daily routine dropped into a chapter on pricing strategy, with no sentence linking it to pricing.
- **Timestamp:** 15:26–18:31.

#### 3.7 Readers assume every sentence is there for a reason (Grice's maxims)
- **What it is:** Everyone who speaks a language assumes that ideas cohere: this sentence follows that one for a reason. Readers can only read on the assumption that you meant the sequence (18:31–19:21).
- **Why it works (as a diagnosis):** When the links are missing, the reader has to do the work.
  - They invent a connection and misread you.
  - Or they blame themselves ("I must be the stupid one") and get frustrated from working too hard.
  - Or they skip over the gap, which means they are no longer reading (18:31–20:19).
- **How to apply:** For each pair of adjacent sentences or paragraphs, say out loud why B follows A. If you cannot, add the link or move B.
- **Symptoms:** Beta readers who "don't see the connection," misread your point, or say they felt dumb.
- **Example:** "Sleep debt accumulates. Most offices keep the lights on until 9 p.m." The reader has to guess at the link. With it: "Sleep debt accumulates, and office design makes it worse: most offices keep the lights on until 9 p.m."
- **Timestamp:** 18:31–20:19.

#### 3.8 Context first, meaning second (the red-yellow-blue test)
- **What it is:** He asks viewers to picture the colours "red, yellow, blue" and then "blue, yellow, red." Yellow leans orange after red and green after blue. The same word shifts meaning with its context. Words mean things only in relation to one another (20:19–21:12).
- **Why it works:** Order is part of meaning, so the sequence of ideas is itself a message.
- **How to apply:** Decide what the reader must already have in mind for each idea to mean what you intend, and put that first.
- **Symptoms:** An idea that seems to "mean something different" to readers than you intended, usually because it came before its context.
- **Example:** "The trial failed" means one thing after "we expected the drug to work" and another after "we designed the trial to test the placebo effect."
- **Timestamp:** 20:19–21:12.

#### 3.9 An index at the start of every unit
- **What it is:** Every unit of discourse opens with an "index" that sets out the high-level idea up front. In a paragraph that is usually the first sentence; larger units get a larger index. The rest of the unit unpacks it with examples and evidence (21:12–22:07).
- **Why it works:** Context comes first (3.8), and readers want to be guided rather than wade through a pile of paragraphs (25:41).
- **How to apply:** At the top of every paragraph, section and chapter, state the idea that the rest will develop.
- **Symptoms:** The point surfaces mid-paragraph or not at all. Skimming the first sentences tells you nothing.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated here. V19 (53:46) allows the point at the *end* as an advanced, storytelling choice.
- **Example:** Before: a paragraph that walks through three studies and ends "so remote work may help focus." After: it opens with "Remote work helps deep focus but hurts quick coordination," then the studies follow.
- **Timestamp:** 21:12–22:07.

#### 3.10 One paragraph, one idea: underline and promote
- **What it is:** A paragraph is the unit of one idea: "one paragraph, one simple thought" (22:33).
- **How to apply (his client exercise, 22:33):**
  1. Underline the main point of the paragraph or section.
  2. If you cannot, rework the paragraph.
  3. If you can, move that statement up to the index position and rewrite the rest to follow from it.
- **Symptoms:** Nothing is underlinable, or two competing points are.
- **Example:** A paragraph whose best sentence ("The cheapest fix is a checklist") is its sixth. Promote it to first and cut what doesn't support it.
- **Timestamp:** 22:07–22:33.

#### 3.11 Label the relationships with transitions
- **What it is:** Use phrases such as "in addition," "by contrast" and "for example" to show how sentences relate (22:33–23:07).
- **Why it works:** Unlabelled relationships make the reader fill the gap and ask why this sentence follows that one.
- **When it does not apply:** When the relationship is painfully obvious (23:07).
- **Example:** "Prices rose. Demand held." becomes "Prices rose; *even so*, demand held."
- **Timestamp:** 22:33–23:07.

#### 3.12 A table of ideas for each chapter
- **What it is:** Just as a book has a table of contents, each chapter needs a "table of ideas," an outline (23:07).
- **When it applies:** When you compile a book. It is not for exploratory drafting (see 3.6).
- **Example:** Before drafting a chapter, list its five point-sentences in order and check that each leads to the next.
- **Timestamp:** 23:07.

#### 3.13 Think in fractals, and the uneven U *(the uneven U is Eric Hayot's term)*
- **What it is:**
  - **Fractals:** The index-then-discussion pattern repeats at every scale: paragraph, section, chapter, book (23:07–24:03).
  - **The uneven U** (credited to "an academic named Eric Hayot"):
    1. Start a paragraph or section with an index statement (a claim or topic sentence).
    2. Move down to more and more specific material until you reach a piece of evidence: an anecdote, example, data or a quote.
    3. Don't just drop the evidence. Bring it back into your web of interpretation, climbing back to a general level.
    4. End at a *more general* level than you began (24:03–25:41).
- **Why it works:** Ending higher than you began gives momentum. You push off into the larger context instead of circling back to the start. When the same pattern runs at every scale, the book feels cohesive, and readers get "rails that they can ride on," so even complex ideas feel easy to follow (24:55–25:41).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Open the unit with a claim.
  2. Step down through explanation to concrete evidence.
  3. Interpret the evidence.
  4. Step back up.
  5. Close on a broader implication that leads into the next unit.
  6. Check that sections and chapters follow the same shape.
- **Symptoms:** Evidence left undiscussed at the end of a paragraph. Paragraphs that end where they began. Chapters that stop rather than lead onward.
- **Example:** Claim: "Checklists work because they move memory out of the head." → Specific: "In surgery, the checklist covers steps experts skip when tired." → Evidence: one hospital's error log before and after. → Interpretation: the errors fell in exactly the steps that were written down. → Close, higher: "Any team whose work depends on memory under pressure is running an unguarded system."
- **Timestamp:** 23:07–25:41.

#### 3.14 The author persona is not the author
- **What it is:** The Dan Koe on the page is not the flesh-and-blood Dan Koe. He is a persona made of sentences and language choices, and readers do not have the real person as context (26:30–27:28, 34:19–35:20).
- **Why it works:** Reviewers were not rejecting Koe's ideas; they were rejecting the voice on the page. One said every sentence need not be an aphorism. Another found calling his ideas "teachings" pompous, like a sage talking down (25:41–26:30). If the persona feels arrogant or preachy, readers bail however much they like the YouTube channel.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Read the draft as if the author were a stranger. What kind of person do these sentences create?
  2. Adjust word choices, sentence subjects and claim strength until the persona is "a partner and not a preacher" (35:20).
- **Symptoms:** A string of aphorisms. Grandiose labels for your own ideas ("my teachings"). A tone that talks down.
- **Example:** Before: "Here is the first teaching: the undisciplined mind is a slave." After: "Distraction usually isn't a character flaw. It's what happens when nothing on the desk is clearly more important than the phone."
- **Timestamp:** 25:41–27:28, 34:19–35:20.

#### 3.15 Take "you" out of the subject position (the stage and the spotlight)
- **What it is:** Habitually making "you" the grammatical subject sounds preachy and condescending (27:28–28:19). He says this is not a rule against personal pronouns.
- **Why it works:**
  - Picture the text as a stage. Each paragraph or section is a scene with two or three characters, four at most; any more is hard to follow (28:19–29:43).
  - Whatever sits in the subject position is the actor in the spotlight, the thing the reader is watching (29:43).
  - Putting "you" there is like dragging the reader out of the audience onto the stage and breaking the fourth wall. It feels like a lecture. Put the *idea* in the subject position and let the reader relax (30:37–31:22).
  - Koe is not writing an advice column. It reads as though he is leaving notes to himself ("you need to lock in") (29:43–30:37).
  - Korean treats "you" (dangshin) as a subject as rude and often uses passive forms to avoid naming the listener (31:40–32:35).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Underline every "you" in a section (32:35).
  2. For each, ask whether the sentence is really about the reader or about an idea.
  3. If it is about an idea, make the idea the subject.
  4. Use the passive to put the emphasised thing in focus.
  5. Keep only the "you"s that are truly about the reader.
- **Symptoms:** Pages dense with "you"; imperatives addressed to nobody in particular; a finger-wagging tone.
- **When it does not apply:** Advice columns, where the writer really is addressing a reader's situation (29:43). V19 (1:00:28) encourages personal pronouns used well.
- **Example (his, 31:22–31:40):** "You let your brain form grooves" becomes "The brain forms grooves over time," or, to emphasise the grooves, "Grooves are formed in the brain over time." Mine: "You need to stop checking email before 10" becomes "Email checked before 10 sets the agenda for the whole day."
- **Timestamp:** 27:28–32:35.

#### 3.16 Use qualifiers to soften claims, in balance
- **What it is:** Words like "might," "can," "often" and "in some cases" temper claims so you don't come across as arrogant or overconfident (32:35–33:25).
- **Why it works:** An unedited author persona can come across as cocky and put readers off.
- **When it does not apply:** He adds that he dislikes writers who are too hedgy and not assertive enough. It takes balance, and that is part of what an editor is for (33:25).
- **Example:** "Meditation cures anxiety" becomes "For many people, daily meditation reduces anxiety within weeks." Not "It could perhaps be argued that meditation might sometimes help."
- **Timestamp:** 32:35–33:25.

#### 3.17 Make the idea the star of each sentence; watch dummy subjects
- **What it is:** Be deliberate about what occupies the subject position. It should usually be the topic or idea, not "you," and not dummy subjects such as "there is" or "it is" (33:25–34:19).
- **Why it works:** The subject position has a psychological effect. Used carelessly, it makes prose hard or uncomfortable to follow.
- **When it does not apply:** Dummy subjects are "not always wrong" (34:19).
- **Example:** "There are many factors that contribute to burnout" becomes "Three factors drive burnout."
- **Timestamp:** 33:25–34:19.

### 4. His stances in V18
- **Argues against:**
  - Bringing social-media conventions into a book (1:47).
  - Vague, noun-phrase chapter titles and inside references (3:24).
  - Writing to please yourself: side roads that interest the author but not the reader (14:32).
  - Editing by personal taste or arbitrary rules (14:32–15:26).
  - Planning a book the way you would explore by "pantsing" (17:41).
  - Habitual "you" as a subject (32:35).
- **Balances:** Hedging (33:25).
- **Warnings:**
  - Readers don't owe you attention (7:47).
  - Unperceived value is, in practice, no value (9:14).
  - Readers invent the links you leave out (19:21).
  - Self-publishing without an editor is risky. He quotes reviewers saying so and uses "that's what working with an editor is all about" (33:25).
- **Framing:** The roast "comes from a place of love." Books are worth writing as business assets (0:45).

### 5. People and books he names

## V19: The ultimate guide to writing WITHOUT AI (2025-08-08)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it solves

It is for writers, especially experts, online writers and academics, who feel outpaced by people publishing with AI. They wonder whether their skills are becoming useless (0:00), they write as if they were still in school, they stare at blank pages, and they fear publishing. He promises three things: how to use writing to *generate* ideas, how to break school habits so readers can see the value, and a two-draft system that "cuts writing time in half" (0:23).

### 2. Core argument

AI writers are starting to sound the same. The human advantage is original thinking, which comes from writing to think. His "expert writing system" runs through these phases, 27 steps in all:

1. **Foundation** (steps 1–7): unlearn school writing; replace "communication" with moving the reader; value before clarity; a specific reader; objections; community code words; the functions of sentences.
2. **Process** (steps 8–10): a messy writer's draft, then a complete rewrite for readers, because writing patterns interfere with reading patterns.
3. **Structure** (steps 11–14): problem-and-solution architecture; entering the conversation politely; research that creates tension.
4. **Organisation** (steps 15–17): consistent subjects; a naming system readers are trained in; subjects chosen by the reader's problems.
5. **Value** (steps 18–20): value words; value at the top of each paragraph; personal costs and benefits.
6. **Reader psychology** (steps 21–23): three standard objections; personal language; confidence without arrogance.
7. **Quality control** (steps 24–27): a value audit; the "so what?" test; removing friction; testing with real readers.

It ends with daily habits and signs of success. Throughout, clarity is useless without value, and value means solving a problem the reader already has.

### 3. Techniques

**Phase 1: Foundation (1:18)**

#### 3.1 Step 1: Stop writing as if you were in school
- **What it is:** Unlearn the patterns that exist to make grading easy (2:10–6:26).
- **Why it works:**
  - Teachers aren't real readers. They grade hundreds of essays and look for their own familiar patterns, so what they value differs from what real readers value (2:10–3:01).
  - Expert writing says, in effect: here is your problem, and here is my solution (3:01).
  - "Essay" comes from the French for "to try": a writer attempting a hard problem. There is no such thing as an essay about your favourite food or school uniforms (3:19).
- **Two school patterns to drop:**
  1. **Opening with background or context.** In school it proves to the teacher that you understand the context. Readers who came for a solution don't care how much you researched (4:05–4:55).
  2. **Narrating your thought process.** "At first I thought X, then I read Y, then I talked to my friend…", with the answer at the end. It drives readers crazy; they want the answer (4:55–5:41).
- **How to apply:**
  1. At the start of any essay, identify the question it answers.
  2. Say out loud: "I don't know. What evidence would I need to answer this?" This resists the preconceived answer (3:19–4:05).
  3. In the reader's version, cut the background opening and the narrative of how you got there.
  4. Aim to help people make better decisions, not to show what you know (5:41).
- **Symptoms:** First paragraphs of history or definitions. "In this essay I will…". "When I first started looking into this…". The answer on the last page.
- **When it does not apply:** He notes that writing to discover is *good*, but it belongs in the thinking draft (4:05; steps 8–10).
- **Example:** Before: "Onboarding has been studied since the 1970s. In this essay I will first review…" After: "New hires decide whether to stay within their first ten days, and most companies spend those days on paperwork."
- **Timestamp:** 2:10–6:26.

#### 3.2 Step 2: GPS directions, not a garden hose
- **What it is:** Two ways of thinking about what a text is for.
  - The "communication" model treats writing as a garden hose that carries fully formed ideas from your head to the reader's. It is self-centred: it assumes readers want to hear your thoughts (6:26–7:13).
  - Replace it with GPS directions, or a map: the text meets readers where they are (point A) and takes them to where you want them (point B) (7:44–8:34).
- **Why it works:** People want help getting from where they are to where they want to be (7:13). Even in writing about ideas, you are solving conceptual problems: readers hit a wall in understanding, and you help them replace one idea with a better one (7:13–7:44).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Stop asking "how do I explain my ideas?" Ask "how do I move readers from A to B?" (7:44–8:34)
  2. Treat the text as a journey you guide them through, not a capsule of your knowledge or a brain dump.
- **Symptoms:** A draft organised around your ideas ("my framework has five parts") instead of the reader's route ("you're here; here's the next step").
- **When it does not apply:** In the thinking draft, where you *should* develop your own ideas (8:34).
- **Example:** Garden hose: "My theory of pricing rests on three principles…" GPS: "If you price by adding a margin to cost, here is why you're undercharging your best customers, and what to change on Monday."
- **Timestamp:** 6:26–8:34.

#### 3.3 Step 3: Value before clarity
- **What it is:** Ask "is there value in this, and to whom?" before you work on clarity, concision or organisation (8:34–9:44).
- **Why it works:** "If your text is clear and useless, it's useless" (9:25). The same goes for organised and useless, and for persuasive and useless. Readers care only about value. If you don't help them decide, solve a problem or understand the world, they stop reading however well it is written (9:44).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Ask what readers will be able to do differently afterwards.
  2. For a practical problem, the answer is a change in behaviour. For a conceptual problem, it is a change in thinking, which sits upstream of behaviour (9:44–10:39).
  3. Remember that value is what helps readers reach *their* goals, not what you find interesting (10:39).
- **Symptoms:** Polishing sentences in a piece whose reader and payoff you can't name. Clients obsessing about being "clear and organised" (8:34).
- **Example:** A beautifully clear history of the Pomodoro timer, written for readers whose actual problem is having no uninterrupted hours in their calendar.
- **Timestamp:** 8:34–10:39.

#### 3.4 Step 4: Know exactly who you are writing for (start from your goal)
- **What it is:** Name a specific reader with a specific problem. Before that, name your goal, so you know who has to read the piece (10:39–14:33).
- **Why it works:**
  - "Business people" or "a general audience" is a target you can't see (10:39).
  - Fiction's "I never think about the audience" doesn't carry over. Generic readers don't exist; only real people with specific problems do. In his view nobody reads nonfiction for entertainment; they read it to solve problems (11:31–11:50).
  - Without a clear reader, an editor can only impose pet peeves. A good editor helps you picture the reader and asks whether your language connects with them (13:47–14:33).
  - Nonfiction is persuasion, whether toward actions or beliefs (14:33–15:13).
- **Changed his mind:** His early videos were vague and indiscriminate, for example "don't edit while you're writing." Coaching consultations taught him writers' specific problems (11:50–12:45).
- **How to apply:**
  1. What do you want to achieve with this piece or book?
  2. Who must read it for that to happen?
  3. What is their problem?
  4. Design the piece around that person (13:47).
- **Symptoms:** Audience written as a demographic. Advice that would fit anyone. An editor's comments that feel arbitrary.
- **Example:** "Content creators" becomes "newsletter writers with 500 to 2,000 subscribers whose open rates have slipped for three months."
- **Timestamp:** 10:39–15:13.

#### 3.5 Step 5: Beat readers to their objections ("good writing is like telepathy")
- **What it is:** Anticipate the reader's doubts and answer each one at the moment it arises (15:13–16:59).
- **Why it works:**
  - People dislike changing their ideas and dislike changing their behaviour even more, so expect resistance.
  - A one-sided argument falls flat. A reader whose objection goes unanswered feels overlooked and rejects the argument (15:13–16:04).
  - Without knowing their doubts, you prove what they already believe and ignore the real obstacles (16:04).
  - He cites Stephen King: good writing is telepathy (16:04).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Talk to readers, interview them, or read how they respond to similar ideas online.
  2. List their likely objections.
  3. Place each counterargument where the objection would occur to them (16:04).
- **Symptoms:** Long stretches proving the uncontroversial. No sentence beginning with anything like "you might object that…".
- **Example:** Claim: "Scrap annual performance reviews." Reader: "But we need a paper trail for firing." Answer it in the next sentence: what documentation replaces the review.
- **Timestamp:** 15:13–16:59 (developed in step 21).

#### 3.6 Step 6: Learn the community's secret language (the anthropologist exercise)
- **What it is:** Every professional or social community has coded words that signal "this matters." Find them and use them (16:59–18:46).
- **Why it works:**
  - Successful writers do this. Humanities academics say "problematise"; many people dislike the word, but it signals value in that community (17:16).
  - Classic value words ("however," "critical," "enables") are not just transitions; they flag the important part (18:21).
  - Bring language from another field and your ideas won't land (18:21).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Pick four or five prominent books or writers in the niche.
  2. Take standout articles from them.
  3. Read "like an anthropologist" for words that signal value and status.
  4. Keep a running document.
  5. Mirror those patterns (16:59–18:46).
- **Symptoms:** Your vocabulary comes from your home discipline, not the readers'. Readers skim past your key claims.
- **Example:** Writing for hospital administrators you might find "throughput," "patient safety" and "readmission." Writing for startup founders you might find "velocity," "wedge" and "retention."
- **Timestamp:** 16:59–18:46.

#### 3.7 Step 7: Focus on what words *do*, not what they say (every sentence is a tile in a path)
- **What it is:** Beneath the words is a layer of *functions*. The same sentence does different jobs depending on where it sits (18:46–20:33).
- **Why it works:**
  - AI enthusiasts seem to assume that words have fixed meanings. The result is pages of well-patterned words that answer no reader question and anticipate no reader expectation (19:38–20:33).
  - "Sales were down 15%" could be a problem, a piece of evidence or background, depending on what you need it to do (19:59).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Treat every sentence and paragraph as a tile on the path from A to B.
  2. For each, name its job: contrasting with the previous idea, introducing a problem, raising a reader's objection.
  3. If you can't name the job, delete it (20:33).
- **Symptoms:** Sentences that are "interesting" but do nothing. Paragraphs whose role you can only describe by topic.
- **Example:** "Churn rose to 8% last quarter" as the problem (first line of a pitch), as evidence (supporting a claim that the price rise backfired), or as background (one number among many in a history).
- **Timestamp:** 18:46–21:25.

**Phase 2: Process, the two-draft system (21:25)**

He says trying to think and write for readers at once is "complete torture." The first draft is for you, to generate ideas; the second is for readers (21:46).

#### 3.8 Step 8: Permission to write terrible first drafts (the writer's draft)
- **What it is:** A messy draft, just for you, where you work out what you think (21:46–25:11).
- **Why it works:**
  - Everyone has generated their own knowledge, so "I have nothing to say" isn't true (22:35).
  - The problem is that your ideas are too complex to sort out in your head. He shows a "cognitive map" image he attributes to *Gödel, Escher, Bach*: loosely connected concepts like a plate of spaghetti (22:35–23:29).
  - Only when you bring thoughts into language do they cohere (23:29–24:00).
  - Try to write perfectly while still thinking and you do both badly (24:00).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Follow your natural thought process.
  2. Ask questions and think out loud.
  3. Note contradictions and come back to them later.
  4. Ignore readers: you don't yet know what you think, so you can't worry about whether they will (24:34–25:11).
- **Symptoms that you are mixing the phases:** Worrying about readers before you have an answer. Stalling on sentence polish in a draft whose point is unknown.
- **Example:** A page that begins "Why do our best engineers leave after two years? I don't know. Maybe pay, but the exit interviews rarely mention pay…" and wanders until it arrives at a hunch.
- **Timestamp:** 21:46–25:11.

#### 3.9 Step 9: Writing patterns versus reading patterns (interference)
- **What it is:** The way you think while writing interferes with the way readers process text (25:11–27:22). He names two writer patterns:
  1. **Chronological organisation:** the order in which you discovered things (26:31–27:22).
  2. **Background up front,** when readers want "the meat and potatoes" first (27:22).
- **Why it works:**
  - In a thinking draft the answer appears at the bottom of the page, because that is where you found it (25:58).
  - When readers hit interference they slow down, get frustrated and leave. They won't say "you interfered with my reading pattern." They'll say "unclear" or "disorganised," meaning they couldn't use the text or find what they needed (26:31).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Learn to recognise writer patterns in your own draft.
  2. Keep them out of the reader version.
- **Symptoms:** Feedback like "unclear," "disorganised" or "hard to follow" on a piece whose ideas are strong. Sections in the order "first I found…, then…".
- **Example:** Writer pattern: "I began by surveying 40 teams; then I noticed…; eventually it became clear that…". Reader pattern: "Teams that skip the kickoff meeting ship later. Here's the evidence from 40 teams."
- **Timestamp:** 25:11–27:22.

#### 3.10 Step 10: Rewrite completely, in a new document
- **What it is:** Don't revise the thinking draft. Start a new document and write the reader's draft fresh (27:22–29:54).
- **Why it works:** The first draft is a record of your thought process. Copy and paste from it and you import writer patterns (28:15).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Open a blank document. Don't copy and paste.
  2. Ask: where is my reader now? What drives them to pick this up, and what about the title? What gets them through the first sentence, then the first paragraph? (28:51)
  3. Think of the text as a path or guided tour, not a storehouse of information.
  4. Account for where they are, what they believe and what they will object to. Be a guide, not an adversary (28:51–29:54).
- **Symptoms:** A "final" draft that is the first draft with paragraphs moved around and semicolons adjusted (28:15).
- **When it does not apply:** He is emphatic here. Later videos (per the project summary) soften this into salvaging points and rebuilding the structure.
- **Example:** The first draft ends with your insight in paragraph 14. The new document opens with that insight as the answer to the reader's problem.
- **Timestamp:** 27:22–29:54.

**Phase 3: Structure (29:54)**

He notes that perfectionists throw in everything to be exhaustive, which overwhelms readers who came for the answer to one question (29:54–30:43). The "problem–solution framework" takes the guesswork out of structure, down to paragraph length and section breaks (30:43).

#### 3.11 Step 11: Start with what's broken
- **What it is:** Open with the problem, not background (30:43–33:26).
- **Why it works:**
  - Opening with background forces the reader's first question: why should I care? That is the question readers are trying to answer as they start (32:26).
  - He compares it to recipe blogs that make you scroll through the grandmother's life story to reach the recipe. A lot of Substack writing feels like that; not for ad revenue, but because writers think readers need background (31:36–32:26).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Put the broken thing in the first sentences.
  2. Use problem language ("despite," "however," "but"), plus your community's own problem words.
  3. Make clear the text addresses the problem the reader already has (32:26–33:26).
- **Symptoms:** First paragraph could be cut with nothing lost. No "but" or "however" in the opening.
- **Example:** Before: "Performance reviews originated in the military in the early twentieth century…" After: "Your annual review tells people what they did wrong eleven months too late to fix it."
- **Timestamp:** 30:43–33:26.

#### 3.12 Step 12: Organise everything around problems and subproblems
- **What it is:** Structure the whole piece, not just the introduction, around the main problem and its subproblems. The subproblems become paragraphs and sections (33:26–35:19).
- **Why it works:** Without problem–solution structure, readers can't see why your ideas matter (33:26). Problems nest. A business not making money can't get clients; that breaks down into where leads come from, how calls get booked, and so on (33:49).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Ask: what problem have I solved?
  2. Where is the reader now?
  3. Which subproblems must they pass through to accept my final solution, my thesis? (34:34)
  4. Lay those out as an outline and assign each idea from your thinking draft as the solution to one subproblem (34:54–35:19).
- **His own caveat:** This structure is "a bit of a conceit." Real writing is expressive and full of discovery, and the problem frame is imposed in the reader draft (34:34–34:54).
- **Symptoms:** Sections organised by topic ("History," "Theory," "Applications") rather than by the reader's obstacles.
- **Example:** Problem: "Nobody reads our internal docs." Subproblems as sections: "People can't find them"; "They're out of date when found"; "They answer the writer's question, not the reader's."
- **Timestamp:** 33:26–35:19.

#### 3.13 Step 13: Disagree without making enemies (concession first)
- **What it is:** Valuable ideas overturn other people's ideas, so learn to disagree politely. Acknowledge the value of the existing thinking before you knock it down (35:19–40:04).
- **Why it works:**
  - If you aren't disagreeing with anyone, you are "off in a corner having a conversation with yourself" (35:19).
  - Think of joining a conversation. You pick up where the last speaker left off, credit what others said, and then add what they overlooked. You don't say "you're stupid and wrong" (36:00–36:50).
  - His example is a North Korea scholar who lives in his city (the book is *The Cleanest Race*; the author, B. R. Myers, is my identification). The book "comes out swinging" at everyone. It is a great read, but the tone probably cost him with his own academic community, whatever the quality of his ideas (37:13–38:53).
  - To use writing socially, to advance a career, build relationships or win peers' acceptance, soften your claims and show deference even while you disagree (38:19–38:53).
- **How to apply (the problem sequence he sketches):**
  1. State the status quo or consensus.
  2. Concede: what is right about it, and what has it helped its believers do? Tip your hat before you "come off the top ropes."
  3. Introduce the destabilising condition that shows the problem. This is where the writing gains its value (39:42–40:04).
- **Symptoms:** An opening that dismisses the field. A tone that reads as combative to the people you need.
- **When it does not apply:** "You don't always have to do this," he says (38:53). He also admits he can be "a little bit flip on the internet" (35:19).
- **Example:** "Checklists made surgery measurably safer, and that success is why every team now wants one. But a checklist only guards the steps someone thought to write down."
- **Timestamp:** 35:19–40:04.

#### 3.14 Step 14: Use research to build problems, not to show you have read
- **What it is:** Bring in sources to create tension and contradiction, not to display reading (40:04–42:56).
- **Why it works:**
  - "Smith says, Jones argues" citations show you've read something but give the reader nothing to do. Readers don't care what books you've read (41:02–41:54).
  - Research done with AI is confirmation bias. Ask for evidence of a flat earth and you'll get some (40:04).
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each quote or citation, ask what you expect the reader to do with it. What is its function, and how does it move the reader from A to B? (41:54–42:06)
  2. Place sources so they conflict or complicate: "Smith's findings conflict with Jones's conclusion, which creates uncertainty" (42:06).
  3. Make the situation more complex and more problematic, not just better informed (42:06–42:56).
- **Symptoms:** "As X writes…" followed by a quote that nothing follows up. Substack posts that say "I read an interesting book" (41:02).
- **Example:** Before: "Author A says open offices boost collaboration." After: "Author A found that open offices increased chance conversations; a later study of the same kind of firm found face-to-face interaction fell by two-thirds. Which should a manager planning a move believe?"
- **Timestamp:** 40:04–42:56.

**Phase 4: Organisation, or flow (42:56)**

He says 80% of clarity comes from organising information within sentences and paragraphs, and that this produces "flow" (42:56).

#### 3.15 Step 15: Keep subjects consistent
- **What it is:** Choose two or three main subjects for a paragraph or section and keep them in the grammatical subject position. Don't vary them for variety's sake (43:50–45:45).
- **Why it works:**
  - The belief that you should avoid repetition is a myth. A paragraph where "William" does one thing after another is, he says, "fantastic": smooth to read (43:50–44:22).
  - The stage analogy again: only two or three characters can be on stage at once. Every sentence is an action; a new subject each time is a new character acting each time, and the audience darts back and forth wondering what the scene is about (44:22–45:45).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Keep the "camera" on one subject until you deliberately move focus to another key character (45:45).
- **Symptoms:** Each sentence in a paragraph has a different subject. Synonyms used for "variety."
- **Example:** Varied: "The committee met in March. A decision was reached by members. Budget constraints shaped the outcome. Staff were informed in April." Consistent: "The committee met in March. It decided to cut the pilot. It cited budget limits. It told staff in April."
- **Timestamp:** 43:50–45:45.

#### 3.16 Step 16: Train readers in your naming system
- **What it is:** When you need variety, use several names for one entity. Establish them early so readers know they refer to the same thing (45:45–47:22).
- **Why it works:** Introduce new names mid-text ("the English professor," "the YouTuber") and readers can't tell whether you mean the same person or a new one, which wastes their mental energy (46:28–47:22).
- **How to apply:**
  1. At the first appearance, show the equivalence. His example: make clear that "customer retention software" and "these platforms" mean the same thing.
  2. Keep that naming system consistent afterwards (47:22).
- **Symptoms:** A reader asking "wait, who is 'the vendor'?" New labels appearing halfway through.
- **Example:** "The Board of Review (the board, or simply the reviewers) meets quarterly…" Afterwards, use only those three names.
- **Timestamp:** 45:45–47:22.

#### 3.17 Step 17: Choose subjects by the reader's problems *(implicit step, 47:22–49:20)*
- **What it is:** Decide which "characters" go in the subject positions according to the problem–solution structure.
- **Why it works:** Writers who don't think about subjects end up with random ones. Readers follow problem-solving logic much more easily; random characters on stage make them lose where you're going (48:19).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Ask: what's the next problem my reader needs solved?
  2. Order the sections in the order readers will meet those problems.
  3. Use transitions to show the logic between moves.
  4. Make the subjects track the problems and solutions as they appear (48:19–49:20).
- **Symptoms:** Subjects that belong to your research notes ("the 2019 survey," "Table 3") rather than to the reader's problem ("new managers," "their first review").
- **Example:** In a section on a new manager's first month, the subjects run "New managers… their first one-to-ones… those meetings… the manager…", not "Research… the literature… Table 2… it…".
- **Timestamp:** 47:22–49:20.

**Phase 5: Value (49:20)**

#### 3.18 Step 18: Master the words that signal importance (three columns)
- **What it is:** Build a list of value words in three columns:
  - **Opportunity:** "enables," "becomes."
  - **Cost:** "prevents," "disrupts."
  - **Urgency:** "critical," "crucial."
  - Also the general signals ("however," "but," "the problem is") and your community's own words (50:16–51:23).
- **Why it works:** Without them, writing feels generic. He singles out Substack, where writers pursue their interests without turning them into something readers see as valuable (50:39).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Examine four or five prominent pieces from writers who have the audience you want.
  2. Harvest their opportunity, cost and urgency words.
  3. Use them deliberately (51:23).
- **Symptoms:** Paragraphs with no "but," no stakes words, and no sense of what is at risk.
- **Example:** A list for school principals might hold opportunity words ("unlocks," "frees up"), cost words ("erodes," "burns out") and urgency words ("before enrolment," "this term").
- **Timestamp:** 49:20–51:23.

#### 3.19 Step 19: Make the value obvious at the top of every paragraph (circle the value words)
- **What it is:** State each paragraph's and section's value proposition at its start, where readers look for it (51:23–54:13).
- **Why it works:**
  - "None of this is about rules." It is about reading patterns: readers look in particular places for particular information (51:23–52:32).
  - Readers expect the action in the verb and time or place in adverbial phrases. At the paragraph level they look for value at the start, not the middle (52:32–52:56).
  - It is a social contract, not a copywriting trick: readers trade attention for value, so you reassure them up front and then deliver (52:56–53:46).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Put the value at the top of each unit.
  2. As an advanced, storyteller's move, put it at the end. Never bury it in the middle (53:46–54:13).
  3. **Diagnostic:** circle every value word in the draft. You want a dense cluster at the beginning and at least one in the first sentence of every paragraph. A long stretch with nothing to circle marks a problem (54:13).
- **Symptoms:** Paragraphs that open with a date, a name or a transition, and put their stakes in the middle.
- **When it does not apply:** When the point comes at the end as a deliberate storytelling choice (53:46).
- **Example:** Before: "In 2021 our team moved to a new ticketing tool, and after some adjustment we found response times fell." After: "A new ticketing tool cut our response times by a third, but only after we changed one habit."
- **Timestamp:** 51:23–54:13.

#### 3.20 Step 20: Make costs and benefits personal
- **What it is:** Show the consequence of *not* solving the problem, for this reader or for a cause they care about (54:13–57:08).
- **Why it works:**
  - "This creates some inconsistencies" is academic and abstract. "This prevents you from retaining your best customers" is urgent: food off the reader's table (55:02–55:31).
  - The same problem carries different stakes for different audiences. A good academic presents one argument to colleagues in conceptual terms and to people at the local library in terms of why it matters to them (55:31–56:18).
- **How to apply:**
  1. For each problem, write the cost ("this limits your ability to…") or the benefit of the solution ("this enables you to…").
  2. Phrase it for this reader group (56:18–57:08).
- **Symptoms:** Problems described only as inconsistencies, gaps or tensions, with no one affected.
- **Example:** Before: "The data pipeline has latency issues." After: "Your Monday forecast is built on Friday's numbers, so the board sees last week's business."
- **Timestamp:** 54:13–57:08.

**Phase 6: Reader psychology (57:08)**

#### 3.21 Step 21: Answer the three standard objections
- **What it is:** For every major claim, anticipate the top three objections, which almost always fall into three buckets (59:30):
  1. There are **other causes** of this problem than the ones you name.
  2. There are **counterexamples**; you left out evidence.
  3. I **define the terms** differently.
- **Why it works:**
  - Readers actively look for reasons to doubt you, especially when your idea demands effort or change (57:59).
  - Sales scripts handle every objection ("I need to ask my wife," "after payday") because the one you miss lets the buyer off the hook. Miss one objection and readers think you don't understand the situation (58:53–59:30).
- **How to apply:**
  1. List your major claims.
  2. For each, draft a response to all three buckets.
  3. Place each response before the reader raises the objection (59:30–1:00:28).
- **Symptoms:** Claims left bare. Readers replying "but what about…" with one of the three.
- **Example:** Claim: "Four-day weeks raise output." Other causes: maybe the trial firms were already high performers, so address the selection. Counterexamples: some firms dropped the trial, so explain why. Definitions: "output" means billable hours versus projects delivered, so define it.
- **Timestamp:** 57:59–1:00:28.

#### 3.22 Step 22: Make readers feel personally invested (personal language)
- **What it is:** Use personal pronouns ("you," "I"), humour and personal anecdotes to build a relationship with the reader (1:00:28–1:02:00).
- **Why it works:**
  - Abstract, impersonal prose sounds authoritative, but writing is social. Your language choices set your relationship with the reader (1:01:27).
  - Readers who like the author on the page are more easily persuaded (1:00:28–1:01:27).
- **When it does not apply:** Don't overdo it. He mentions a book "filled with the pronoun you," written like a journal to himself ("you need to lock in"), plainly Koe's from V18 (1:01:27–1:02:00).
- **Symptoms of too little:** A cold, bureaucratic voice and no author presence. Of too much: lecturing, finger-wagging.
- **Example:** "It has been observed that teams underestimate tasks" becomes "I've watched every team I've led underestimate tasks by about half, including the time I swore we wouldn't."
- **Timestamp:** 1:00:28–1:02:00.

#### 3.23 Step 23: Sound confident without sounding arrogant
- **What it is:** Match your confidence to the audience. Make direct statements, and always back them with evidence the reader accepts (1:02:00–1:05:09).
- **Why it works:**
  - Academics fill their writing with hedges ("perhaps," "seems," "could be"). That suits peer-review communities, which expect vigorous attack, but exported to other fields it makes you seem unsure, and readers wonder why they should listen (1:02:55–1:03:38).
  - Overshoot and you sound arrogant, and readers dismiss you out of hand (1:03:38–1:04:23).
- **How to apply:**
  1. In general nonfiction, write direct confidence statements.
  2. Make sure each idea has its three parts: the claim, evidence the reader accepts, and reasoning (the "warrant") linking them (1:04:23–1:05:09).
  3. Then "be confident, be strident" (1:05:09).
- **Symptoms:** Stacked hedges; or bold claims with no evidence.
- **When it does not apply:** Peer-reviewed academic writing expects more hedging (1:02:55).
- **Example:** Over-hedged: "It could perhaps be suggested that onboarding may affect retention." Arrogant: "Onboarding is the only thing that matters for retention." Calibrated and backed: "Onboarding drives first-year retention: in our three offices, new hires with a named buddy stayed at twice the rate."
- **Timestamp:** 1:02:00–1:05:09.

**Phase 7: Quality control (1:05:09)**

He opens with the **paradox of the fear of publishing.** Writers fear both that nobody will read them and that everybody will read and judge them, and both can't be true. A quality-control routine answers the second fear (1:05:09–1:05:58).

#### 3.24 Step 24: Audit everything for value
- **What it is:** Keep or cut each element according to the value it gives the reader, not because it is true, interesting, or something you worked hard on (1:05:58–1:07:30).
- **Why it works:** Weak content dilutes your best ideas and wastes the reader's attention, "the most precious resource you have" (1:07:30).
- **How to apply:**
  1. For every paragraph, quote and section, ask: why should my reader care? What problem does this solve?
  2. If you can't name the value, rewrite or delete (1:07:30).
- **Symptoms:** Your defence of a passage is "but it's true," "it's interesting," or "I spent a week on that."
- **Example:** A fascinating paragraph on the etymology of "deadline" in a guide to project scoping. It's true and interesting, and it goes.
- **Timestamp:** 1:05:58–1:07:30.

#### 3.25 Step 25: The "so what?" test
- **What it is:** Every major point should let readers easily answer: what does this do for me, what does it mean for me, why should I care? (1:07:30–1:08:47)
- **Why it works:** When you don't spell out the link to the reader, they do extra work on top of reading and interpreting (1:08:15).
- **How to apply:**
  1. After each major point, ask "so what?" in the reader's voice.
  2. Write the answer into the text.
  3. If the passage explains your thinking process instead of solving a problem, rewrite it (1:08:47).
- **Symptoms:** Points that end on a fact. The reader's likely response is "okay… and?"
- **Example:** "Most customers churn in month three." So what? Add: "which means your onboarding emails stop exactly when customers need them most."
- **Timestamp:** 1:07:30–1:08:47.

#### 3.26 Step 26: Remove everything that makes reading hard
- **What it is:** Strip out the writer-focused patterns that make decoding hard: background, chronological organisation, and explanations of your thought process. Replace them with problem architecture (1:08:47–1:11:07).
- **Why it works:** He recalls David Foster Wallace telling Bryan Garner, in an interview, that good writing comes down to the reader's cognitive experience. The easier the decoding, the "better" the writer (1:08:47–1:09:25). Interference slows readers, frustrates them, and attaches bad feelings to you and your ideas (1:10:09).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Hunt for the three writer patterns.
  2. Restructure each passage around the problem, its causes and its solution as the reader meets them (1:10:09–1:11:07).
- **Symptoms:** See step 9.
- **Timestamp:** 1:08:47–1:11:07.

#### 3.27 Step 27: Test with real people
- **What it is:** Watch real readers try to use your text. That can mean an editor or beta readers, but ideally your own audience (1:11:07–1:13:02).
- **Why it works:**
  - Until you see someone using the text, you are guessing (1:11:07).
  - Editors and writer friends are not who you are writing for. Your readers' reactions reveal their reading process (1:12:01–1:13:02).
  - Paul Millard's curiosity conversations, unstructured one-to-one Zoom calls with Substack readers, are his model (1:11:07–1:12:01).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Share early, messy work with even a small audience.
  2. Hold conversations with readers.
  3. Watch where they struggle.
- **Symptoms that you skipped it:** You can't predict readers' questions or objections, and your picture of the reader is a guess.
- **Timestamp:** 1:11:07–1:13:02.

#### 3.28 Bonus: daily habits and signs of success (1:13:02–1:14:43)
- **Daily habits:**
  - Before writing, ask what specific value you are creating.
  - Begin with a reader problem, never background.
  - Write first drafts to think, and ignore readers.
  - Revise by rewriting completely.
  - Before publishing, circle the value words. Big gaps between them mean a problem.
- **Signs of success:**
  - Readers act on your writing.
  - You can predict objections before readers think of them.
  - People who disagree with you still find it valuable.
  - You write faster because you know your readers' reading patterns and codes.
  - People share and reference the work because it helps them.
- **Warning:** Don't try to apply all 27 steps at once; you will freeze (1:14:43).

  - AI for writing: writers using it "all sound the same" (0:23).

## V20: Writing is a two-player game (here's how to win) (2025-08-14)

### 1. Who it is for, and what problem it solves

It is for experts, "busy experts writing books that matter" (0:00), who are frozen by perfectionism at the blank page, and for anyone whose writing habits were formed by school assignments.

### 2. Core argument

Writing is a two-player game, like chess, tennis or Catan: you move, the reader responds, you adjust (0:47).

The game has a prior phase. Experts get stuck because they jump straight to imagining a demanding reader, which he calls the **expert's curse**. They should first write to think, alone, messily, as in a laboratory. When they adapt their ideas for readers, they should organise by **functions**, the moves a genre needs to reach a goal shared by writer and reader, not by the **templates** school taught (thesis first, topic sentences). School trained writers to *demonstrate knowledge*; real readers want *value*.

Nonfiction's job is persuasion, so plan the piece as a decision tree of the reader's likely responses.

### 3. Techniques

#### 3.1 The two-player game *(his framing)*
- **What it is:** When writing for readers, think about what move the reader will make next, and write in response to it (0:47).
- **Why it works:** In any game you can't make the same moves every time and win; you have to watch the other player (0:00).
- **How to apply:**
  1. After each paragraph of the reader draft, write the reader's likely response in brackets.
  2. Make your next paragraph answer it.
  3. Delete the brackets.
- **Symptoms:** The draft reads as a monologue, and the reader's likely "yes, but…" is never answered.
- **When it does not apply:** The first, thinking phase, which is "just for you" (8:25).
- **Example:** "Most teams should drop daily stand-ups. [Reader: but how will I know what's blocked?] A blocked-work channel surfaces problems the moment they happen, not the next morning."
- **Timestamp:** 0:00–0:47, 22:49–23:28.

#### 3.2 The expert's curse *(his term)* and the Dunning–Kruger effect of writing
- **What it is:** A diagnosis. The more you know, the harder writing gets. You feel the crushing weight of everything you could say, so every sentence seems inadequate and you write, delete and restart (1:05–2:09).
  - Beginners are confident because they don't know what they don't know. His example: someone back from a three-day meditation retreat starts a blog, while a neuroscientist who has published on meditation can't get out a first sentence (2:53–3:40).
  - Experts know their own gaps (3:40–4:04).
- **Why it matters (his reframe):** The pressure is a *good sign*. It means you are imagining a reader you respect and want to give something of value (2:09–2:53). The block comes from being in the reader phase too early (5:22).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Recognise the feeling as evidence of reader awareness, not of incompetence.
  2. Then switch explicitly to phase one, writing for yourself (see 3.3 to 3.5).
- **Symptoms:** A cycle of starting and deleting. Sentences judged inadequate before the paragraph exists. Ideas that flow in conversation but not on the page (1:05).
- **Timestamp:** 1:05–4:04.

#### 3.3 Writing as a laboratory: writing to find out what you think
- **What it is:** Writing is a method for working out what you think, not just a way to record what you know. He calls using writing to generate value "expert writing" (4:04–4:47).
- **Why it works:**
  - Valuable writing means thinking thoughts that haven't been thought before, rather than "pastiche AI writing" that shuffles other people's ideas into new patterns (4:47).
  - New knowledge is too complex to sort out in your head, so the page becomes a laboratory where you hold pieces, smash them together and test them (5:06–5:22).
  - It is close to talking. You discover ideas while thinking out loud, which is why they come out when you explain something to a friend (1:05, 5:22).
- **How to apply:** Keep showing up and keep chiselling. Work on a piece here, go down a tangent there; ideas emerge (5:22–6:12).
- **Symptoms that you're doing the opposite:** Trying to write only what you already know, in finished form.
- **Timestamp:** 4:04–6:12.

#### 3.4 A timed freewrite anchored to a question *(his variation on morning pages)*
- **What it is:** A daily freewrite of 10 to 20 minutes (a "brain dump") with the question you want to answer written at the top of the page (6:12–7:40).
- **Why it works:** The morning pages in *The Artist's Way* let anything come up. That is fine, but readers won't care about your journal or dream log. Anchoring the session to a question lets anything come to mind while keeping your "centre of gravity" on a problem readers will value (7:00–7:40).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write the question your essay, chapter or book must answer at the top of the page.
  2. Set a 20-minute timer.
  3. Write without stopping.
  4. When you drift, glance back at the question.
  5. Do this every day (6:12–7:40).
- **Symptoms that you need it:** Waiting to know where you're going before you start.
- **Example:** At the top: "Why do our clients stop replying after the second proposal?" Then twenty minutes of guesses, memories, half-arguments.
- **Timestamp:** 6:12–7:40.

#### 3.5 Talk it out
- **What it is:** Use voice-to-text transcription to empty your thoughts, then work from the transcript as raw material (7:40).
- **Why it works:** Talking gets things flowing; "there's something special about your voice" (5:22).
- **His position on AI here:** He says that if you want to use AI to clean up the transcript, you can (7:40). This is milder than V19 and than later videos, which (per the project summary) limit AI to transcribing or bulleting a private voice note.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Record yourself explaining the idea as if to a friend.
  2. Transcribe it.
  3. Treat the transcript as material for the writer's draft, not as the reader draft.
- **Timestamp:** 7:40.

#### 3.6 Embrace the mess: the writer's draft goes in the drawer
- **What it is:** The first phase has nothing to do with what readers will see. Don't edit the first draft. It goes "in the bottom of a drawer" or stays on your hard drive. When it starts to make sense to you, set it aside and start a new reader's draft (7:40–9:12).
- **Why it works:**
  - Being strict about the difference between the writer's draft and the reader's draft is liberating (8:25).
  - He compares it to painters and potters, who arrive at the studio without a fixed plan, move colour around and let the image emerge. Analytical nonfiction writers need more of that artistic process (9:12–9:33).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Label the file "writer's draft."
  2. Forbid yourself to edit it.
  3. Stop when it makes sense to you.
  4. Open a new file for the reader.
- **Symptoms:** Line-editing a draft whose point you don't yet know. Feeling you can't write freely because someone might see it.
- **Timestamp:** 7:40–9:33.

#### 3.7 The school writing trap *(his term)*: knowledge versus value
- **What it is:** School writing teaches you to demonstrate understanding to a paid reader, the teacher, who wants to know whether you did the work. You learn to supply the teacher's reader functions in the places the teacher looks (9:33–11:11). He apologises on behalf of English professors (11:11).
- **Why it matters:**
  - Real readers aren't paid. They are busy, sceptical and quick to leave, and TikTok is competing for them (11:11–12:27).
  - His example is from that same day: he searched for how to fix a leaking kitchen sink and got intros, backstory and too much information (12:27–13:19).
  - The difference is between knowledge and value. Independent writers aren't literally showing off, but when they ignore readers' problems, "all you're offering is knowledge," and nothing is positioned so the reader sees value (13:19–14:41).
  - Beneath the sentences is a layer of *functions*. Expert writing respects readers' time and solves their problems (14:41–15:02).
  - Trust now comes from value, not credentials: "nobody really cares what university you went to" (15:02).
- **How to apply:** Reframe "I'm not interesting, I have nothing new" as "I'm not yet *mobilising* my ideas as solutions" (15:41). Then use 3.8.
- **Symptoms:** Drafts that tour everything you know. Intros full of backstory. Readers who can't find the one thing they came for.
- **Example:** A post on "The history and principles of zero-based budgeting" (knowledge) versus "Find the £400 a month you didn't know you were losing" (value).
- **Timestamp:** 9:33–15:41.

#### 3.8 The benefit-or-cost positioning question
- **What it is:** For any idea, ask: what benefit will my reader get from this, or what cost will it help them avoid? (15:41)
- **Why it works:** It turns knowledge into value by tying it to the reader's gain or loss. He says common positioning patterns exist but declines to give templates, because template writing is where writers go wrong (15:41–16:36).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write the idea.
  2. Underneath, write "Benefit:" or "Cost avoided:" and fill it in for this reader.
  3. Lead with that line.
- **Example:** Idea: "Batching small tasks reduces context switching." Benefit: "You get back the ninety-minute block you keep losing to ten-minute jobs."
- **Timestamp:** 15:41–16:36.

#### 3.9 The template trap *(his term)*
- **What it is:** Rules such as "always start with a thesis" and "every paragraph needs a topic sentence" were designed for grading, not for real readers (16:36).
- **Why it matters:** They served a teacher with a grading sheet. Your new reader grades by a different, self-centred standard: their own problems and concerns. Match *that*, not the second-grade teacher he nicknames "Mrs. Thistlebottom" (16:36–17:08). Templates make writing inflexible and robotic (21:22).
- **How to apply:** Replace "which template is this?" with "what function does this reader need here?" (3.10). Keep a form only when it serves a reading function.
- **Symptoms:** A structure chosen before you know the reader. Five-paragraph-essay bones in professional writing. Paragraphs that open with topic sentences announcing nothing the reader needs.
- **When it does not apply / tension:** In V18 (21:12) and V19 (51:23) he recommends an index or value statement at the top of each paragraph, which looks like a topic sentence. He reconciles it himself: "none of this is about rules," only about where readers look (V19 51:23). The form is fine when it serves the reader's function; the *rule* is what he rejects.
- **Timestamp:** 16:36–17:08, 20:58–21:22, 24:25.

#### 3.10 Genre as a sequence of functions: the Starbucks test *(my label for his example)*
- **What it is:** A genre is a predictable sequence of *functions* that help two people cooperate toward a shared goal. At Starbucks the functions are greeting, order, confirmation of size, for here or to go, and payment. The surface wording varies from café to café ("tall" versus "large"), but the functions stay the same (17:08–18:21).
- **Why it works:**
  - If the barista asks "What's your favourite color?" the question is grammatically flawless, "an A+ from ChatGPT," yet it is weird and disappointing, because it serves no function in the genre (19:13–20:35).
  - The goal at Starbucks is a purchase. An essay's goal is better understanding, or solving a practical problem through changed behaviour (19:13–20:12).
  - Readers care about *relevance*: how the language helps them reach their goal (20:58).
  - Template-following writing is like the scene in *The Truman Show* where a character breaks off a conversation to advertise a cocoa drink. It looks like language but talks to nobody (21:22–21:38).
  - Effective writing is coordinated like a dance: both parties move toward a shared goal (21:38).
- **How to apply:**
  1. State the goal the reader and writer share.
  2. List the functions the genre needs to reach it.
  3. Tag each paragraph with its function.
  4. Anything with no function, a "favourite colour" paragraph, gets cut, however well written.
- **Symptoms:** Polished paragraphs that make the reader wonder why they are there. Asides that break the sequence.
- **Example:** In a memo recommending a supplier, a paragraph about the founder's childhood is a "favourite colour" question. The genre needs: the decision required, the options, the recommendation, the risks, the next step.
- **Timestamp:** 17:08–21:38.

#### 3.11 Plan the reader's journey as a decision tree
- **What it is:**
  1. Decide what you are trying to do. In nonfiction that is almost always persuading, and he says those writing nonfiction to entertain can "good luck with that" (21:38–22:49).
  2. Ask who the reader is and where they are now.
  3. Begin with a "greeting function" that brings them into your world.
  4. Plan the structure as a decision tree of their expected responses: I'll say this, they'll think that, they'll have this question so I answer it now, they won't agree with this part (22:49–23:28).
- **Why it works:** The goal is to bring readers to the understanding you reached through freewriting, but on *their* terms: meet them where they are, answer their questions, handle their objections, and walk together until you both reach the point (23:28–24:03).
- **How to apply: answer these questions about the reader before structuring (24:03–24:25):**
  - Do they have reservations?
  - Do they already agree with the premise, or do I need to warm them up?
  - Will they resist? Is this idea dear to them?
  - Or are they looking for a critique, with a vague sense the idea is wrong and wanting a model argument?
  - Do I need counterarguments, and which ones?
  - Then drop the templates: "templates are for dummies" (24:25). You know your stuff and your reader, so write to that person. Writing is language, and language is social.
- **Symptoms:** A structure that follows your notes rather than the reader's likely questions. An argument that never meets resistance because it never imagines any.
- **When it does not apply:** The thinking phase.
- **Example:** Reader: a finance director sceptical of a four-day week. The tree: open with a cost she already worries about (burnout-driven turnover). She'll ask about client coverage, so answer that next. She'll doubt the productivity data, so use comparable firms. She'll fear the precedent, so propose a reversible three-month trial.
- **Timestamp:** 21:38–24:58.

  - Templates and formulas as rules (16:36, 24:25).
  - School writing habits. He apologises on behalf of English professors (11:11).
  - Credentials as the basis of trust (15:02).
  - Editing the first draft (8:25).
  - AI writing, which "ignores readers altogether" (24:58). He still allows AI clean-up of your own voice transcripts (7:40).

## Across these five videos

### How the ideas connect

**1. Two groups of videos, one engine.**
- V16 to V18 are about *books*. V16 covers positioning (Vow, Victim, Villain). V17 covers choosing a type by goal (six models). V18 is a post-mortem of a book that got type, promise, structure and voice wrong.
- V19 and V20 generalise to *any* nonfiction piece, framed against AI and against school.
- The same engine runs through all five. Value means solving a problem the reader already has; the reader must be able to perceive it; and everything is filtered by one question: does this move this reader from where they are to where I want them?

**2. The purpose chain appears three times.**
- V18 (10:47–13:56) sets it out: the writer's goal → who must read → their pain → the table of contents as a bridge → filter.
- V19 (13:47) repeats it for any book or piece.
- V17 is the same chain turned into a menu: the goal selects the type, and the type selects the reader, shape and stance.
- V16's Victim is the "who must read" link seen from the marketing side.

**3. The promise, at every scale.**
- The book subtitle is the Vow (V16 6:54).
- A chapter title is a promise (V18 7:12).
- The title of an essay promises a solution (V19 4:55).
- The opening of a paragraph states its value proposition (V19 53:46).
- The same social contract sits under all of these: readers trade attention for value (V18 7:12; V19 53:46; V20 11:11). A skill could treat "promise first, then deliver" as a single rule that scales from book to paragraph. That scaling is his idea, the "fractal" (V18 23:07).

**4. Practical versus conceptual problems** runs through V18 (4:21), V19 (7:44, 10:39) and V20 (19:13). The reader either changes a behaviour or swaps an old idea for a new one. This lets every idea-driven piece still promise a concrete change.

**5. The anatomy of an idea is consistent.** An idea is a claim, evidence, and an explanation (warrant) linking them: V17 (6:42) and V19 (1:04:23).
- Its corollaries: let claims emerge from evidence (V17 7:38).
- An idea must be arguable (V17 13:18; V19 35:19).
- Confidence must be backed by evidence the reader accepts (V19 1:05:09).

**6. The stage metaphor serves two lessons.**
- V18 (28:19) uses it to explain why "you" in the subject position feels like a lecture.
- V19 (44:22) uses it to explain why subjects should stay consistent.
- The shared principle: the subject position is a spotlight, a scene holds two or three characters, and the reader's attention follows whatever is in the subject position.

**7. The two-draft system.**
- It is seeded in V17: expert writing extends thinking and generates knowledge (4:07), and a writer can begin with questions (2:28).
- It is laid out fully in V19 (21:46–29:54): terrible first drafts, interference, a complete rewrite in a new document.
- It is dramatised in V20 (4:04–9:33): the expert's curse, the laboratory, the question-anchored freewrite, the drawer.
- V18 supplies the boundary. Pantsing is fine for exploring, but compiling a book needs structure and outlines (17:41, 23:07). The outline belongs to the reader draft, not the thinking draft.

**8. School is the common villain of his teaching.**
- V16: "be nuanced, be objective."
- V18: teachers are paid to read, so students never learn to earn attention (8:24).
- V19: background openers and narrating the thought process (4:05–5:41).
- V20: the school writing trap and the template trap.
- He also practises V16's Villain move on his own channel, naming AI "slop" as the enemy (V16 13:51; V19 0:23; V20 24:58).

**9. Test with real readers.**
- V16's pitch-to-a-stranger loop (6:17).
- V17's and V19's curiosity conversations (V17 34:36; V19 1:11:07).
- V18's mining of sympathetic reviews (1:47).
- All four put real reactions ahead of the writer's own judgement.

### Developments, shifts and tensions

**1. Boldness versus modulation: the biggest tension in this set.**
- **Bold:**
  - V16 says authors aren't in the business of nuance, and tells them to pick a fight (2:43, 12:21).
  - V17 says to make your boldest case and let readers qualify it (12:13), and that Gift books speak with calm authority and never say "it depends" (35:19).
- **Moderate:**
  - V18 recommends qualifiers ("might," "often") to avoid a preachy persona (32:35).
  - V19 recommends concession and deference toward the existing conversation if you want peers' acceptance (38:19–40:04), and "confident but evidence-backed" (1:04:23).
- **How he appears to reconcile them:** Boldness belongs to the *stance and positioning* (the title, the thesis, the villain as a system). Courtesy belongs to *how you enter the conversation*. The strength of claims is set by the community and the goal: academic peers expect hedging, general readers take it as weakness (V19 1:02:55). Arrogance is a matter of *persona*, not of how bold the thesis is (V18 26:30). A skill should calibrate by genre and audience rather than apply one rule.

**2. "You": avoid it or use it?**
- V18 says to take "you" out of the subject position (32:35).
- V19 says don't be afraid of personal pronouns (1:00:28), and in the same passage cites Koe's overuse of "you" as the failure case (1:01:27).
- The resolution: personal presence and warmth are good. Habitually making the reader the grammatical actor in sentences that are really about ideas reads as lecturing.

**3. Templates versus his own fixed forms.**
- V20 calls templates "for dummies" and school rules like topic sentences a trap (16:36, 24:25).
- Yet V18 prescribes an index sentence first, transitions and outlines (21:12–23:07), and V19 prescribes the value statement at the top of each paragraph (53:46).
- He resolves it in V19 (51:23): these are *reading patterns*, where readers look, not rules. A form is justified only by the function it serves. V20's Starbucks model is the conceptual basis: functions are stable, surface forms vary.

**4. Narrow versus broad audience.**
- V16's Victim says to narrow unless you're after a mass-market bestseller (8:36).
- V17's Booking Agent deliberately goes broad (25:25), while the Lead Getter goes narrow (18:20).
- Breadth is set by the book's goal, not by any fixed principle.

**5. The strength of the rewrite rule.** V19 insists on a *complete* rewrite in a new document with no copy and paste (28:15). V20 is similar (8:25). ### What a writing skill should take from these five videos
1. Before drafting a reader version, ask for:
   - the goal;
   - the one reader and their pain in their own words;
   - the promise (a changed behaviour or a replaced idea);
   - the villain or status quo it pushes against.
2. For book projects, classify the book by goal into his six types. Apply that type's reader, length and stance, and warn about mismatches, such as an ideas book sold as a practical guide.
3. When revising:
   - rewrite chapter and section titles as promises;
   - move each unit's point or value to its opening;
   - run the uneven U;
   - check that every sentence has a nameable function;
   - circle the value words;
   - run "so what?" on each point;
   - answer the three standard objections;
   - make costs personal;
   - keep subjects consistent and train readers in any naming variants;
   - replace habitual "you" subjects with the idea;
   - calibrate confidence to the community, backed by evidence.
4. Diagnose feedback such as "unclear," "disorganised" or "unfocused" as writer patterns (background first, chronological order, narrated thinking) or a purpose mismatch, not as a sentence-level problem.
5. Keep the thinking draft sacred and separate. Never polish it into the final.
