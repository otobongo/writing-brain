## V36: The 5 Writing Skills A.I. Will Never Replace (2026-04-01)

### 1. Who it is for, and the problem
Nonfiction writers who fear AI will make them redundant, and writers tempted to let AI draft and then disguise the result. The problem: what stays valuable about a human writer when fluent text costs nothing? He says at the start (V36 0:00-0:39) that this is not a list of surface tips like avoiding em-dashes or the word "delve". It is about the three-way relationship between writer, reader and text.

### 2. Core argument
AI is the average of every voice on the internet. It has no body, no life, nothing at stake, and it has been tuned towards the most probable answer. Five human abilities follow from having a body, a biography and a reputation, and he says they are what readers will value: (1) a voice that emerges from lived experience; (2) metaphors grounded in the body; (3) breaking rules on purpose; (4) writing under real stakes; (5) whimsy, meaning writing as open-ended play. The answer to AI is not to hide AI tells but to write in a way only you can. He calls this your "moat" (V36 24:24). Writing without AI is a different approach to writing, not a handicap.

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics, exercises

#### 3.1 Voice as an emergent property (framework)
- **What it is:** Voice is not your sentences, grammar or word choice. It emerges, the way an ant colony's structure or a market's behaviour does, when those elements meet a particular blend of experience, expertise and perspective. He describes voice as a complex adaptive system that can't be understood by breaking it into parts (V36 1:10-3:20).
- **Why it works:** You can list a writer's grammar and vocabulary and still not have captured their voice (2:31). Lived experience is what AI lacks. It can't be embarrassed in a school gym and carry that for years (4:04). AI blends thousands of distinct voices into something smooth and clean, and the result is slop (5:02). Readers come to connect with a person, so the connection is the thing only you can offer (5:55).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Don't try to build a voice through surface rules: banned-phrase lists, custom instructions to an AI, avoiding dashes.
  2. For each section, ask what could only have come from you: a specific experience, a professional scar, a perspective from your field.
  3. Bring that material in where it serves the point.
  4. Judge the draft by whether it shares something of you and reaches a real reader, not by whether it passes as human-written.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** Smooth, correct prose that anyone in the field could have signed. No specific experiences. Edits aimed at removing "AI tells" rather than at saying something.
- **When it does not apply:** He gives no exception. He rejects one tactic outright: instructing an AI to avoid certain phrases, which treats fooling the reader as the goal (5:02).
- **Illustration:** Before: "Leading a team through change requires resilience and adaptability." After: "The first time I had to announce a reorg, I rehearsed it in the car park for forty minutes and still opened with the wrong sentence."
- **Timestamp:** V36 1:10-5:55.

#### 3.2 Embodied metaphor, and the portal test
- **What it is:** A metaphor works when it takes the reader from something unfamiliar to something familiar, and the familiar thing changes or deepens how they understand the unfamiliar one. He credits the "portal" image to the poet Ocean Vuong (V36 9:00-9:21). The best metaphors come from bodily, lived experience. His examples are a mother and daughter as close as an orange to its navel (a simile, he admits), and New York garbage collectors calling maggots "disco rice" (6:48-7:50).
- **Why it works:** Drawing on *Metaphors We Live By*, he argues that metaphor is how brains make sense of the world, not decoration. Everyday phrases such as attacking an argument, or time running out, reveal the concepts that organise our thinking (7:50-9:00). Those concepts rest on bodily experience. AI has no body, so its metaphors have the right form but link images that feel a few degrees off (6:22-6:48). You and your readers share a body-based frame of reference, so the metaphors that come to you naturally speak their language (9:58).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Name the unfamiliar thing you need the reader to grasp.
  2. Look in your own physical and sensory experience, work life or trade slang for a familiar thing with the same shape.
  3. Test it: does the familiar thing tell the reader something new about the unfamiliar one? If it only decorates, cut it or find another.
  4. Test for strangeness: does the pairing sound like something a person who had held both things would say? If it sounds formally correct but alien, it fails.
  5. As a thinking tool, notice the hidden metaphors in your field's ordinary phrases. They show how your readers already frame the subject.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** Metaphors with the right grammar but no physical grip ("a tapestry of", "a symphony of", "navigating the landscape of"). Images that no one could picture. Comparisons the reader has to decode rather than feel.
- **When it does not apply:** He gives no limit here. (Inferred: it concerns metaphors you choose to use. It does not require adding metaphors.)
- **Illustration:** Before: "Onboarding is a rich tapestry of interconnected learning." After: "Onboarding a new engineer is like handing someone the keys to a house where every light switch is wired to a different room."
- **Timestamp:** V36 5:55-9:58.

#### 3.3 Learn the rules, then break them on purpose
- **What it is:** Great writers break rules to get a specific effect. His example is Cormac McCarthy ending *Suttree* with "Fly them", a strange verb where "flee" was expected (V36 9:58-11:15). Shakespeare follows the sonnet's fixed rules while straining against them (12:32-13:06).
- **Why it works:** The strangeness makes the reader stop and look again, and it stays in the ear. He remembered the line for six years (11:15). Straining against a form carries a human energy through the language (13:06). An AI assistant would flag the verb as an error and smooth it away. AI is the "teacher's pet" that always follows the rules (13:06-13:25).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Learn the convention first. He compares this to a chef learning technique or a musician learning scales before improvising (12:09).
  2. When you break a rule, name the effect you want: a double take, emphasis, a line that lingers.
  3. Protect deliberate breaks from "correction" by editors, grammar checkers and AI tools.
  4. Check that the break is rare enough to register.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** Prose that is fully correct and completely inert. Every sentence smoothed to the expected word. Or the opposite problem: breaks that come from not knowing the rule, which read as errors rather than effects.
- **When it does not apply:** Not before you know the rule. He expects writers to dislike this, as if he were a finger-wagging professor (12:09).
- **Illustration:** Before: "We collected data for three years and ended up with only one chart we could use." After: "Three years of data. One usable chart." (Fragments break the school rule on purpose, for emphasis.)
- **Timestamp:** V36 9:58-13:48.

#### 3.4 Write with stakes
- **What it is:** Real writing happens when people you respect, and want respect from, will read and judge your work under your name (V36 17:19). Three models:
  - **The précis seminar** he took as an undergraduate (13:48-16:10). Each week one student wrote a two-page paper that summarised the reading's argument in their own words and then argued against it, then presented it to peers. There were no summaries to fall back on.
  - **Jeff Bezos's memo:** no slides. The meeting starts in silence while everyone reads a memo that lays out the issue, traces the writer's thinking and grounds the proposal in facts (17:19-18:08). He says five pages; the practice is usually described as six.
  - **David Foster Wallace's class rule:** nothing could be submitted until it had been revised at least two or three times (18:08).
- **Why it works:** Accountability produces clarity and depth (18:08). AI has no stakes. Corrected, it simply agrees and carries on, with no embarrassment and no growth, like a summer intern who doesn't care what you think (18:08). When the stakes disappear, writing becomes a chore, and that is when people turn to AI (19:06).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Publish something under your name that you would not be embarrassed for others to read (19:06).
  2. Choose readers whose judgment you care about.
  3. For decisions at work, write a memo that states the issue, shows your reasoning and ties the proposal to evidence. Expect it to be read closely.
  4. Revise several times before you submit.
  5. To build understanding of hard material, write a précis: summarise the argument in your own words, then argue against it.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** Writing that feels like a chore. Reaching for AI to "get it done". Mediocrity with no cost. A private draft that has never faced a reader.
- **When it does not apply:** He doesn't give a case here. (Inferred from V38 and V36 3.5: stakes belong to the reader-facing work. Exploratory writing needs safety, not judgment.)
- **Illustration:** Private note: "Pricing is confusing; maybe simplify." Stakes version, a one-page memo to the leadership team: "Customers abandon checkout at the plan-comparison step. The data shows [x]. I propose three tiers instead of five because [reasoning]. Here is what would prove me wrong."
- **Timestamp:** V36 13:48-19:29.

#### 3.5 Whimsy: treat writing as a play space (his terms: "whimsy", "creative unpredictability")
- **What it is:** Writing approached as following a thread into the unknown, pulling at a loose thread of a sweater to see where it goes (V36 21:12). Writing is a space you enter, an extension of your thinking, with no fixed end (22:59-24:24). He draws on a story about Martha Nussbaum, whom he calls a psychoanalyst. For her, her father's typewriter was a "transitional object", like a child's security blanket: a toy to play with, not an instrument of anxiety (21:37-22:59).
- **Why it works:** Play only happens where you feel safe. If writing feels like a place where you and your ideas are judged, you will approach it anxiously (22:01, 22:59). If it feels like a play space, you stop performing and start discovering (22:59). Real creativity comes from being alive, having a body and having something at stake. He points to David Deutsch for the full argument (24:24). Modern AI has been tuned towards the expected, safe and average (21:12, 24:24).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Start from something you are curious about, not from a planned destination.
  2. Frame the session as "let's see where this goes", not "I need to finish this".
  3. Follow whatever interests you. No pre-planned route.
  4. Let the writing surprise you.
  5. Optional: use a deliberately wild text generator as a springboard for your own divergent thinking, never as copy. He praises early GPT-2 for this, with a nonsense shower-and-lemon continuation as the example (19:52-20:15). He notes newer models have had this unpredictability trained out.
- **Symptoms in a draft:** Anxiety before starting. Writing that performs expertise instead of exploring. Every paragraph heading towards a conclusion fixed in advance. No surprises in the draft.
- **When it does not apply:** He admits he rarely manages to lose himself this way (22:59). (Inferred: this is a thinking-phase stance. It is not how you structure the reader-facing draft. He ends V36 by sending viewers to a video on writing as a tool for thinking versus a tool for communication (25:12).)
- **Illustration:** Instead of "Today I'll write the section on why our onboarding fails", open with "Something odd: our best hires were the ones who got the worst onboarding. Why?" and see where it goes.
- **Timestamp:** V36 19:29-25:12.

## V37: Expert Writing Patterns that Keep Readers Hooked (2026-04-05)

### 1. Who it is for, and the problem
Experts whose writing about their field makes sense to them but not to readers. His opening diagnosis: the patterns you use to write differ from the patterns readers use to understand (V37 0:00). The problem he fixes is a paragraph that is grammatical and uses the right words but reads like "a plate of spaghetti" (0:19).

### 2. Core argument
Clarity comes from organisation, not vocabulary or grammar. The patterns that organise words matter more than the words (0:19). Every sentence has fixed positions and variable content. Two principles decide what goes where: (1) put concrete characters in the subject and important actions in the verb; (2) put familiar, backward-linking context at the start (the topic) and new, emphasis-worthy information at the end (the comment). He demonstrates live by rewriting a tangled paragraph about Truman and the hydrogen bomb decision. The result is a set of consistent "topic strings" that let readers follow.

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics, exercises

#### 3.1 Sentence anatomy: two fixed levels, two variable dimensions (framework)
- **What it is:**
  - Fixed at the level of syntax: subject, verb, object, with modifiers (adverbials, prepositional and transitional phrases) on either side. Their order never changes (V37 2:14).
  - Fixed at the level of organisation: the **topic** runs from the start of the sentence, including opening adverbials, to the end of the subject. The **comment** is everything from the verb on (2:34).
  - Variable: which words are characters and which are actions (3:13), and which information is old and which is new (4:21).
- **Why it works:** The positions are constant, so readers form expectations about them. The writer's freedom lies in what to put where.
- **How to apply:** For any problem sentence, label the topic and comment, then ask two questions. Is a character in the subject and the action in the verb? Is old information in the topic and new information in the comment?
- **Symptoms:** see 3.4 and 3.13.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** "[Topic: After the audit,] [the finance team] [Comment: cut three vendor contracts that had been renewing automatically for years.]"
- **Timestamp:** V37 2:14-4:53.

#### 3.2 Principle 1: characters as subjects, actions as verbs
- **What it is:** The same content can be arranged as "Bees make honey", "Honey is made by bees" or "The making of honey…" (V37 3:13-3:35). The principle is to put a concrete character in the subject and the real action in the verb.
- **Why it works:** Readers follow who does what. When the action is buried in a noun ("support was shrinking") and the subject is an abstraction, readers have to rebuild the story themselves.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Highlight the characters and the actions (see 3.4).
  2. If the subject is not a character (for example "support", "risk"), find who is acting and try them as subject.
  3. Put the action in a verb, changing verbs where needed ("shrinking support" becomes "losing faith", 12:26-13:11).
  4. Check the result against Principle 2 (see 3.14). He reversed one of these changes when it broke old-before-new.
- **Symptoms:** Abstract subjects ("support", "risk", "the recommendation") where people could be. Actions stuck in nouns.
- **When it does not apply:** When moving a character into the subject would put new information in the topic position. In his demo, old-before-new wins (13:39-14:29).
- **Illustration:** Before: "Implementation of the policy by the council resulted in a reduction in complaints." After: "The council implemented the policy, and complaints fell."
- **Timestamp:** V37 3:13-4:21; applied at 8:16, 12:26, 20:02.

#### 3.3 Principle 2: old before new (topic, then comment)
- **What it is:** Put consistent, familiar context at the start of the sentence and new, emphasis-worthy information at the end (V37 4:21-4:53). Old information is either what readers already know or what the passage has already introduced (4:21).
- **Why it works:** As readers move through a sentence, they expect backward-linking context at the start and new, complex material at the end (22:52). His image is a runway: start simple and familiar, then let readers take off into the new complexity (19:15-19:35).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find the recurring character or idea, and put it in the topic.
  2. Move the new point to the end of the sentence.
  3. Use the passive voice, "since" clauses or introductory phrases to get things into the right slot. For example, he rebuilt the opening as "When Truman was considering…, he was faced by many issues…" (6:21-7:25).
- **Symptoms:** Sentences that open with unfamiliar or heavy material. New terms appearing before the reader has context. Reference lines that cross back and forth (3.4).
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Before: "A caching layer, which three engineers built in two weeks, fixed the slow checkout." After (following a sentence about slow checkout): "The fix for slow checkout turned out to be a caching layer, which three engineers built in two weeks."
- **Timestamp:** V37 4:21-4:53, 19:15, 22:52.

#### 3.4 The colour-and-line diagnostic
- **What it is:** Mark each sentence: characters in one colour (he uses green), actions in another (red), and function words such as prepositions in grey, since they carry no content. Then draw lines connecting each mention of the same thing across sentences (V37 4:53-5:39).
- **Why it works:** It makes the organisation visible. Many crossing reference lines are "a sign that something has gone wrong" (4:53).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Copy the paragraph.
  2. Highlight characters, actions and function words.
  3. Draw lines linking repeated characters and ideas.
  4. If the lines zigzag between the starts and ends of sentences, restructure using Principles 1 and 2.
  5. After revising, redraw. The lines should now run down the left side (the topics).
- **Symptoms it catches:** Tangled reference patterns. Characters buried in the middle or at the end of sentences. Grey filler outweighing content.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** In a paragraph about a product launch, "the launch" appears as the object in sentence 1, the subject in sentence 3 and inside a prepositional phrase in sentence 4. The lines zigzag. Revised, "the launch" or "the team" opens every sentence.
- **Timestamp:** V37 4:53-5:39.

#### 3.5 Index position (his term) and enumerating links back to it
- **What it is:** The comment (end) of the paragraph's first sentence holds the idea that governs the passage. Later topics "index" it by linking back up to it (V37 7:25). In the demo, "many issues other than science, domestic politics in particular" goes in that slot, and later sentences open with "First", "Second" and "Third", each naming one of those issues (11:33-12:26, 16:03).
- **Why it works:** Emphasis at the end of the opening sentence sets the frame for everything that follows. Numbered links help readers keep heavy, complex information attached to that frame (11:59-12:26).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Decide what the passage is about. Put it at the end of sentence 1.
  2. Open later sentences with elements that link back to it.
  3. For heavy lists of causes, issues or factors, use plain enumerators (first, second, third) that point back to the index.
- **Symptoms:** A paragraph whose governing idea appears mid-sentence or late in the paragraph. Readers can't tell which items belong to which category.
- **When it does not apply:** He says he doesn't love signposts scattered everywhere. They earn their place when information is complex and heavy (11:59).
- **Illustration:** "When the clinic switched to text reminders, it saw three changes in how patients behaved. First, patients missed fewer appointments. Second, they arrived earlier. Third, more of them rebooked on the spot."
- **Timestamp:** V37 7:25, 11:33-12:26.

#### 3.6 Subordinate the interpretation: ", which" and then "-ing"
- **What it is:** Joining two clauses with "so" (a coordinating conjunction) gives them equal weight. When one clause is an event and the other interprets it, they are not equal. Turn the interpretation into a non-restrictive relative clause (comma plus "which"), then reduce it to an "-ing" phrase: "…a Sino-Soviet bloc, making the Cold War an issue" (V37 9:04-10:48).
- **Why it works:** Grammar should match importance. The -ing reduction works well for the common uses of these clauses: giving a definition, stating an effect, or offering an interpretation (10:17).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find "X, so Y" or "X, and Y" where Y explains, defines or interprets X.
  2. Rewrite as "X, which [verb] Y".
  3. Delete "which" and turn the verb into its -ing form.
  4. Replace any intransitive verb in the process (see 3.7).
- **Symptoms:** Chains of equally weighted clauses joined by "so" or "and". An event and its meaning given the same emphasis.
- **When it does not apply:** When the two clauses really are equally important (inferred from his rule "anytime… one of them is providing an interpretation of the other", 9:27).
- **Illustration:** Before: "The supplier raised prices by 30 percent, so our budget was no longer workable." After: "The supplier raised prices by 30 percent, making our budget unworkable."
- **Timestamp:** V37 9:04-10:48.

#### 3.7 Use a transitive verb for anything that was caused
- **What it is:** Replace intransitive "becoming" verbs with transitive verbs that show the cause. "The Cold War was becoming an issue" becomes "…making the Cold War an issue" (V37 10:48-11:33).
- **Why it works:** He says he hates these intransitive verbs. Few things in the world are truly intransitive: natural processes are (the sun rises, leaves fall), but historical and social changes are caused by someone's action (11:12).
- **How to apply:** Find "became", "grew", "emerged", "arose" or "was becoming" used for a social or organisational change. Ask what made it happen, and make that the agent, or the source of an -ing phrase.
- **Symptoms:** Things that "became issues", "emerged", "grew" with no cause given.
- **When it does not apply:** Natural processes that really are agentless (11:12).
- **Illustration:** Before: "Morale became a problem in the spring." After: "The spring layoffs made morale a problem."
- **Timestamp:** V37 10:48-11:33.

#### 3.8 Split an overloaded sentence, and demote the less important clause with "since"
- **What it is:** When a sentence holds too many characters and actions, split it into cause and effect. Then play down the part that is less relevant to the passage's focus by putting it in a "since" clause: "Third, since the Russians had been first to test the atom bomb, the public demanded…" (V37 14:29-16:03).
- **Why it works:** The passage is about domestic issues, so the foreign event should be background, not emphasis (14:55). "Since" marks its clause as given.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Count the characters and actions. If there are many, split.
  2. Decide which half serves the passage's focus (the index).
  3. Put the other half in a subordinate "since" or "because" clause at the front.
- **Symptoms:** One sentence carrying several actors and actions. The less important event getting main-clause emphasis.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Before: "A competitor launched a cheaper plan and our customers started demanding discounts from the account team." After: "Since a competitor had launched a cheaper plan, customers began demanding discounts."
- **Timestamp:** V37 14:29-16:03.

#### 3.9 Keep weak words out of the stress position
- **What it is:** Don't let a pronoun or minor element occupy the end of the sentence. "The public demanded a strong response from him" becomes "demanded that he respond strongly" (V37 16:03).
- **Why it works:** The end is where emphasis lands (see 3.3). Wasting it on "him" means the important idea gets no weight, and the backward-linking reference belongs earlier.
- **How to apply:** Read the last two or three words of each sentence. If they are a pronoun, a stock phrase or an attribution, rearrange so that the meaningful new word ends the sentence.
- **Symptoms:** Sentences that end on "him", "it", "for them" or "in this regard".
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Before: "Investors wanted a clear plan from her." After: "Investors wanted her to present a clear plan."
- **Timestamp:** V37 16:03.

#### 3.10 Fix the overloaded subject (the runway)
- **What it is:** A long, front-loaded subject is an instant sign of trouble (V37 16:40). Fix it with a short subject, a strong verb and the heavy detail moved into the comment. He also turns an adjective into an adverb ("inevitable" becomes "inevitably"). "Truman's conclusion that… was an inevitable one" becomes "Truman inevitably concluded that…" (17:02-19:15).
- **Why it works:** Readers hold their breath until they reach the verb. Heavy information is easier to manage at the end of the sentence (19:15). A sentence is like a runway: start simple, then take off (19:15-19:35).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Measure the words before the main verb. If there are many, the sentence is front-loaded.
  2. Find the character (often inside a possessive such as "Truman's conclusion") and make them the subject.
  3. Turn the buried noun ("conclusion") into the verb ("concluded").
  4. Move the detail after the verb.
  5. Turn evaluative adjectives into adverbs if needed.
- **Symptoms:** Subjects running to ten words or more. Possessive nouns hiding the agent. An empty verb ("was") at the centre.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Before: "The CEO's belief that the company could not let a competitor be first to ship an AI feature was the reason for the rushed launch." After: "The CEO rushed the launch because she believed the company could not let a competitor ship an AI feature first."
- **Timestamp:** V37 16:40-19:35.

#### 3.11 Demote attributions; turn abstract nouns into a character and a verb
- **What it is:** "The risk in the recommendation was worth taking, according to some historians" becomes "According to some historians, Truman should have risked following the recommendation" (V37 19:35-20:28). The attribution moves out of a prominent slot into an opening adverbial phrase, and "risk", which is not a character, becomes a verb with Truman as the subject.
- **Why it works:** It keeps the topic string on the main character and puts the action in a verb. His stated aim is to move the historians out of the prominent position (20:02).
- **How to apply:** Find abstract subjects ("the risk", "the benefit", "the concern"), ask who took, gained or worried, and rebuild. Move "according to…" phrases to the front as framing, or into a minor slot.
- **Symptoms:** Abstract nouns as subjects. Attributions sitting in emphatic positions.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Before: "The benefit of the delay was clear, in the view of several auditors." After: "In the view of several auditors, the team was right to delay."
- **Timestamp:** V37 19:35-20:28.

#### 3.12 Close the loop: link the last sentence back to the first
- **What it is:** The rewritten final sentence ("Truman could not ignore these powerful political issues") echoes the index in sentence 1 and sums up the passage (V37 21:04).
- **Why it works:** It closes the paragraph by returning to its governing idea.
- **How to apply:** Make the last sentence's topic or comment restate, in closing form, the idea that ended the first sentence.
- **Symptoms:** Paragraphs that end on a side detail or an attribution.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Opening: "When the clinic switched to text reminders, it saw three changes in how patients behaved." Closing: "Those three changes, not the software itself, are why the clinic kept the system."
- **Timestamp:** V37 21:04.

#### 3.13 Topic string check (his term)
- **What it is:** After revising, look at the sequence of topics (sentence openings). In the demo it runs "Truman, Truman, Truman" or points back to the domestic issues (V37 21:54-22:52).
- **Why it works:** Short topics that keep returning to one or two anchors produce clean chains of reference. Readers expect the start of each sentence to connect backwards.
- **How to apply:**
  1. List the subject of every sentence, top to bottom.
  2. Check that each subject is short.
  3. Check that every topic links back to the main character or to the index idea.
  4. If a topic introduces something unconnected, restructure.
- **Symptoms:** Subjects that change every sentence. Long, lopsided subjects. A topic list that reads as random.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated here.
- **Illustration:** Topic list before: "A decline in no-shows / Staff morale / The board's expansion decision / Texting software costs". After: "The clinic / First, patients / Second, patients / Third, the clinic's staff / The clinic".
- **Timestamp:** V37 21:54-22:52.

#### 3.14 When the two principles conflict, old-before-new won in his demo
- **What it is:** He first moved "Republican leaders" into the subject to satisfy Principle 1 ("Republican leaders were losing faith in Truman's foreign policy", 12:26-13:11). He then reversed it: "Truman's foreign policy" is old information that links back to Truman (and to Russia and China), while the leaders are new. So he wrote "Truman's foreign policy was losing support from Republican leaders in Congress" (V37 13:39-14:29).
- **Why it works:** The old-to-new flow, and the topic string it creates, carried more weight for readers than a human subject did in that sentence.
- **How to apply:** When a human agent would be new information and a non-human topic would be old, consider keeping the familiar topic and putting the new agent in the comment. Use the passive voice where needed.
- **Symptoms:** A revision that "fixes" the subject but breaks the chain of reference.
- **When it does not apply:** He presents this as a judgment call. He invites viewers to suggest alternatives (13:11).
- **Illustration:** Previous sentence ends on "…the new pricing page." Next: "The pricing page was criticised by the sales team" keeps the chain. "The sales team criticised the pricing page" breaks it.
- **Timestamp:** V37 12:26-14:29.

Small extra move: **keep parallel names in a consistent order**. The passage says "Sino-Soviet" but then "Russia and China". He admits it is a small point and reorders for parallelism (V37 7:53).

### 4. His stances in V37
- Organisation matters more than vocabulary and grammar (0:19, 1:23).
- He dislikes intransitive verbs for caused events (10:48).
- He dislikes heavy signposting in general, but accepts it for complex lists (11:59).
- Coordinating conjunctions such as "so" wrongly give an event and its interpretation equal weight (9:04).
- He treats rewriting as judgment, not rule-following. He hesitates aloud over grammar ("was faced with" or "faced by") and over word choice, and he reverses his own change (6:57, 13:39).

A skill author may want to check this. He does not cite it here.) He ends by pointing viewers to his video on nine reader-psychology techniques (23:08).

## V38: Why You Should Write an Essay (Even If No One Reads It) (2026-04-09)

### 1. Who it is for, and the problem
People who write or want to write online (tweets, newsletters, micro-essays, books) and people trained by school to think first and write it up afterwards. The problem is twofold. They start from a thesis or topic, so the writing produces no new knowledge. And they are pulled between two bad pieces of advice: write for yourself and ignore readers, or give readers what they want.

### 2. Core argument
Swap "write well" for "write valuably". Readers trade attention for value, and value means answering a question or solving a problem (V38 0:58-1:36). That requires generating new knowledge, which means using writing to think. His method (the "inquiry method") is: start with a question, not a topic; let the question filter evidence; explain the evidence with your own reasoning; let the claim emerge as evidence plus reasoning. Style is a by-product of this struggle with the truth, not something planned. Writing also brings power, since being articulate and being authoritative amount to the same thing. The title is deliberate bait. He attacks the "write as if no one reads it" meme, which he calls a bad import from fiction. The reconciliation is two phases: in phase 1 you think alone, in phase 2 you position the ideas for readers. Skip either phase and it fails. Even unread, the essay gives you what matters most: an articulate mind.

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics, exercises

#### 3.1 Write valuably, not well (reframing)
- **What it is:** Replace the goal of "writing well" (grammar, word choice) with "writing valuably": answering questions or solving problems for readers (V38 0:58-1:36).
- **Why it works:** In nonfiction, readers are not interested in your grammar. They trade their attention for value (1:36).
- **How to apply:** Before drafting, write down the question you are answering or the problem you are solving. If there isn't one, you are not yet writing valuably.
- **Symptoms:** Polishing sentences in a piece that answers nothing. Drafts judged by how they sound.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated. (Inferred from his framing: fiction plays by different rules, 16:38.)
- **Illustration:** "An essay about leadership styles" becomes "Why do teams led by quiet managers report higher trust?"
- **Timestamp:** V38 0:58-1:36.

#### 3.2 Question, not topic; and the "writing in reverse" diagnostic
- **What it is:** Don't start from a topic (a noun) or a thesis. Start from a question (V38 3:03). Writing in reverse means starting with the conclusion and then hunting for evidence, anecdotes and metaphors to stuff it with (2:14). Evidence gathered that way becomes decoration (2:33).
- **Why it works:** A question holds the conclusion in suspension (3:23), so you can generate knowledge rather than decorate a belief you already hold. With a topic, everything counts as evidence and nothing directs the thinking (3:03).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write the question your piece is trying to answer.
  2. If you have a thesis, turn it back into the question it answers.
  3. Check each quote, story or statistic: was it found while answering the question, or recruited to prop up a view you already held?
- **Symptoms:** A thesis sentence written before any exploration. Evidence that only ever agrees with you. Quotes and anecdotes that feel like ornaments. A piece that could be titled with a noun.
- **When it does not apply:** He notes that fast, divergent thinkers resist this because answers jump into their heads. He treats this as a reason to adopt the discipline, not an exception (5:42-6:40).
- **Illustration:** Topic: "remote work". Reverse writing: "Remote work boosts productivity", then hunting for studies. Question: "Why did our most productive team ask to come back to the office?"
- **Timestamp:** V38 2:14-3:23.

#### 3.3 The "I don't know" ritual and the take-sides trap (classroom exercise)
- **What it is:** Students write down their essay's question, then say out loud (paraphrased): "Hmm, I don't know. What evidence would I need to answer this?" (V38 3:23, 5:42). To test whether they really understand, he poses a debate question (online versus offline education) and asks them to walk to opposite sides of the room. When they do, he says "Busted": how can they have an answer before they have written about it (4:17-4:39)?
- **Why it works:** He says anyone who thinks they already understand this point probably doesn't (3:23). The pull to take a side is automatic, and saying "I don't know" aloud interrupts it.
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write the question at the top of the page.
  2. Say, or write, "I don't know."
  3. List the evidence you would need to answer it.
  4. Notice whether you have already picked a side. If so, put it back in suspension.
- **Symptoms:** An opinion formed before any writing or research. Instant certainty on a hard question.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Question: "Should our startup hire generalists or specialists first?" "I don't know. I'd need: which roles blocked us last year, what our three closest competitors did, and how long each type took to ramp up."
- **Timestamp:** V38 3:23-5:42.

#### 3.4 The question is your filter (his phrase)
- **What it is:** From all the evidence, quotes and experiences available, the question tells you what is relevant (V38 5:06-5:42).
- **Why it works:** Without a question you are drowning in information, with no principle for choosing (5:42).
- **How to apply:** For every candidate piece of material, ask: does this help answer the question? Keep it if yes, drop it if no.
- **Symptoms:** A research pile with no rule for including or cutting. Material included because it is interesting, not because it answers anything.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Question: "Why do our trial users churn on day 3?" A fascinating industry-wide churn statistic stays out. A support ticket from a day-3 user goes in.
- **Timestamp:** V38 5:06-5:42.

#### 3.5 The inquiry method: evidence + reasoning = claim (he names the "inquiry method")
- **What it is:** A method for reaching principled conclusions on questions of value, which the scientific method can't settle (V38 6:40-7:24). The steps:
  1. **Question** (7:24).
  2. **Evidence**, filtered by the question. He links the word to the Latin root for seeing: raw data anyone can observe, with no values built in, neither good nor bad (7:24-7:52).
  3. **Reasoning**: values and explanations from your own thinking that make sense of the evidence (8:29).
  4. **Claim** = evidence + reasoning: your original idea (8:29).
- **Why it works:** Humans are biased. Just as science built a method to get closer to the truth, this method does the same for value questions (6:40). It also makes the idea "mathematically sound" and open to challenge. A critic has to say whether they dispute the evidence or the reasoning, and both are visible on the page (8:29-9:12). That is where the power of writing comes from (9:12).
- **How to apply:**
  1. State the question.
  2. Gather evidence the question calls for, and describe it neutrally.
  3. In writing, explain what the evidence means and why: your reasoning, stated openly.
  4. State the claim as what follows from 2 and 3.
  5. Test it: if someone disagrees, can you point to exactly which piece of evidence or step of reasoning they would have to reject?
- **Symptoms:** Claims with no visible reasoning. Evidence described in loaded language, with values mixed into the "facts". Disagreements that can't be pinned down.
- **When it does not apply:** He says it suits value questions, where the scientific method doesn't reach (7:24). (Inferred: for purely empirical questions, use empirical methods.)
- **Illustration:** Evidence: after going remote, one team's ticket closures fell 20% while other teams held steady. Reasoning: that team's work depends on quick, unplanned conversations to unblock each other. Claim: remote work hurts teams whose work runs on interruptions, not teams in general.
- **Timestamp:** V38 6:40-9:31.

#### 3.6 The full sequence from question to style
- **What it is:** His overview (V38 9:31-10:24):
  1. Start with a question.
  2. Read widely about it, trying to answer it for yourself.
  3. As you find relevant evidence, explain it to yourself in writing.
  4. Synthesise the evidence into a framework that makes sense to you.
  5. Only then refine style at the level of sentence, paragraph and word choice.
- **Why it works:** This is what "writing is thinking" means in practice. You use writing to discover what you believe, not to carry ideas you formed earlier (10:24).
- **How to apply:** As listed. Don't polish sentences until step 5.
- **Symptoms:** Line-editing a draft whose argument isn't settled yet. Reading with no question in mind.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** A consultant writing on why clients ignore reports: the question goes at the top; read five post-project reviews; write a paragraph on each; notice a pattern (reports arrive after the decision is made); build a small framework; then edit.
- **Timestamp:** V38 9:31-10:43.

#### 3.7 Style as integrity (framework)
- **What it is:** Style can't be reduced to mechanics. You can't explain Hemingway, McCarthy or Wallace by listing their grammar (V38 10:50-11:44). Style emerges as a by-product of the writer's struggle with their own inquiry: grasping at an idea nobody has put into words yet, then refining the mess (11:44-12:34). He calls style an "integrity", meaning an integration between what you think and what you write. When the two line up, the words you reach for become your style (15:41-16:38).
- **Why it works:** Readers respond to a writer who found something true and said it in a way that resonated with them (12:57). Nobody great was planning fancy sentence structures (12:34).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Don't plan style. Do the inquiry.
  2. When a sentence feels hollow, ask whether you actually believe it and have seen it for yourself.
  3. Refine the mess towards what you really think. Let the style arrive.
- **Symptoms:** Decorative phrasing for its own sake. Borrowed flourishes. Prose that sounds impressive and says little. The "enthusiastic din" of AI-flavoured prose (10:50).
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated. He promises a longer video on style (15:41).
- **Illustration:** Before (performed): "In an era of unprecedented disruption, leaders must embrace radical adaptability." After (integrated): "I've watched three reorganisations fail the same way: nobody told the people doing the work what would stop."
- **Timestamp:** V38 10:50-13:22, 15:41-16:38.

#### 3.8 Articulate equals authoritative (framework on purpose)
- **What it is:** Treat the text as a third party. There is you, the reader, and the text you send into the world to do work: persuade customers, overturn an assumption, earn status with people you respect (V38 13:22-14:11). He defines power as the ability to cause change and to make it more likely that others will comply with a request (14:11). Hence there is no meaningful difference between being authoritative and being articulate (14:30).
- **Why it works:** Writing practises the same skills you use in boardrooms and sales pitches, even when an essay goes unread (14:48).
- **How to apply:** For each piece, name what you want the text to do in the world and to whom. Judge the draft by whether it would do that.
- **Symptoms:** A piece with no intended effect. "Content" that doesn't try to change anything.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Weak purpose: "Share my thoughts on vendor selection." Strong purpose: "Get procurement to add a 30-day trial clause to every software contract."
- **Timestamp:** V38 13:22-14:48.

#### 3.9 Two phases, and the phase-skip diagnostic
- **What it is:** Phase 1 is writing to think, for yourself only. Phase 2 is positioning the ideas as solutions to problems readers care about (V38 18:28). Mixing them paralyses writers, which is why "ignore readers" is helpful up to a point (18:28).
  - Skip phase 2 and you are writing a "Substack" nobody reads, for yourself (19:34).
  - Skip phase 1 and you get audience capture: nothing new, just what readers want (19:34).
- **Why it works:** Bringing readers in too early causes writer's block and corrupts your thinking. We are social animals, and our opinions start bending to whoever has crept into the writer's room (18:28). Writing to please an audience is like a student repeating the professor's opinions back to them (19:34-20:20). Because thinking done in writing becomes part of your mental structure, then your perception, behaviour and character, giving it up to an audience costs something vital (20:20-21:34).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Phase 1: write to answer your question. Keep readers out of the room.
  2. Once you know what you think, phase 2: identify the readers' problem and frame your idea as its solution.
  3. Diagnose: is anyone reading this (phase 2 missing)? Is there anything new in it (phase 1 missing)?
- **Symptoms:** Block when you imagine readers while drafting. Opinions that drift to match the expected audience. Published pieces that are self-absorbed (no phase 2) or pandering (no phase 1).
- **When it does not apply:** He grants that "write for yourself" advice suits fiction (16:38).
- **Illustration:** Phase 1 note: "I think our churn is really an onboarding problem, not a pricing one. Here's why…" Phase 2 opening for the product team: "If you own the trial experience, the fastest lever on churn may be the first ten minutes, not the price."
- **Timestamp:** V38 16:38-21:34.

### 4. His stances in V38
- **Against** the school habit of thinking first and writing it up afterwards (1:36). It works in school but not in nonfiction.
- **Against** the "write for yourself and ignore readers" meme, a bad export from fiction that has produced an ecosystem of Substacks everybody writes and nobody reads (16:38-17:35). He says teachers who push it are themselves "stuck in phase two", telling people what they want to hear (20:20).
- **Against** writing only for an audience (audience capture). It corrupts your thinking and character (19:34-21:34).
- **His position:** obsess over readers, but only in phase 2 (17:35-18:28).
- **On changing your mind:** it is fine to write X today and not believe it in three years. The value was never the content. It was building an articulate mind (21:57).
- **Language and thought:** he states here that they are inseparable. You use language to think, and your language takes the shape of your thinking (21:34-21:57). Compare V40, where he entertains the opposite view.
- Writing is the single most practical skill you can develop (0:00).
- He admits the title and thumbnail are clickbait aimed at the meme (16:38).

- The scientific method, as the model (6:40).

## V39: The writing advice that's keeping smart people small (2026-04-23)

### 1. Who it is for, and the problem
Experts (he names scientists and engineers) writing about science and abstract ideas for general, educated readers, who believe they must "dumb down" their ideas. The problem is expository, encyclopaedia-style writing that lectures readers who don't yet care.

### 2. Core argument
The best science writing uses four storytelling moves, shown through Siddhartha Mukherjee's New Yorker article on epigenetics. (1) Drive the piece with a single question, not a topic. That lets in apparent digressions and forces the writer to make judgment calls. (2) Put what you personally witnessed in the foreground (people, rooms, props) and push research to the background, teaching inside scenes rather than breaking for exposition. (3) Structure the piece in time, because the reader walks it one sentence at a time. Mark every time shift and plant setups that pay off later. (4) Assume the reader doesn't care, and build a bridge from what they do care about, often through people shown as characters rather than labels. You don't dumb down. You trust the reader and carry them there.

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics, exercises

#### 3.1 Drive the piece with one question (so digressions become relevant)
- **What it is:** Instead of a topic ("genetics", "epigenetics", "nature versus nurture", a family memoir), Mukherjee picks one question that threads them together: why are identical twins alike, and why do they differ (V39 0:56-1:57)? He points out that "epigenetics" appears in neither the title nor the opening (0:32).
- **Why it works:** A question lets you include episodes that would otherwise be digressions: a researcher's wartime politics, the Hunger Winter of 1944, an experiment on ants. They stay because each helps answer the question (1:57-2:47). A topic gives you "a pile of things to say", while a question gives you a reason to say them (2:47). A topic alone produces a Wikipedia entry (3:20). Topics bloat: Mukherjee's full topic took a 500-page book (0:56).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write your topic.
  2. Find a specific, puzzling case inside it and phrase it as a why or how question.
  3. Use the question to decide which episodes, however far afield, belong.
  4. Keep the topic word out of the title and opening if it would put readers off.
- **Symptoms:** A draft that reads like an encyclopaedia entry. A title that is a noun. Digressions you can't justify, or relevant material you cut because it seemed off-topic.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** His example is climate policy versus why a town voted against solar panels it had already paid for (2:47). Mine: "urban heat" becomes "Why is one street in Phoenix eight degrees cooler than the next one over?"
- **Timestamp:** V39 0:32-3:20.

#### 3.2 Make the judgment call
- **What it is:** Unlike a topic, a question forces the author to judge (V39 3:20). After reviewing claims that trauma can be inherited epigenetically, Mukherjee steps forward, says they invite scepticism and calls them fantasies (3:43-4:15).
- **Why it works:** Every story has its own moral universe and inner logic, like the Marvel universe. A story without judgment isn't a story; it is back to being a Wikipedia article that passes on information without evaluating it (4:15). Narrative nonfiction is not objective reporting (4:58).
- **How to apply:**
  1. At each contested point, decide what you think the evidence supports.
  2. Say so plainly.
  3. If you lack the credentials to be believed, borrow credibility by interviewing and quoting someone who has them. Don't skip the judgment (4:15).
- **Symptoms:** "Some say X; others say Y" with no verdict. Long neutral surveys. A piece that never tells readers what to make of anything.
- **When it does not apply:** He doesn't give an exception. The only adjustment is how you back the judgment (your own credentials or a borrowed expert).
- **Illustration:** Before: "Some studies suggest standing desks improve productivity, while others find no effect." After: "The studies claiming big productivity gains are small and short. Treat them as hints, not findings, as the ergonomist I spoke to put it."
- **Timestamp:** V39 3:20-4:58.

#### 3.3 Foreground what you saw; background the research (his framework: foreground and background)
- **What it is:** In the foreground, put what you personally witnessed: who you talked to, what was in the room, props, a detail of the office that reveals character, a person's face (V39 8:57-9:54). In the background, put the primary research: papers, statistics, historical context, definitions (10:16). A rounded piece draws on three sources: **research, interviews and reporting** (10:45).
- **Why it works:** The instinct of anyone who knows a lot is to teach: define, explain, give background (5:39-6:00). Readers don't want to be taught at; they want to be shown (6:00). In the age of AI, research is essentially free and readers can get it themselves. What they can't get is you in the room, noticing (11:11-11:29). His emphasis is not that writers reverse foreground and background, but that many never do any in-person reporting, so the foreground holds nothing but information (10:16).
- **How to apply:**
  1. List your material under three headings: research, interviews, reporting.
  2. If the reporting column is empty, go and get some: visit, observe, talk.
  3. Build the scenes from reporting and interviews.
  4. Bring research in behind the scene, in support.
  5. Keep only visual details that help answer the question. No visuals for their own sake (9:54).
- **Symptoms:** An author who never left the office. Paragraphs of definitions and statistics up front. People appearing only as sources of quotes.
- **When it does not apply:** He says this is not mainly about metaphor or visual language. It is about deciding what goes in front (8:57). Visual detail that doesn't serve the question is out (9:54).
- **Illustration:** Before: "Mycorrhizal fungi form networks that connect plant roots and move nutrients between them." After: "Dr. Ruiz knelt, peeled back the leaf litter and held up a clump of soil laced with white threads finer than hair. 'That,' she said, 'is the plumbing.'"
- **Timestamp:** V39 5:39-11:29.

#### 3.4 Teach inside the scene (no exposition breaks)
- **What it is:** When readers need a technical term, don't stop the story to define it. Mukherjee explains histones, which the piece depends on, while the scientist gets up, crosses his cluttered office and points to a model, which Mukherjee likens to a python coiled around a skewer of marshmallows (V39 6:54-7:57).
- **Why it works:** Exposition breaks feel like the clunky dialogue in biopics that crams in information. He mentions *The Social Network* and *Bohemian Rhapsody* (4:58-5:39). No narrator hovers above the story to explain (7:57). The image does more work than paragraphs of explanation, and it is a sign of trust in the reader, not dumbing down (8:17). He praises the image as idiosyncratic, shaped by the writer's own subconscious and experience. He suggests an Indian writer was the one to reach for a python (8:17-8:57).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find each point where the draft stops to define a term.
  2. Attach the explanation to something happening in a scene: an object, a gesture, a demonstration.
  3. Give one concrete, personal image that makes the concept stick.
  4. Keep the narrative moving through the explanation.
- **Symptoms:** "X is a Y that does Z" definitions interrupting the story. Glossary-style paragraphs. A drop in energy wherever a term appears.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Before: "A load balancer is a device that distributes incoming traffic across servers." After: "Priya pulled up the dashboard: twelve green bars, and one red one spiking. 'That's the traffic cop falling asleep,' she said, and tapped the box that was supposed to be spreading requests evenly across the servers."
- **Timestamp:** V39 5:39-8:57.

#### 3.5 Orient the reader in time: god's-eye view versus walking the path
- **What it is:** The writer sees the whole piece from above, like a map. The reader walks the route one sentence at a time with no idea what is round the corner (V39 11:29-12:27). Structure the piece in time, and mark every jump with a plain signal. Mukherjee moves between 1942 Delhi, the 1970s, the present, the 1940s, 1944 and 2003, and each shift has a marker such as "by the time I was born" or "one afternoon in April" (12:45-13:36).
- **Why it works:** He says these are some of the most boring phrases in the language, yet they do a great deal of work: they tell readers where they are on the map (13:36). The gap between the writer's view and the reader's walk is where much nonfiction falls apart (12:27).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Draw the timeline of the piece's scenes.
  2. At every shift, add an explicit time or place marker at the start of the paragraph.
  3. Don't worry about the markers being dull.
- **Symptoms:** Readers confused about when things happen. Flashbacks with no signal. Tenses carrying the whole job of orientation.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** "In the spring of 2011, the plant still ran three shifts." … "Eight years later, only one line was running." … "Back in 1987, when the founder bought the site…"
- **Timestamp:** V39 11:29-13:36.

#### 3.6 Set up, then pay off
- **What it is:** Plant an image early and bring it back later with new meaning. Mukherjee uses freckles twice at the start (a child searching for the exact freckle pattern to tell his mother from her twin) and returns to them at the end as the marks experience leaves on the genome (V39 15:28-16:37). His closing metaphor, that the twins shared the "first derivative of identity", only lands because the whole piece set it up (14:14-14:33).
- **Why it works:** A payoff is impossible without a setup, and a setup is impossible unless you keep the reader oriented in time (16:37). A metaphor that would be merely clever on its own "pops" in context (14:33).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Pick a concrete image from the opening that is connected to your question.
  2. Show it more than once early on.
  3. Near the end, use the same image to express the abstract idea you have built up to.
- **Symptoms:** Closing metaphors that come from nowhere. Opening images that never return. Endings that summarise instead of resolve.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Opening: a grandfather's watch that runs four minutes fast, which the family sets their plans by. Ending, in a piece on institutional habits: organisations keep their own four-minutes-fast clocks, errors everyone has quietly built their routines around.
- **Timestamp:** V39 13:36-16:37.

#### 3.7 Assume the reader doesn't care; build a bridge from what they do care about
- **What it is:** Even educated readers aren't mainly interested in your field, or they would be reading a journal (V39 16:37-17:32). Start from the assumption that they don't care about what you care about. Find what they do care about and build a door from there. Mukherjee enters epigenetics through a family story: two women born in Delhi in 1942 (17:32-18:18).
- **Why it works:** If you assume readers share your values, you are preaching to the converted, and the writing adds nothing (17:32). He cites environmental writing that treats "sustainability", "diversity" and "progress" as obviously good (17:32). By the time readers reach the lab and the histones, they already care what the writer cares about (18:18). You don't debate or persuade readers onto your side. You build a bridge (19:04).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Write down what you want readers to care about.
  2. Write down what they already care about: family, money, health, status, place, fairness.
  3. Open with a story from the second list that leads into the first.
  4. Remove, or earn, any value word you're treating as self-evidently good.
- **Symptoms:** Openings that assume the subject matters. Value words carrying the argument ("resilient", "sustainable", "innovative"). A piece that only works for people who already agree.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Before: "Sustainable urban drainage is essential for resilient cities." After: "The Okafors' basement flooded three times in one summer. The third time, their insurer stopped answering."
- **Timestamp:** V39 16:37-18:44.

#### 3.8 The one-word hyperlink
- **What it is:** A single word from an earlier, emotionally loaded section can carry that emotion into a dry one. Mukherjee describes an ant experiment using the word "caste", and it pulls in the family story, Indian society and the stakes (V39 18:44-19:04). "Hyperlinked" is his word for the effect.
- **Why it works:** A technical digression stays attached to the human story readers already care about. It is part of building the bridge (19:04).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Find the vocabulary of your human or emotional thread.
  2. In the technical sections, choose one accurate word from that vocabulary to describe the technical subject.
  3. Check that it is apt, not forced.
- **Symptoms:** Technical sections that feel cut off from the piece's heart. A sudden drop in warmth.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated. (Inferred: only where the word is accurate for the technical subject. "Caste" is a real term for social insects.)
- **Illustration:** After an opening about a family bakery handed down three generations, a section on gene regulation describes some genes as "the family recipe" that each cell copies but not every cell uses.
- **Timestamp:** V39 18:44-19:04.

#### 3.9 Characters, not labels
- **What it is:** Show scientists and experts as people through specific actions. Calling a researcher passionate is a label. Telling readers he got an A-minus in his first biochemistry course, felt he hadn't mastered it and took it again: that's a character (V39 21:35). Mukherjee's portrait of Allis (a studious fraternal twin sister, his growing up in her shadow) looks like a digression but illuminates the research question Allis devoted his life to (20:04-21:35).
- **Why it works:** Science doesn't happen on its own; people do it, and they run studies, ask questions and make decisions (21:58). If readers can see people, they will follow you anywhere, even into epigenetics (22:57).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Replace every adjective about a person (dedicated, brilliant, passionate) with a concrete action that shows it.
  2. Look for a biographical detail that echoes the question.
  3. Cast widely: patients, homeowners, students and neighbours, not just experts (22:21).
- **Symptoms:** People described with adjectives. Experts appearing only as quoted sources. No one in the piece has a motive.
- **When it does not apply:** He grants that "show, don't tell" is familiar; the difficulty is in the execution (21:00-21:35).
- **Illustration:** Before: "Dr. Lee is deeply committed to her fieldwork." After: "Dr. Lee drives four hours each way, every weekend, to check the soil sensors herself, because the one time she trusted the telemetry, it lied."
- **Timestamp:** V39 20:04-22:57.

#### 3.10 Interview the person, not the source
- **What it is:** Don't interview someone only for information. Interview them as a person pursuing their own motives (V39 21:58). He suggests asking how they came to spend their life on this subject, and what their most important experience was (22:21).
- **Why it works:** Such questions open doors that fact-gathering never will (22:21).
- **How to apply:** Add the "how did you end up doing this?" and "most important experience" questions to every interview. Listen for the detail that connects their life to your question.
- **Symptoms:** Interview notes that contain only facts and quotable claims.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** A fact question: "What does your study show?" A person question: "What made you care about soil in the first place?" Answer: "My dad's farm failed the year I turned twelve."
- **Timestamp:** V39 21:58-22:57.

### 4. His stances in V39

- **Against** exposition that breaks the story (4:58-5:39).
- **Against** treating research as the most important material. In the age of AI it is the least valuable part (11:11).

## V40: I learned a system for writing effortlessly (2026-04-26)

### 1. Who it is for, and the problem
Nonfiction writers who collect notes (in apps such as Obsidian) but treat them like a filing cabinet, so notes never become manuscripts. Also writers who tried the Zettelkasten method and found it dead, and writers who want AI's help without letting it do their thinking.

### 2. Core argument
Build a note system that works like a windmill, not a filing cabinet (V40 0:00). The purpose of a system is what it does, and this one should turn inputs into more valuable outputs (0:28-1:02). Inputs come from two streams, culture and life. They pass through a daily note and three stages of notes, each more refined than the last: fleeting, literature and permanent. Permanent notes link to each other until patterns emerge. AI is added as a read-only navigation layer: indexes, wiki-style maps, a weekly review and a question-and-answer tool over your own notes. It never summarises your sources, never writes your notes and never feeds its output back into your thinking. The rule is that AI works on your thinking; it does not do your thinking (15:54).

### 3. Techniques, frameworks, diagnostics, exercises

#### 3.1 Windmill, not filing cabinet: design for output (framework)
- **What it is:** Judge a note system by what it produces. He borrows a line he attributes to Elon Musk: a successful business turns inputs into more valuable outputs (V40 0:28). Outputs are essays, newsletters and scripts. There are two zones: outside the vault (life and culture flowing through you) and inside the vault (processing) (1:02-1:39).
- **Why it works:** Notes that are only stored never become writing. The aim is a highly concentrated idea you can write about on demand (1:39).
- **How to apply:** Name the outputs you publish. Check that every part of the system moves material towards them. If some part only stores, redesign it.
- **Symptoms:** Thousands of saved highlights and no drafts. A system that feels like filing (15:54).
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** A consultant with 2,000 saved articles and no published pieces: a filing cabinet. The same consultant writing one permanent note a day, linked to an existing note: a windmill.
- **Timestamp:** V40 0:00-1:39.

#### 3.2 Two input streams: culture and life, plus a dream log
- **What it is:**
  - **Culture**: books, essays, podcasts, articles. Saved raw and unprocessed in a sources folder. He uses the Obsidian Web Clipper (V40 1:39-2:45).
  - **Life**: conversations, dreams, thoughts, experiences. Record a voice memo, have AI clean up the transcript, and save it as a fleeting note (2:45-3:21).
  - **Dream log**: he strongly recommends one. Citing Freud's idea of dreams as the royal road to the unconscious, he says the unconscious is the source of creativity and vision, and the better you understand it, the more your output takes on a unique style (3:00-4:00).
- **Why it works:** Capture everything that might be valuable to your thinking, without analysing it yet (1:39-2:06).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Keep one inbox for sources, stored raw.
  2. After a conversation or insight, record a voice memo, clean the transcript and save it as a fleeting note.
  3. Log dreams.
- **Symptoms:** Losing good conversations. Insights that never reach the page.
- **When it does not apply:** Don't analyse in the sources folder; it is for collecting only (2:06).
- **Illustration:** Walking out of a lunch where a colleague said "our clients buy certainty, not software", you record 60 seconds of your reaction and file it that afternoon.
- **Timestamp:** V40 1:39-4:00.

#### 3.3 Daily note and weekly note
- **What it is:** A daily template with (a) an automatic list of every note written or edited that day and (b) two or three high-value tasks linked to the relevant files. The tasks are pulled from a weekly note, which comes out of a weekly review (V40 4:00-4:30). Over the years this becomes a calendar in which you can trace how your thinking developed (4:30-5:20).
- **Why it works:** It is where the whole system starts. Because journal entries, dreams and projects all pass through it, it becomes a rich record of your thinking (5:20).
- **How to apply:** Each morning, open the daily note, copy two or three tasks from the weekly note, and write. Each week, review and set the next week's items.
- **Symptoms:** No record of what you were thinking. Daily work disconnected from weekly aims.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** Daily note for 3 May: "1. Draft opening of churn essay → [[Churn essay]]. 2. Process two fleeting notes from Tuesday." Auto-list below: four notes edited today.
- **Timestamp:** V40 4:00-5:53.

#### 3.4 Fleeting note (catch the experience without friction)
- **What it is:** A quick, rough, unprocessed note about an experience or reaction, given an automatic timestamp ID (year, month, day, time) so you never have to think of a title (V40 6:36-7:02).
- **Why it works:** Not having to title it removes friction and structure, so you can capture the thought before judging it (7:02). His example: a Dan Koe tweet claiming you can't think if you don't write struck him as going too far, even though he has argued that writing is thinking. So he wrote a fleeting note about the reaction (5:53-6:36).
- **How to apply:** When something provokes you, create a note with a timestamp ID and type what you think without tidying.
- **Symptoms:** Reactions left unwritten because they "aren't ready". Time spent naming notes.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** "202605031412: That speaker said 'data beats intuition' and I bristled. Why? Maybe because our best call last year was a hunch that the data later confirmed."
- **Timestamp:** V40 5:53-7:26.

#### 3.5 Literature note: dialogue, not highlights ("where are things the same, where are they different?")
- **What it is:** A literature note is like marginalia: the quote that set you thinking, plus your own reply. It is a record of your dialogue with the text (V40 9:00-9:19). It is not a set of highlights (he criticises Readwise-style imports of unprocessed highlights), not a summary, not a thesis and not a review (9:00-10:14). His active-reading question, from a former professor, is: where are things the same, and where are they different? (10:14)
- **Why it works:** Quotes on their own have no value. They matter only as far as they make you think (9:19). The same/different question runs in the background and draws out your own ideas (10:14-10:37).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Open the source next to a new literature note.
  2. Copy only the lines that provoke you.
  3. Under each, write your response, asking how it resembles or differs from what you already think.
  4. Don't summarise the whole work.
- **Symptoms:** A highlights file you never reread. Summaries without reactions. Reading without a question in mind.
- **When it does not apply:** Most literature-note entries will never be used. That is fine; only some become permanent notes (10:37).
- **Illustration:** Quote from a management book: "Culture eats strategy for breakfast." Note: "Same as my view that incentives beat memos. Different: the author treats culture as fixed, but I've seen one new manager change a team's culture in a quarter."
- **Timestamp:** V40 7:26-10:37.

#### 3.6 Permanent note: one self-contained idea, in your words, linked twice
- **What it is:** An atomic idea: a single self-contained idea in your own words, with a link to its source and a link to a note already in the system (V40 10:37-11:28). It can also be a question you are developing, or an idea you don't agree with (11:28, 14:32). Alphanumeric IDs (for example 3.1A2) exist not to impose a hierarchy but to make sure no note is orphaned. He also quotes a line he attributes to Musk about seeing knowledge as a tree whose leaves attach to branches (12:35-13:26). The resulting structure is messy and associative, "like a rhizome" (13:50).
- **Why it works:** Linking makes ideas talk to each other. His worked chain runs from the Dan Koe reaction, to McCarthy's essay "The Kekulé Problem" (why many scientific problems are solved in dreams, and McCarthy's claim that we don't use language to solve problems), to a permanent note that "we don't think with language", linked to a question note "is language used to think?" A future counter-argument could become the next ID in the chain (7:26-12:17).
- **How to apply:**
  1. Choose one idea from a literature or fleeting note.
  2. Write it in your own words, as one idea.
  3. Link to the source.
  4. Find an existing note it speaks to and link it.
  5. Give it an ID that places it next to that note.
- **Symptoms:** Notes that are quotes, not ideas. Notes covering several ideas. Orphan notes that link to nothing.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated. He says permanent notes need not be beliefs (14:32).
- **Illustration:** "Teams adopt tools they helped choose and resist tools handed to them, even when the handed tool is better. Source: [[Interview with ops lead]]. Links to: [[Ownership predicts adoption]]."
- **Timestamp:** V40 10:37-13:50.

#### 3.7 Let patterns surface, then add value by comparing
- **What it is:** As permanent notes pile up, patterns appear on their own. He noted a Lacanian psychoanalyst's claim, from a podcast, that Lacan grounds truth in "the truth of desire". Days later David Deutsch tweeted something similar: follow your desire in the moment and use long-term goals to critique it (V40 13:50-14:54). He then added his own value by noting where the two ideas agree and where they differ (14:54-15:54).
- **Why it works:** Processed notes become the framework through which you interpret the world, and you start noticing unexpected connections to it (15:54).
- **How to apply:** When a new input echoes an old note, link them and write a short note on how they agree and how they differ.
- **Symptoms:** Each input handled in isolation. No notes that span two domains.
- **When it does not apply:** Not stated.
- **Illustration:** A permanent note from a nursing memoir ("triage is deciding who not to help first") links to one from a product book ("roadmaps are lists of what we won't build"). A new note on the similarity and difference: both are about saying no, but triage is reversible within hours and roadmaps aren't.
- **Timestamp:** V40 13:50-15:54.

#### 3.8 AI as a read-only navigation layer (principle plus three agents and a Q&A tool)
- **What it is:** He resisted putting AI into his notes because he doesn't want it doing his thinking. The principle: AI works on his thinking rather than doing it (V40 15:54). In practice it doesn't process his inputs, summarise articles, tell him the main ideas or point out links between notes (15:54-16:48). He brought AI in because in a scattered week he forgot Monday's thread and wanted to see the big patterns over time (16:48). The agents run weekly with read-only access. They can't rewrite, delete or touch the human notes (16:48-17:36):
  1. **Keyword index:** like the index at the back of a book, covering the whole vault. It surfaces themes and cross-connections and creates higher-order links, "a source of serendipity" (17:36-18:33).
  2. **Wiki agent:** "hub notes" (maps of all notes on a topic under headings) and "structure notes" (Wikipedia-like narrative syntheses built only from his permanent notes). Both flag gaps, open questions, unfollowed links and thin areas (18:33-19:40).
  3. **Weekly review** (Sunday morning): summarises tasks done, patterns in dreams, themes in fleeting notes and key ideas. It compares the week against quarterly goals and suggests a focus (19:40-20:35).
  4. **Q&A tool**, which he calls the most powerful: ask the vault "Have I thought about this before? Do I have notes that might help?" His example: a debate about South Korea's fertility rate led him to wonder whether the decision is emergent at group level, like an ant colony. The Q&A surfaced forgotten notes and showed him which sources and fleeting notes to process next (20:35-21:47).
  - Q&A outputs go in their own folder and are excluded from the wiki agents' inputs, so AI output never muddies his thinking (21:47-22:41).
- **Why it works:** It keeps the thinking his. AI helps him move around the vault without rewriting it (22:41). His summary is AI as a navigation layer over a knowledge graph you built yourself (23:23). Many people misread the promise of AI as help with writing, but you want it neither writing nor thinking for you (22:41).
- **How to apply (as rules for any AI helper, including a writing skill):**
  1. Give the AI read-only access to the writer's own material.
  2. Let it index, map, flag gaps, remind and retrieve ("have I thought about this before?").
  3. Don't let it summarise sources in place of the writer, generate their ideas or edit their notes.
  4. Keep AI output apart from the writer's notes, and never feed it back as if it were the writer's thinking.
  5. Use what it retrieves as a to-do list of what to process yourself next.
- **Symptoms of misuse:** AI summaries standing in for reading. AI-written text in your notes. Your "ideas" traceable to AI output.
- **When it does not apply:** He is explicit that AI should not do the writing or the thinking (22:41).
- **Illustration:** A writer asks: "I'm starting to think our churn is a status problem, not a price problem. Have I written anything related?" The helper returns links to three old notes (one on luxury pricing, one on a customer interview, one fleeting note about "people leave when they feel foolish") and says which to reread. It does not write the argument.
- **Timestamp:** V40 15:54-23:23.

## Across these five videos

### How the ideas connect
1. **The question is the spine, at every stage.** V38 makes the question the filter for evidence and the start of the inquiry method. V39 makes it the device that admits apparent digressions and forces judgment calls, repeating V38's point about topic versus question with a new example. V40 carries it into note-taking: a permanent note can be a question, the literature-note question is "same or different?", and the AI Q&A asks "have I thought about this before?" V36's whimsy is the loose, playful version: following a curiosity rather than a destination. Together these videos say that inquiry, not topic, drives both thinking and the reader-facing piece.

2. **Old before new works at three scales.** V37 applies it to the sentence: familiar topic first, new comment last, topics indexing the paragraph's governing idea. V39 applies the same logic to the whole piece and to values. Readers walk the text one sentence at a time, so orient them (time markers). Start from what they already care about (family, place) before the new thing (epigenetics). Plant a setup before the payoff. V39's "caste" hyperlink is the whole-piece version of V37's topic string: a backward link that keeps new material attached to what the reader already holds. For a skill, this is one principle, "start where the reader already is", applied to the sentence, the structure and the argument about values.

3. **The two-phase model organises the set.** V38 states it outright: phase 1 is writing to think, readers kept out; phase 2 is positioning for readers. V36's play space and V40's note system are phase 1 tools. V37's sentence mechanics and V39's storytelling moves are phase 2 tools. V36's "stakes" belongs to the reader-facing side. V38 shows how the two fit: readers too early cause block and corrupt thinking, readers never means no one reads.

4. **Judgment is the human contribution.** V38 treats evidence as value-free and puts values in the reasoning; claim = evidence + reasoning. V39 insists a story without judgment is a Wikipedia entry and shows Mukherjee calling a claim a fantasy. V36 locates value in stakes and perspective. V37 shows judgment at sentence level: he weighs trade-offs aloud and reverses his own fix when the two principles conflict.

5. **What only you can supply.** V36 lists it (lived experience, a body, rule-breaking, stakes, whimsy). V39 makes it practical: research is now almost free, so what you personally saw belongs in the foreground. V40 builds the store of it: life inputs, dreams, and your own words in permanent notes, with AI kept out of the content.

6. **Metaphor and image.** V36's test (the familiar must change how we understand the unfamiliar, and must come from the body) is illustrated in V39. Mukherjee's python-and-marshmallows image is praised as idiosyncratic, shaped by his subconscious and experience, which is exactly V36's point. V39 adds a structural point: a metaphor that is merely clever on its own lands at the end of a piece because of its setup ("first derivative of identity"). So metaphor works both through bodily familiarity (V36) and through position within the piece (V39).

7. **Style and voice are emergent, never planned.** V36: voice emerges from lived experience interacting with language (complex adaptive systems, ant colonies). V38: style is a by-product of struggling with an inquiry, an "integrity" between thought and words. It can't be explained by mechanics; he names Hemingway, McCarthy and Wallace. V39: the image only Mukherjee could have given. V40: the unconscious (via dreams) colours output and gives it "unique style". Across these weeks he moves from "lived experience" (V36), to alignment of thought and word (V38), to cultivating the unconscious as the source (V40). He announced a longer video on style in V38.

### What develops or shifts between them
- **"Writing is thinking" gets qualified.** In V38 (9 April) he says language and thought are inseparable and you think with language (21:34-21:57). In V40 (26 April) he calls Dan Koe's "if you don't write, you can't think" a step too far, and he builds a note chain around McCarthy's claim that problem-solving happens unconsciously, not in language. He leaves it open. The permanent note says we don't think with language, but he expects a counter-argument might follow. For a skill: treat writing as the best tool for thinking and discovery, not as the only place thinking happens. Incubation, dreams and unconscious work count too (V40 3:21-4:00, 7:26).
- **The AI stance develops without contradiction.** V36 frames the human edge as writing without AI, allowing AI only as a surprise-generating co-pilot, and says newer models have lost even that. V39 turns the AI age into a craft rule: research is cheap, so report. V40 brings AI into his own workflow, for cleaning up voice-memo transcripts and as read-only agents over his notes, and says the system only became possible with AI. The constant line is that AI must never do the thinking or the writing.
  - One tension within V40: he says he didn't want AI "pointing out connections between my notes" (15:54-16:48), yet the keyword-index agent surfaces connections and creates higher-order links (18:05), and the Q&A summarises how retrieved notes connect (21:47). His resolution seems to be where the output lives and how it is used. AI output sits in a separate read-only layer, serves as a pointer to what he should process himself, and never enters the human notes.
- **Readers: from attack to reconciliation.** V38 attacks "write as if no one reads", yet the title still claims value when no one reads: the benefit to the writer is an articulate mind. V36 says real writing needs readers you respect and your name on the work. Taken together: aim at real readers (stakes, phase 2), knowing the thinking pays off regardless.
- **Rules.** V36 insists on learning rules before breaking them, and anticipates being cast as a finger-wagging professor. V37 is the most rule-like video in the set, but its rules are about how readers process sentences, not grammar etiquette, and he applies them with visible judgment. Both V36 and V38 open by saying they are not about grammar or style tips. V37 opens by saying words matter less than the patterns that organise them. So even his sentence-level teaching is organisational, not stylistic.
- **Recurring motifs** that tie the set together. Ant colonies as emergence: voice in V36, "caste" ants in V39's example, South Korea's fertility decision as a hive effect in V40. Cormac McCarthy: *Suttree* in V36, style in V38, "The Kekulé Problem" in V40. David Deutsch: creativity in V36, desire and goals in V40. The unconscious: the play space in V36, Mukherjee's subconscious image in V39, dreams in V40. Wanting the respect of people you respect: V36 17:19 and V38 13:22.

### Notes for turning this into a skill (drawn from the above)
- **Diagnose before prescribing phase.** Is the user still thinking (phase 1: question, evidence, reasoning, notes, play) or shaping for readers (phase 2: bridge, foreground, time markers, old before new)? Don't line-edit a phase 1 draft (V38 9:31).
- **Phase 1 prompts the skill can ask:** What question are you answering? What evidence would you need? What is your reasoning, and which part would a critic reject? Where is this the same as, or different from, something you already believe? Have you written about this before?
- **Phase 2 checks the skill can run:**
  - Sentence level: characters as subjects, actions as verbs, old before new, index position, topic strings, overloaded subjects, weak words at the end, intransitive "became" verbs, and ", which / -ing" for interpretations (V37).
  - Piece level: a driving question, a judgment made, foreground versus background, teaching inside the scene, time markers, setup and payoff, starting from what the reader already cares about, characters not labels (V39).
- **Voice checks:** Is there lived experience in the draft? Are metaphors bodily and illuminating rather than formally correct but "a few degrees off"? Is each rule-break deliberate? Is there something at stake (V36)?
- **Guardrail, in his own terms:** the skill should navigate, question and diagnose. It should not generate the writer's ideas or rewrite their thinking (V40 15:54, 22:41; V36 5:02).
