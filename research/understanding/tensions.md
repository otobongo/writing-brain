# Tensions in the Writer Science method, and how a skill should settle them

This file collects every place where William Fitzpatrick's advice seems to pull two ways across the 50 videos (V1 to V50). For each one it gives both sides with citations, explains what is actually going on, and states the resolution a writing skill should adopt.

Sources: the ten deep-read reports (read_01 to read_10), checked against the transcripts in `readable/`. The load-bearing timestamps were re-verified in the transcripts.

Conventions:
- Citations are V-number plus m:ss (for example V23 36:18). Ranges use "to".
- Everything is paraphrase except one short quotation (in F1). Elsewhere, quotation marks mark rule names, words used as words, or my own examples, not his wording.
- All examples are mine, written in the spirit of his. None are copied from his videos.
- "Resolution" means what the skill should do, not a claim about what he says. Where he reconciles a tension himself, I say so. Where I am inferring, I say that too.

---

## 1. Six lenses that dissolve most tensions

Almost every contradiction in the corpus disappears once you ask which of these six things differs between the two pieces of advice.

1. **Stage.** He works with three or four modes, and most "opposite" advice belongs to different ones:
   - **Venting:** daily, goal-free freewriting that is for nobody and is not a draft (V41 22:53 to 25:56).
   - **Exploring (phase 1, the writer's draft):** writing to find out what you think, sometimes tethered to a question (V19 21:46; V20 6:12; V38 18:28; V42 16:31).
   - **Shaping (phase 2, the reader's draft):** rebuilding for a specific reader (V19 27:22; V31 8:47; V47 17:30; V50).
   - **Finishing:** compression, certainty calibration, mechanics (V24 21:04; V29; V50 30:18).
2. **Genre and function.** Expository or professional writing (point first, problem-led) differs from narrative and creative nonfiction (image-led, thesis may arrive late), from peer-reviewed academic writing (more modulation), from book positioning (V16, V17), and from spoken scripts (oral signposting is fine, V9 14:50). He is explicit that every genre has a function (V25 3:18; V20 17:08).
3. **Audience.** Peers inside a discourse community differ from outsiders such as funders or the lay public (V47 14:32 to 16:21). Cold readers differ from committed ones such as a teacher or a boss (V46 1:04, 5:13). Readers may be uninformed, indifferent or resistant (V24 10:50). One reader differs from a mass market (V17 24:55).
4. **Scale.** Title, introduction, section, paragraph, sentence and word each follow different rules. The same principle can look opposite at two scales (see D2).
5. **Level.** Parsing and logic (make them effortless and explicit) differ from feeling and meaning (leave room for the reader). Correctness differs from rhetorical effect; he calls this syntax versus semantics (V34 8:51).
6. **Era.** He changes his mind in public, and often says so (V19 11:50; V23 10:07, 38:09; V41 1:13; V42 throughout; V47 12:59; V50 2:00). Where he explicitly reverses himself, the later position wins. Where the later video only sharpens the earlier one, both stand.

A seventh, smaller lens is **packaging versus substance.** His video titles ("hacks," "EXACT," "DELETE your writer's block") are YouTube packaging. He admits it himself (V38 16:38). The skill should take the substance, not the packaging.

## A. Process and stage

### A1. Write for yourself vs write for readers

**Pull toward yourself.**
- While writing, the only audience is you: door closed (V11 2:03).
- In the writer's draft, ignore readers, because you don't yet know what you think (V19 24:34 to 25:11; V20 8:25).
- In phase one, writing about whatever interests you is valid (V31 7:55).
- Bringing readers in too early causes block and corrupts your thinking (V38 18:28).
- "Don't worry about readers" works as a cure for block (V42 20:28 to 20:50).
- Don't think about reading patterns at all while generating (V47 21:09). Ignore sentence-subject work in the first draft (V33 14:11).

**Pull toward readers.**
- Writing only for yourself isn't really writing; it's journaling (V12 18:09).
- A book written without the reader in mind is a diary (V17 37:54; V18 15:26).
- "Write about whatever interests you" is the worst advice (V25 0:00). He attacks writers who say they don't know their reader yet and are just getting ideas down (V25 0:45).
- "Just write for yourself" is one of the worst pieces of advice (V27 30:36). It is misguided advice for published work (V31 7:00), and a bad export from fiction (V38 16:38 to 17:35; V42 20:50 to 21:23).

**What is going on.** Stage. The two-phase model is present from V11 on. V11 itself closes by saying that readers care only whether your ideas solve their problems (V11 15:32 to 15:50). Every "worst advice" attack targets writing for yourself as the **purpose of the finished piece**, not as a drafting mode. He grants the advice for fiction (V38 16:38) and as a block cure (V42 20:28).

V25 0:45 looks like a direct hit on V19's "ignore readers." But its target is the writer who treats the reader as an afterthought forever: the reader treated as a finishing touch rather than a core ingredient (V25 2:24).

Even in phase one there is a light reader orientation. The question you tether a freewrite to should keep your centre of gravity on something readers will value (V20 7:00 to 7:40). That puts the reader in the choice of question, not in the room while you draft.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Ask the stage before giving any feedback.
- In exploring mode, never raise audience critiques ("who is this for?") about the draft's prose. The most the skill should do is ask what question the draft is chasing.
- Before shaping begins, require a named reader and their problem. A reader who is still "TBD" at that point is the V25 failure.
- Never describe a finished nonfiction piece as self-expression.

### A2. Keep editing out of drafting vs revision is writing

**Pull toward separation.**
- Decide whether a session is for producing or perfecting, and edit in its own time slot (V11 2:03 to 2:24, 4:27 to 5:13).
- Thinking and writing for readers at the same time is torture (V19 21:46).
- Don't edit the first draft (V20 8:25).
- You can't sculpt magma: generating and judging at once blocks the flow (V41 27:53).
- Backspacing and inner commentary are signs that judgment has crept in (V42 17:29).

**Pull toward recursion.**
- He calls the advice to separate writing from revision exactly backwards (V23 36:18). He pauses after each section to reread, maps the emerging shape, hunts the strongest thoughts, and builds structure around them (V23 36:18 to 38:09).
- Revising counts toward a writing session (V23 10:07 to 11:49).
- DeLillo gives each paragraph its own page so he can reshape as he goes (V10 10:08).

**What is going on.** Two different things are being called "revision."
- V23 attacks revision as the school model: error-correction on a finished, static text at a separate later moment (V23 37:13). What he endorses mid-draft is **structural rereading**: seeing what you have found and where the piece wants to go. That is the architect glancing in (V23 35:22).
- V11, V41 and V42 attack **line-level judgment at the moment of generation**: fixing the sentence you are still typing.

So the real distinction is by level: shape-finding is welcome while drafting, surface-fixing is not. The audiences differ too. V11 is for beginners stuck on editing. V23 describes his professional practice.

**Resolution for the skill.** Separate three acts.
1. Generating: no judgment, no backspacing.
2. Rereading for shape between bursts: encouraged. Ask what has turned up, which idea is strongest, and what an orphan sentence might be the real point of.
3. Polishing sentences: only once the point is known.

During a drafting session the skill may help with act 2 but should decline to line-edit.

### A3. Rewrite from scratch in a new document vs work from the messy draft

**Pull toward a new document.**
- Start a new document and don't copy and paste. The first draft is for you (V19 27:22 to 29:54).
- The writer's draft goes in the drawer (V20 7:40 to 9:12).
- No amount of line editing makes a thinking draft valuable (V31 8:47, 21:37; V35 12:09 to 13:02; V47 17:30 to 18:15).

**Pull toward salvage.**
- Build the structure around the strongest ideas already in your uncensored draft (V23 36:18).
- A reverse outline labels what the existing draft does (V35 8:11 to 9:41).
- You already have all the pieces (V47 12:03).
- V50 works directly on the messy draft. It finds the point sentences in the introduction and conclusion, compares them, moves each section's point to its index, and preserves meaning and voice (V50 0:00, 4:24 to 15:06).

**What is going on.** Era plus emphasis. Mid-2025 is his most absolute phrasing (V19, V20). By September 2026 (V50) he shows a procedure that reuses material, though it is structural relocation, not line editing.

The constant across both is that you cannot line-edit a thinking draft into a reader draft. The "new document" is a device to break the grip of the writer's route. It is not a ban on reusing good sentences. The earlier system summary reaches the same reconciliation (salvage the points, rebuild the structure).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- The rule: the structure is always rebuilt, and sentences may be salvaged.
- The default method:
  1. Reverse outline the draft.
  2. Compare the point sentences in the introduction and conclusion, and synthesize the sharper one.
  3. Place it at the end of the introduction.
  4. Move each section's point to its index.
  5. Order the sections by the reader's questions.
  6. Rewrite the connective tissue.
- Recommend a fresh document when the whole draft runs in discovery order, or when the writer keeps tinkering with sentences instead of restructuring.
- Never offer line edits on a draft whose point is not yet stated where the reader looks for it.

### A4. Cut ruthlessly vs don't cut early

**Pull toward cutting.**
- Kill your darlings: delete every word that doesn't change the meaning (V6 1:08 to 2:43).
- McCarthy's test, asking whether each element is needed, applies to every mark, word, sentence, paragraph and section (V22 2:24).
- Be ruthless with fillers, doublets and redundancy (V29 4:44).
- Cut material even if it is true and interesting when it gives the reader no value (V19 1:05:58 to 1:07:30). Cut the author's spiritual-journey story if it doesn't serve the reader's problem (V18 13:23 to 14:32).
- Cut good tangents that serve none of the two or three points (V22 17:32 to 19:00). If a section changes nothing for the reader, cut it (V17 38:16).

**Pull against cutting early.**
- You can't worry about concision when you have just started. Cutting 10 of 20 pages is a disaster, and early pruning may throw away the paragraph holding your next idea. His term for it is writing anorexia (V23 34:19 to 35:22). You can only afford to cut when you are rich (V23 35:22).
- Let half-baked ideas grow. X becomes Y only if you don't kill X (V11 5:30 to 8:33).
- Keep freewriting scraps where they can resurface months later (V41 27:53).

**What is going on.** Two lenses.
- **Stage:** cut when rich, meaning in the reader draft and later.
- **Kind of cut:**
  - V18, V19 and V22 describe **content cuts** judged by value to this reader.
  - V6 and V29 describe **word-level compression** judged by redundancy.
  - V23 is about neither in the reader draft. It is about pruning during generation.

Era matters a little. V6 (2024) on its own makes pruning look like the main event. The later videos place it late: V29 calls compression the start of the polishing work (V29 20:02), and V24 puts mechanics last (V24 22:56). V23's own loop says to "cut or save" weaker ideas (V23 36:18). So cut from the piece, but keep the material in the notes.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Never cut during generation.
- Make content cuts in the reader draft using a value test: does this serve this reader's problem, or the theme and its two or three points?
- Do word-level compression near the end, after structure and flow.
- Send cut material to a scraps file, not the bin.
- Compression is not shortness (V29 2:15). It must never remove a hedge the evidence requires (see B1) or the repetition that carries coherence (see E3).

### A5. No outlines and emergent structure vs fixed structures and outlines

**Pull toward emergence.**
- Outlining first reliably blocked him. He changed his mind: find the through-lines after the first draft (V42 14:20 to 20:01).
- Outline-first doesn't fit expert thinking. Use a reverse outline (V47 20:14; V35 8:11).
- Shape should emerge from function, not a template (V31 2:16 to 3:35, 22:48).
- He tells writers they don't need templates at all (V20 24:25). There is no universal template and no master prompt (V49 7:34, 9:16).
- Structure should emerge from the text, not from imposed chronology (V50 13:22).

**Pull toward fixed structures.**
- Sharpen your axe: don't start a book without a blueprint (V17 38:16).
- Write with an outline, and give every chapter a table of ideas (V18 23:07).
- The problem–solution framework takes the guesswork out of structure (V19 30:43).
- Five-move introductions: V22 5:23; V27 25:12; V28 14:52.
- Six arrows: write the first and last lines first. He rejects the objection that the structure cramps your thinking (V43 5:43, 7:55).
- Five fixed reader locations (V46) and a six-part editing system (V50).

**What is going on.** Stage, plus what the structure governs. The forms he endorses name **reader-facing functions and locations**: the promise, the problem, the index, the key terms, the counterargument, the push-off. They apply once you know your point. The forms he rejects are **content templates imposed before thinking**: five-paragraph shapes, "three points" outlines, the thesis-first rule designed for grading.

He says this himself:
- None of it is about rules, only about where readers look (V19 51:23).
- The problem–solution frame is something of a conceit, imposed in the reader draft (V19 34:34).
- The fractal index pattern is not something he is prescribing (V46 9:53).
- V18 separates pantsing, which is fine for exploring, from compiling a book, which needs structure (V18 16:48 to 17:41).

V17's axe is project planning (goal, reader, promise, table of contents as a bridge) before drafting the book, not before exploratory writing.

The one sharp edge is V42 versus V43, eleven days apart. The reader-9 reconciliation holds:
- The six arrows are reader moves, not content slots.
- Knowing your first and last lines presupposes that you already know your position.
- The title at the centre does the job of V42's tether question.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Never ask for an outline before a thinking draft exists.
- After the thinking draft, run a reverse outline.
- For the reader draft, offer his sequences as checks, or as scaffolds once the writer can state the point:
  - Introduction: common ground, problem, solution.
  - Every unit: index, then discussion.
  - Essay: the six arrows.
- For books and long projects, plan the goal, reader, pain and table of contents after enough exploratory writing to know the promise.
- Describe structure by function (what the reader needs here), in the spirit of the Starbucks test (V20 17:08 to 21:38).

### A6. Start from a question and don't know your ending vs commit to a thesis

**Pull toward open inquiry.**
- Start with a question, not a topic or a thesis. Say "I don't know" aloud. Starting from a conclusion and hunting evidence is writing in reverse (V38 2:14 to 5:42; V19 3:19 to 4:05).
- Let the claim emerge from logged evidence (V17 6:42 to 7:38).
- Don't know your conclusion when you start, and don't hoard your best idea for the end (V23 32:41 to 34:19).
- X becomes Y (V11 6:14). Write first, understand later (V35 4:54).

**Pull toward commitment.**
- Write the opening and closing lines first; commit and don't waffle (V43 5:43).
- Lead with the point (V24 4:18 to 7:28; V18 21:12).
- A claim should be significant, non-obvious and debatable (V3 8:00). An argument is claim, evidence and warrant (V25 8:27).
- Synthesize one crisp point sentence (V50 4:24 to 8:23).

**What is going on.** Stage, and the direction of reasoning. Discovery runs question, evidence, reasoning, claim (V38 6:40 to 9:31). Presentation runs claim, reason, evidence (V50 17:24; the uneven U starts at level 4, V50 22:13). The committed thesis of V43 and V24 is the **output** of the question phase. V23 even calls "don't know your ending" perhaps just his personal preference (V23 32:41).

Genre nuance: in narrative science writing a driving question can stay visible in the published piece as its spine, but the writer must still make judgment calls along the way (V39 0:56 to 4:58).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- While exploring, ask for the question and what evidence would answer it, and discourage a preset thesis.
- When shaping, ask for the point sentence and place it.
- Triage rule: if the writer cannot state the point in one arguable sentence, they are still exploring. Send them back to question-driven writing, not to sentence polish.

*Illustration (mine).* Exploring: "Why do our trial users stop on day three? I don't know. I'd need the day-three support tickets and the onboarding emails." Shaping: "Trial users quit on day three because that is when the product first asks them for data they don't have."

### A7. Freewrite tethered to a question vs goal-free freewriting

**Pull toward a tether.**
- Put the question your piece must answer at the top of the page. Morning pages are fine, but readers won't care about your journal or dream log (V20 6:12 to 7:40).
- Write your question at the top of a fresh note each morning (V35 6:40).
- A tether question is optional, to go back to when you drift (V42 17:29).

**Pull toward no goal.**
- Daily freewriting is not newsletter material, not the first phase of an essay, not even a first draft. Its only goal is to open a vent (V41 22:53 to 24:26).
- Don't use "good" or "bad" about it (V41 24:03). Expect roughly 90% dross (V41 31:10).
- He strongly recommends a dream log (V40 3:00 to 4:00).

**What is going on.** Two practices with two purposes.
- Venting keeps the unconscious channel open and supplies scraps.
- Tethered exploration works a specific piece's question.

V20 and V40 agree that a journal or dream log is not reader-facing output. They differ on whether it is worth keeping. V40 (2026) treats it as input to the note system, which fits his later interest in the unconscious (V41 16:41 to 20:09).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Offer both practices, labelled separately.
- Never evaluate venting. After it has cooled, help the writer look for lines with "shine" (V41 27:01).
- For project work, ask for the tether question.
- Don't turn venting into a draft, and don't let the tether harden into a thesis.

### A8. Discipline and habits vs give up discipline

**Pull toward discipline.**
- Make an unconditional commitment and protect the new habit for about two months (V23 3:26 to 6:52).
- Write in the morning (V23 6:52).
- Aim for about two pages, or just complete the session. The Seinfeld-style rule: you don't have to write, but you can't do anything else (V23 10:07 to 11:49).
- Announce the habit, not the project (V23 9:17).
- Use timers and blockers (V11 3:01 to 4:27).
- Accountability partners and deadlines (V44 7:46, 27:14).
- Beat the perfectionism loop: set a release date (V23 27:37 to 29:19).

**Pull against discipline.**
- During his year-long block he doubled down on discipline and habit science, and it made things worse (V41 1:13).
- The Seinfeld locked-room routine is more myth than practical advice (V41 13:10 to 13:46).
- Discipline is a polite word for forcing the unconscious (V41 21:17).
- The project itself may belong to a past self-image; put it away (V41 1:13 to 3:07, 9:08).
- Real improvement comes from who you are with, not from forcing yourself (V44 0:00, 34:31).

**What is going on.** Three lenses.
- **Era:** an explicit change of mind in May 2026.
- **Type of writing:** V41 itself exempts low-value writing such as emails and reports, where a forcing function may work (V41 13:46).
- **Type of block:** a getting-started or finishing problem differs from deep, persistent dread on a long, self-chosen creative project.

V23 already had the gentle half: aim low, forgive misses, drop the drill sergeant, keep it fun (V23 13:37 to 19:52). V11 had the escape valve: if resistance survives every method, stop and listen to it (V11 10:41). V44's "finishers" still need deadlines and a partner (V44 4:35 to 8:01). V41 adds that he is still for goals and for discipline in the rest of life (V41 21:17).

Persisting and dropping also pull against each other. Finish the piece; it is your next work, not your magnum opus (V23 27:37). Or let go of a project that belongs to an old self (V41). The distinction is between avoiding judgment, which means you should ship, and working against present evidence, which means you should consider shelving.

**Resolution for the skill.** Diagnose the block before prescribing anything.
- **Can't start or can't finish:** a small, kind, consistent container: a timer, a low target, a partner, a deadline. Never carry debt forward to the next day (V23 16:20 to 19:52).
- **Endless polishing or research:** the perfectionism loop. Set a release date.
- **Months of dread, with other ideas that keep interrupting:** the V41 route. Stop forcing, ask whose project this is, freewrite daily without a goal, and look at what keeps pulling.
- **Frozen by imagined judges:** see A9.
- Never prescribe stricter word counts for the third case.

### A9. Readers cause block vs readers cure block

**Pull: readers cause block.**
- The expert's curse: imagining a demanding reader too early produces start-and-delete cycles (V20 1:05 to 5:22).
- Readers brought in too soon cause block (V38 18:28).
- The social self (Lacan's Symbolic register) seals off the unconscious (V41 5:36 to 10:46).
- Play needs safety. If writing feels like a place where you are judged, you approach it anxiously (V36 21:37 to 22:59).

**Pull: readers cure block.**
- Block and voice problems disappear when you are talking with someone you respect and whose respect you want (V44 0:00, 28:53 to 32:40).
- On a first date, words flow when you actually want to reach the person (V45 1:08 to 3:04).
- Stakes produce clarity: people you respect reading your work under your name (V36 13:48 to 19:06).
- Talk a stuck piece through with one trusted person (V23 24:09).
- Imagine an audience and a 30-minute limit to get your point across (V11 9:14 to 10:00).

**What is going on.** Relationship versus evaluation. The blocking reader is an imagined evaluator during generation: a crowd, a generic "reader," a role you perform. The unblocking reader is a specific person you are in relation with. You talk with them side by side (V43 8:50; V30 13:05), or they are a small trusted group working under a protocol (V44).

V11's imagined audience works because it turns writing into explaining, which is talk mode (A10). V36 holds both sides in one video: safety for play, stakes for the finished work.

V23's fear sits between the two. Fear about a specific paragraph is a metal detector pointing at the valuable part (V23 30:00 to 31:54). Fear of being judged as a person is the Symbolic clamp.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- While generating, remove every evaluator ("nobody will read this").
- While shaping, replace "the audience" with one named person the writer respects, and ask what the writer wants that person to see (V45 5:09).
- For feedback, recommend a few trusted early readers under a protocol rather than a crowd.
- If a particular passage frightens the writer, treat it as a likely heart of the piece, not as a sign to cut.

### A10. Talk it out vs don't write the way you speak

**Pull toward talk.**
- Dictate when writing feels impossible (V11 10:00 to 10:41). Talk it out; there is something special about your voice (V20 5:22, 7:40).
- Write the way you would talk, with concern for your reader (V12 18:09).
- Voice memos feed the note system (V40 2:45).
- In conversation you never plan your sentences (V45 3:04, 5:09).
- The scene of conversation anchors clear writing (V30 2:44).

**Pull against talk.**
- "Write the way you speak" is one of the worst pieces of advice. It fills prose with verbal tics (V29 0:00, 2:36 to 3:28).
- Writing the way you think produces a private structure (V49 0:00 to 6:42).
- Writing is separated from its reader by space and time (V45 3:04).

**What is going on.** It depends which part of speech you mean. V30 openly reinterprets the advice (V30 1:38 to 2:09): what writing needs from conversation is its **scene** (one listener, joint attention, responsiveness), not its casual words. Talk is also a valid **phase-one generator** of raw material. What must go in the reader draft is speech's **noise** (fillers, doublets, stock phrases; V29) and the **order of your own thinking** (V47, V49).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Dictation and "explain it to a friend" are phase-one tools. The transcript is raw material, not the reader draft.
- In phase two, check three things separately:
  - **Presence:** one reader beside you, looking at the same thing (V30 13:05 to 14:03).
  - **Noise:** run the V29 compression passes.
  - **Order:** the reader's route, not yours.
- On AI cleaning up a transcript, see F4.

### A11. Read first vs read when hungry, and where research belongs

**Pull toward reading first and research up front.**
- Be a scholar before you are a researcher: draft the literature review before designing the study (V1 3:13 to 5:23).
- Read widely about the question, trying to answer it (V38 9:31).
- Research counts as writing. He stopped separating research days from writing days (V23 38:09 to 39:56).

**Pull against.**
- Reading is food, not fuel: read when the writing gets hungry, guided by your question (V35 0:50 to 3:38).
- Research can be virtuous procrastination (V11 5:13; V23 25:45 to 27:37, 38:59).
- Research belongs in the background, and in the AI age it is the least valuable part. Put what you witnessed in the foreground (V39 10:16 to 11:29).
- Use research to build problems, not to show you have read (V19 40:04 to 42:56).

**What is going on.**
- **Genre:** V1 is about academic literature reviews, where the synthesis and ranking of sources is itself the contribution (V1 10:40; V4 3:40). V39 is about narrative science writing for general readers.
- **The question as filter:** the question tells you what is relevant (V38 5:06; V35 3:15). That is what separates research that serves the piece from research that avoids it (V23 39:56).
- **Era:** the AI-age argument that research is cheap arrives in 2026.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Ask what question the reading is answering. If there is none, it is probably avoidance.
- Encourage interleaving: write, hit a gap, read for that gap, write again.
- In academic reviews, synthesis, ranking and cross-field links are the value (V1; V4).
- In general-audience work, research supports scenes and problems from behind.
- Every citation must do a job: create tension, complicate, or support a named claim (V19 41:54 to 42:56).

### A12. Imitate models vs voice is your own and emergent

**Pull toward imitation.**
- He typed out a whole McCarthy novel; Pound told Merwin to translate (V7 7:00, 13:45).
- Sentence bird-watching and Great Sentences Mad Libs (V10 2:27 to 10:08).
- Steal style (V12 27:40 to 29:53). Copy work from memory (V14 12:35 to 13:50).
- Study a proven model book (V17). Annotate a model essay (V21).
- Reproduce the community's patterns, like an anthropologist (V31 14:02; V35 17:57).

**Pull toward your own voice.**
- Voice emerges from lived experience and cannot be built from surface rules (V36 1:10 to 5:55).
- Style is a by-product of struggling with an inquiry, an integrity between thought and word (V38 10:50 to 16:38).
- Voice-seekers had stitched together storytelling and copywriting tricks that didn't feel like them (V44 4:35 to 6:29).
- Dreams and the unconscious colour your output with a unique style (V40 3:00 to 4:00).

**What is going on.** Practice versus performance (V10 10:46), and form versus substance. Imitation trains the repertoire of forms. Voice in a finished piece comes from having something to say to someone. He draws the line himself: style is open to all, ideas belong to their authors (V12 29:15). V44's voice-seekers failed because they bolted tricks on during performance, without a relationship to a reader. Register matching (V31) is part of the social relationship, not a costume.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Prescribe imitation drills as practice: copying from memory, Mad Libs, annotating a model.
- Never offer imitation as the way to give a finished piece its voice.
- When a writer asks how to find their voice, point to three things:
  - one specific reader relationship (V44);
  - what only they have seen (V36; V39);
  - thinking the inquiry through (V38).
- Don't point them to a style checklist or a banned-phrase list (V36 5:02).

### A13. Writing is thinking vs thinking happens off the page

**Pull toward writing as thinking.**
- Writing is thinking (V3 11:39; V11 6:14).
- Writing is how you become an expert (V17 2:43 to 4:07).
- The page is a laboratory (V20 4:04 to 5:22).
- Write first, understand later (V35 0:50 to 6:15).
- Language and thought can't be separated (V38 21:34).

**Pull away from it.**
- A tweet claiming you can't think if you don't write goes too far. McCarthy's Kekulé essay argues that problems get solved outside language, and his permanent note records the claim that thinking doesn't happen in language (V40 5:53 to 12:17).
- The unconscious works the night shift and reports back in images (V41 16:41 to 20:09).
- Maybe the thinking happens in the shower or asleep, and writing is how you discover it (V42 16:02).

**What is going on.** Era. This is a genuine revision that is still open, in April and May 2026. He leaves it open deliberately as a chain of notes that invites a counter-argument (V40 12:17). His practice doesn't change: writing remains the main tool for discovering and making thinking legible.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Use his later phrasing: writing is where you find out and make visible what you think.
- Build in incubation. When progress stops, stop. The next day, capture images and hunches (V41 17:55).
- Don't claim writing is the only place thinking happens.

### A14. Writing is hard vs writing should feel free

**Pull toward hard.**
- Only amateur writers say writing is easy (V12 0:00).
- Easy writing makes for hard reading (V26 0:00).
- Stakes and struggle make you learn (V36 13:48 to 19:06). Style comes from struggling with the truth (V38 11:44).
- Rewriting for readers is emotionally hard (V47 10:18).

**Pull toward free.**
- Writing should be fun. People hate it because they treat what is really two processes as one (V22 34:30).
- The habit has to be fun to last (V23 18:04 to 19:52).
- Whimsy and play (V36 19:29 to 24:24). The V40 title promises effortless writing.
- Get out of the way (V41 27:53, 29:57). The artist phase should feel free and energised (V42 17:29).

**What is going on.** Stage. Generation should feel like play; the hard part there is getting out of your own way. Shaping for the reader is where the labour lives, because the writer does the work so the reader doesn't have to. V40's "effortlessly" is packaging for a note system.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Set expectations by stage: exploring is light, fast and unjudged; shaping is effortful and methodical.
- If exploring feels like grinding, judgment has probably leaked in.
- If shaping feels effortless, suspect the writer is still reading from inside their own head (A15).

### A15. Rest the draft and reread it vs you can never read your own draft cold

**Pull toward rereading.**
- When you lose faith, reread everything slowly, as if for the first time, then outline what is actually there (V23 22:22 to 24:09).
- Let freewriting cool before reading it with the analytical mind (V41 24:59 to 27:01).
- Read a piece once for your gut response, put it away for days, then reread it analytically (V44 22:48 to 24:17).

**Pull against.**
- Resting a draft and reading it as a stranger is impossible, and he means that scientifically. Briefed readers miss structural gaps (V50 2:00 to 4:24).
- The coffee stain: rereading your own words triggers your memory of the idea (V24 0:52 to 1:40; V49 1:49 to 3:40).
- You can't judge your own flow by rereading (V27 17:46).

**What is going on.** The purpose of the reread differs.
- Cooling helps you **see what you actually have** and switch from generator to analyst. That covers finding the lines with shine, outlining the actual logic, and locating your reactions.
- It cannot make you a cold reader for **gaps in clarity and coherence**, because you are always briefed.
- V44's two readings are of other people's texts.
- V23's reread ends in an outline of what is there, which is a structural tool, not an intuition check.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Use cooling for two things: evaluating freewrites and making the reverse outline.
- Never accept "it reads fine to me" as evidence of clarity.
- Run clarity and coherence checks by location (skim test, topic underline, point anatomy), or with genuinely cold readers.

### A16. Share and publish early vs revise and test before release

**Pull toward early.**
- Show struggling work to someone you trust; going only when proud is like seeing a doctor only when healthy (V23 24:09 to 25:45).
- Being evaluated teaches more than another month of polishing (V23 27:37 to 29:19).
- Share early, messy work with a small audience (V19 1:11:07).
- Publishing your ideas brings opportunities you couldn't plan (V44 13:07 to 14:02).

**Pull toward holding back.**
- Nothing may be submitted until it has been revised two or three times (V36 18:08).
- Don't write your method book until the method is tested (V17 20:01).
- Self-publishing without an editor is risky (V18 33:25).
- Keep readers out of the room while thinking (V38 18:28).

**What is going on.** It depends what is shared and with whom.
- Struggling work goes to a trusted person, for diagnosis.
- Finished work should be published sooner than perfectionism wants.
- Permanent, high-stakes artifacts (a book, a grant, a method) need real-world testing and editing first.
- Public readers never enter the thinking phase.

**Resolution for the skill.** Separate three things.
1. Early readers: share early, privately.
2. Publication: go once the reader-draft checks pass, and don't loop on polish.
3. High-stakes or permanent work: test with real readers, get an editor, revise several times.

## B. Stance, claims and value

### B1. Hedge vs be bold

**Pull toward hedging.**
- Calibrate certainty with hedges and boosters. Heavy qualifying makes weak reading, but calibration shows you have weighed your claim (V3 9:31 to 11:39).
- Qualifiers such as "might," "often" and "in some cases" avoid a preachy, cocky persona, though he dislikes writers who are too hedgy (V18 32:35 to 33:25).
- Elbowing into the conversation by calling everyone wrong gets you thrown out (V12 16:14 to 18:09).
- The devil's-advocate arrow admits where the claim runs out (V43 6:49 to 7:08).
- A dash pair can carry a mitigation without derailing the point (V34 7:45).
- Aim for the calibrated middle of the scale (V50 30:18 to 35:11).

**Pull toward boldness.**
- School's advice to be nuanced and objective is what readers hate, and bold takes spread (V16 0:22, 2:03).
- In a book meant to start conversations, drop the hedging: argue the strongest version and leave the qualifying to readers (V17 12:13).
- Gift books speak with calm authority, with no "maybe" and no "it depends" (V17 35:19 to 36:43).
- If a book doesn't make someone mad, it may not be worth writing (V17 9:13).
- Make the judgment call; a neutral survey is a Wikipedia entry (V39 3:20 to 4:58).
- Commit and don't waffle (V43 5:43). Then write with full confidence (V19 1:05:09).
- Cut "very" and the fillers that make experts sound unsure (V6 1:48; V29 3:28 to 4:44).

**What is going on.**
- **Genre and audience:** peer-reviewed communities expect vigorous attack, so hedging suits them. Outside academia the same hedging reads as lack of confidence (V19 1:02:55 to 1:03:38; V17 12:13 contrasts academic restraint with idea books).
- **Element:** boldness belongs to the stance and the thesis. Calibration belongs to individual claims.
- **Evidence:** confidence has to be earned with a reason and evidence the reader accepts (V19 1:04:23; V50 17:24 to 18:24).
- **Persona:** arrogance is a matter of how the author sounds on the page, not of how bold the thesis is (V18 25:41 to 27:28).
- **Era:** V50 (Sep 2026) supplies the settled mechanism. Calibrate at the verb and the subject noun, not with stacked modals or intensifiers such as "obviously" (V50 31:47 to 35:11).

**Resolution for the skill.** Be bold in position and precise in scope.
1. Take a clear, arguable stance and make judgment calls.
2. Calibrate each claim by narrowing its subject and choosing one precise verb. Flag stacked hedges and stacked absolutes alike.
3. Set the default level by community:
   - peer-reviewed academic writing: more modulation;
   - practitioner and general nonfiction: direct;
   - idea or manifesto books: the boldest defensible case.
4. Always pair confidence with a reason and reader-accepted evidence.
5. Put limits and concessions in a dedicated move (a devil's-advocate section, a mitigation) instead of diluting every sentence.

*Illustration (mine).*
- Over-hedged: "It may perhaps be the case that some remote teams could possibly lose some cohesion."
- Strident: "Remote work obviously destroys every team."
- Calibrated: "Remote teams that replaced standups with chat threads lost their fastest channel for spotting blocked work."

### B2. Pick a fight vs concede first

**Pull toward a fight.**
- Picking a fight is the quickest way to win readers over (V16 12:21). Name a shared enemy and throw stones at it (V16 12:21 to 15:14).
- His own channel's villain is AI slop (V16 13:10).
- If the book doesn't make someone mad (V17 9:13).

**Pull toward concession.**
- At the researchers' table: acknowledge prior work, show deference, disagree politely, admit your limits (V12 14:37 to 18:09).
- Concede first. Tip your hat before coming off the top ropes. A scholar's combative book probably cost him with his own community (V19 35:19 to 40:04).
- Concede what the status quo got right (V22 8:14; V27 29:22).
- No opening hot take; find common ground first (V28 4:20 to 6:16).
- With resistant readers, acknowledge their position, then show it is less stable than they think (V24 10:50 to 12:20).
- Sit on the same side of the table (V22 20:49 to 23:32).

**What is going on.**
- **Target:** his villains are always systems, trends or harmful shortcuts that the reader already dislikes (V16 14:42). You stand with the reader against something. The concession is owed to the view the reader holds, or to the community you want to join.
- **Goal:** V16 is about positioning a book for word of mouth. V19 is about winning a peer community's acceptance. He adds there that you don't always have to concede (V19 38:53).
- **Order:** even a piece with a villain gets readers nodding before the turn (V28).
- **Era:** the later videos (V27, V28) formalize concession as a standard move.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Never attack the reader's own belief head-on. Use status quo, concession, the turn ("but"), the cost, then the solution.
- A villain is acceptable when it is an external system the reader already resents, and fairness still applies.
- Increase deference when the writer needs a community's acceptance (academia, a professional field).
- Test: would a thoughtful holder of the opposing view feel understood?

### B3. Persuasion vs understanding

**Pull toward persuasion.**
- Authors are in the business of persuasion (V16 2:43).
- Nonfiction is persuasion, toward actions or beliefs (V19 14:33).
- In nonfiction you are almost always persuading (V20 21:38 to 24:25).
- The Arthur/McCarthy article is essentially persuasion (V22 22:52).
- A literature review persuades readers your research matters (V4 8:45).

**Pull toward understanding.**
- Writing manuals are wrong that an essay's goal is persuasion. An essay helps readers understand. White papers persuade (V25 2:24 to 3:18).
- An essay's readers want to use it to understand the world (V43 3:08).
- Nonfiction exists to change the reader's ideas (V42 21:48 to 23:06).

**What is going on.** Genre, and the definition of the word.
- In V16, V19 and V20 "persuasion" is broad: moving any belief or action.
- V25 (Oct 2025) narrows it to getting a position or an action, and assigns that to white papers, proposals and pitches.
- The persuasive means (an inviting tone, answering objections) serve understanding in both.
- The common core, stated in V42, is changing the reader's ideas.
- Conceptual problems are upstream of practical ones, so changing understanding usually changes action too (V18 4:21; V22 10:19).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- The universal target: what will the reader understand afterward that they don't now?
- For proposals, white papers, pitches and grants, add a second target: what should they decide or do?
- In essays, don't push a verdict before the reader understands the situation.
- Prefer an inviting, side-by-side tone ("let's look," "notice") to a debater's tone (V22 22:29 to 23:32).

### B4. Originality is the point vs originality isn't value

**Pull toward originality.**
- Valuable writing thinks thoughts not thought before, instead of pastiche (V20 4:47).
- Greatness means producing new knowledge (V23 30:00).
- If you know exactly what you'll say, AI can replace that writing (V35 8:11).
- Only you can supply lived experience (V36). Research is now free; what you witnessed is not (V39 11:11).
- Most nonfiction is forgettable; you need an argument (V17 9:40). A claim must be non-obvious (V3 8:00).

**Pull against originality as value.**
- Original isn't valuable. An idea is an invention taken to market, and it becomes knowledge only when readers adopt it (V25 3:18 to 7:01).
- The gap model ("nobody has studied X") is not value. Value is repairing the reader's model (V31 14:34 to 18:37).
- Novelty and effort are not value (V27 26:10 to 26:33; V28 1:15 to 3:57).

**What is going on.** Necessary versus sufficient. New thinking is necessary; without it you are reshuffling and replaceable. But novelty alone isn't value. Value is new thinking framed as the solution to a problem readers already have. "Original" also means different things in the two groups:
- In V20 and V35 it describes the process: generating rather than reshuffling.
- In V25 and V31 it describes the pitch: "first to study."

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Flag value claims built on "nobody has written about," "first study," "gap in the literature," or "I spent months." Ask which belief or model of this reader the idea fixes, and at what cost to them.
- Also flag pieces that only reshuffle known ideas, and send the writer back to exploring.

### B5. Serve the reader's interests vs avoid audience capture

**Pull toward the reader's interests.**
- Write about what readers already want to understand. Shifting what they care about is rare, hard and risky (V25 3:18 to 7:01).
- Filter everything by the reader's problem (V18 13:23).
- Readers care about their problems, not your ideas (V24 8:15).

**Pull toward your own curiosity.**
- Skip phase one and you get audience capture. You generate nothing new, and it corrupts your thinking and character (V38 17:35 to 21:34).
- Reader focus is not audience capture (V25 4:50; V42 20:50).
- Follow curiosity: whimsy (V36 19:29 to 24:24), and the elephant that knows where it is going (V41 14:41 to 16:41).

**What is going on.** Stage, and which thing is being shaped. Your ideas come from your own inquiry in phase one. Their positioning comes from the reader's problems in phase two. The overlap is topic choice. V39 shows the reconciliation: assume the reader doesn't care, then build a bridge from what they do care about to what you care about (V39 16:37 to 19:04).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Never suggest what the writer should think in order to please readers.
- Do help find the reader problem the writer's idea answers.
- If no connection exists, say so plainly: find the connection or pick another topic (V25 7:01). Don't bend the idea to fit.

### B6. Narrow reader vs broad audience

**Pull toward narrow.**
- The Victim: one specific person in pain (V16 7:47 to 12:21).
- Write to one person (V17 31:55).
- Name a specific reader with a specific problem (V19 10:39 to 14:33).
- Answer the four reader questions (V24 3:01 to 3:22).
- Writers agnostic about their audience never quite make it (V35 17:57).
- Write to early readers you respect (V44), and to one person, as on a first date (V45).

**Pull toward broad.**
- Broad pain works for a mass-market bestseller (V16 8:36 to 8:58).
- The Booking Agent book is built for reach and stays useful to the mass market (V17 24:55 to 26:24).
- Multiple audiences are fine, like simultaneous games of chess (V25 2:24).
- Present the same argument to colleagues and to a library audience (V19 55:31 to 56:18). Scale the consequence to each reader group (V28 9:34 to 10:19).
- Skilled writers address specialists, then write a popular book (V42 14:20).

**What is going on.** Breadth is set by the goal (V16 8:36; V17 24:55). Even broad pieces are written as if to one person beside you (V30 13:05). Their stakes are then tailored per segment.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Ask the writer's goal first. Narrow by default.
- Allow broad only for reach goals. Even then, pick one representative reader to write to, and state the costs for each segment.
- For multiple audiences, treat each as its own game, with separate stakes and possibly separate versions.

### B7. Use value words vs don't lean on value words

**Pull toward value words.**
- Harvest opportunity, cost and urgency words. Circle the value words in your draft: you want a dense cluster at the start and at least one in each paragraph's first sentence (V19 16:59 to 18:46, 49:20 to 54:13).
- Loaded words frame the status quo as dated (V22 6:18 to 8:14).
- Instability words such as "but" and "however" signal value (V31 4:34; V35 16:49).

**Pull against.**
- Words like sustainability, diversity and progress are used as if self-evidently good. That preaches to the converted (V39 17:32).
- "Obviously" and "of course" usually cover a gap in the argument (V50 31:47).
- Emphasis devices lose force through overuse (V34 8:51 to 9:42).
- The author's belief that a topic is important is not value (V28 1:59 to 3:57).

**What is going on.** Whose words they are, and what stands behind them. A value word works when it names stakes the reader already recognizes, in the reader's own code, and is attached to a real problem and its cost. It fails when it asks the reader to share the writer's values, or when it replaces the reason.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Use the circle-the-value-words check to detect missing stakes.
- For each value word, check that a consequence is stated, and that the word belongs to the reader's community.
- Replace self-evidently-good abstractions with the concrete thing the reader already cares about (V39 17:32 to 18:18).

## C. Reader relationship and voice

### C1. Be personally present vs keep yourself out ("I," "you," the personal essay)

**Pull toward presence.**
- A flesh-and-blood "I" adds a story layer and persuades. "Don't use I" as a blanket rule is a misconception (V4 8:45 to 14:02).
- Use personal language in the abstract and introduction, and own your work: don't be shy (V5 5:53 to 9:01).
- Personal pronouns, anecdotes and humour build a relationship (V19 1:00:28 to 1:02:00).
- "I," "we" and "let's look" (V22 20:49 to 23:32).
- Voice from lived experience (V36). Put what you witnessed in the foreground (V39 8:57 to 11:29).
- Ordinary experience is valuable material (V21 0:00, 35:33).

**Pull toward absence.**
- His professor's margin note challenged his standing to write "I think" (V3 0:51).
- Avoid "I" about your own research, and in controversies (V4 13:03 to 13:24).
- Keep methods and results impersonal (V5 6:47 to 8:01).
- A habitual "you" as grammatical subject is preachy. Make the idea the subject (V18 27:28 to 32:35; V19 1:01:27).
- Essay readers aren't mainly interested in the writer (V43 3:08). Private symbols are like describing a dream (V43 0:21 to 1:11).
- The formal "no I, no you" rule exists for a reason (V22 21:41).

**What is going on.** Five distinctions.
- **Section:** front matter differs from methods and results (V5).
- **Role of "I":**
  - "I" as the owner of a backed claim, or as a witness, is good.
  - "I think" standing in for reasons is bad. That is the lesson of V3.
- **Role of "you":**
  - "You" or "we" addressing the reader inside a shared scene is good (V30; V22).
  - "You" as the habitual actor in sentences that are really about ideas lectures the reader (V18).
- **Personal as material vs personal as subject:** V21 and V43 agree that experience must aim beyond itself. V21 wants a truth beyond the facts; V43 wants passion redirected at the world (his word is sublimate, V43 4:05).
- **Era:** V4 (Apr 2024) turns V3's lesson around. V5 (May 2024) limits it by section and reverses V4's own "avoid I for your own research" exception.

**Resolution for the skill.** Run five checks.
- Is the writer present where ownership, motivation and witnessing matter?
- Is "I think" replacing a reason? If so, supply the reason and evidence.
- Are methods and results about the work, not the researcher?
- Is "you" the subject of sentences that are really about an idea? If so, put the idea there.
- Does personal material work as a window onto something the reader can see, or only as "this mattered to me"? The writer's life is the lens; the world is the subject.

### C2. Readers can't diagnose vs test with real readers

**Pull against reader feedback.**
- Of all people, readers are the least useful source of feedback on a draft (V47 4:44). They report friction, not causes (V47 4:44 to 7:16).
- "Unclear" and "dense" describe feelings (V32 3:24 to 3:50; V24 15:50; V35 12:09).

**Pull toward readers.**
- Pitch the book aloud to strangers and watch their faces (V16 5:28 to 7:47).
- Curiosity conversations: until you watch someone use the text, you are guessing (V17 34:36; V19 1:11:07 to 1:13:02).
- Mine the two- and three-star reviews for recurring complaints (V18 1:47 to 2:38).
- Early readers you respect, working under a protocol (V44 15:30 to 34:31).

**What is going on.** Readers are good sensors and poor diagnosticians. V44's protocol trains readers to do more than report feelings:
- state what the piece is trying to do before critiquing it;
- read twice and locate where each reaction lives (a body scan, V44 23:37 to 24:17);
- discuss the text while the author stays silent (V44 25:15 to 27:14).

V18 mines recurring complaints, which is also sensing, not diagnosis. So use readers for **where**, and the writer's knowledge of reader patterns for **why**.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Treat reader reactions as friction flags tagged to locations.
- Never implement feedback words literally ("dense" does not mean "shorten").
- Map each flag to a structural cause using the diagnostics.
- Recommend small, trusted groups using a protocol, with the author silent, rather than crowds.
- The skill is itself an unbriefed reader of the draft, so it can play the cold reader. It should still diagnose, not just report how the text feels.

### C3. Make it explicit and effortless vs leave work for the reader

**Pull toward explicit.**
- Easy writing makes for hard reading; the writer does the work (V26 0:00).
- Readers invent the links you leave out, or blame themselves (V18 18:31 to 20:19). Make logical connections explicit (V24 18:11 to 21:04).
- Answer "so what?" in the text itself (V19 1:07:30 to 1:08:47).
- Put the point up front (V24 4:18 to 7:28).

**Pull toward implicit.**
- Author-splaining is a cardinal sin: cut speech-verb adverbs and named emotions, use strategic ambiguity, and use ellipsis for shame (V13 1:21 to 19:06).
- Understate the serious; restraint lets the imagination fill the gap (V12 38:43 to 40:20).
- Let readers make the link through a crossover of vocabulary. Deliver the abstraction through a concrete moment. Let the selection of details carry the theme (V21 5:47 to 7:07, 10:34, 27:01).
- An intuition pump lets the reader reach the conclusion (V22 28:15 to 30:25).
- Trust the reader: give an image, not an explanation (V39 8:17).

**What is going on.** Level and genre, as reader 3 saw. **Parsing and logic** (what you claim, why B follows A, why it matters) should never be left to guesswork. **Feeling and inner states** in scenes and anecdotes should be shown, not labelled. V22's intuition pump sits on the line: the reader runs the scenario, and the writer then states the implication (V22 step 4). V39 does both in one video: it shows people and scenes, and it tells the verdict (V39 3:43).

**Resolution for the skill.**
1. Never make the reader guess the claim, the logical relation, or the stakes.
2. Never tell the reader what someone felt when an action or detail can show it.
3. For grave material, understate.
4. Prefer a scenario the reader can run to a bare assertion, then name the implication.

*Illustration (mine).*
- Too explicit about feeling: "The manager felt humiliated when the board rejected his plan."
- Shown: "The manager collected his printouts one page at a time while the board moved to item four."
- Too implicit about logic: "Churn rose. We changed the pricing page." Explicit: "Churn rose after we changed the pricing page, because the new page hid the monthly option."

### C4. Hooks and curiosity vs no hooks

**Pull toward hooks.**
- The title should open a curiosity loop using one of four triggers (V6 2:43 to 5:27; V46 2:16).
- Open with an ambiguous pronoun (V12 24:22 to 25:40). Find the engine of the story (V12 33:24 to 35:03).
- The Bilbo moment: a simple, concrete story before the argument (V12 41:39 to 44:07).
- Planted expectations keep the reader reading, like a hooked fish (V26 11:54 to 13:21).
- An optional prelude: a statistic, an anecdote or a quote (V28 15:14).

**Pull against hooks.**
- Readers don't care because you open with hooks instead of value (V28 0:00). Openers must build common ground, not act as hooks (V28 7:43).
- The two-vendor test: effort, excitement, reading-time warnings and famous quotes create no value (V28 1:15 to 4:20).
- Opening a point with a surprising statistic or quote is terrible (V50 24:25).
- Start with what's broken (V19 30:43).
- A promise the contents don't keep works like clickbait and loses trust (V18 2:38).

**What is going on.** It depends what "hook" means.
- The good hook is a specific open question that the text pays off. A problem is the strongest open question there is.
- The bad hook is value-free attention bait, or a promise the content breaks.
- **Scale:** a title that must earn a cold click legitimately needs curiosity (V46 1:04). A point inside the body should open with the claim, not the statistic (V50 uneven U).
- **Genre:** narrative openings differ from expository ones (see D1).
- V26 itself calls the word hooked a cliché and means a specific, planted expectation.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Test any opening device with two questions. Does it raise a specific question the piece answers? Does it lead within a few sentences to the reader's problem?
- Keep setups that pay off, and map them (V26 12:53).
- Cut value-free bait and unpaid promises.
- Allow a prelude only if it is short and leads into status quo, problem and solution.
- Inside the body, open each point with the claim, then set up the evidence.

### C5. Open with a story, scene or background vs open with the problem

**Pull toward story and background.**
- Build trust with a simple story before the argument (V12 41:39 to 44:07).
- The opening image as a seed that plants the whole cast (V21 7:53 to 8:40).
- Enter through a family story the reader already cares about (V39 17:32 to 18:18).
- Tell a familiar story, then sum it up as named concepts (V22 23:32 to 28:15).
- Uninformed readers need enough background to see the problem (V24 10:50 to 12:20). History is one source of common ground (V28 7:05).
- A film analysis may open with the scene; journalism opens with an incident (V31 3:15 to 3:35).

**Pull toward the problem.**
- Don't open with background. Start with what's broken; recipe blogs make you scroll (V19 4:05 to 4:55, 30:43 to 33:26).
- Start with a problem readers recognize, not background (V24 10:50).
- The martini glass (V31 0:52 to 5:35). Background leading to a thesis (V46 4:50).
- A background lecture before the idea is a failure mode (V28 12:20).

**What is going on.** The function of the opening material. Story and background are fine when they serve the problem:
- as common ground the reader nods at;
- as a frame that sets the stakes (the Bilbo moment);
- as the minimum context an uninformed reader needs to feel the problem.

They fail when they are the writer's warm-up or proof of homework.

Genre matters too. Narrative and creative nonfiction open on an image or scene; journalism on an incident; professional writing on the problem. Reader awareness matters as well (V24 10:50; V27 27:00).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Test: within the first paragraph or two, can the reader name the problem and say why it concerns them?
- If an opening story, image or history delays that, compress it into a prelude that points at the problem, or move it later.
- Calibrate background to the reader's awareness: uninformed, indifferent or resistant.

## D. Structure, openings and navigation

### D1. Point first vs point last

**Pull toward point first.**
- An index at the start of every unit (V18 21:12 to 22:33; V27 37:51 to 43:21; V46 8:20 to 12:43).
- Value at the top of every paragraph (V19 51:23 to 54:13).
- Move the point to the end of the introduction, not the conclusion (V24 7:28).
- Move the epiphany to the top (V31 18:37 to 21:37). Certainty up front (V47 3:55).
- Point first as the default (V50 8:23 to 9:37).

**Pull toward point last.**
- The Gessner essay states its thesis near the end, after an opening image (V21 34:19).
- Putting the value at the end is an advanced storyteller's move (V19 53:46).
- Point last, with a promise and previews of its key concepts, is an advanced option, seen mainly in creative nonfiction and at section scale (V50 9:37 to 11:36).
- Don't know your ending while drafting (V23 32:41).
- The six arrows arrive at the end where the first arrow promised (V43 7:55).

**What is going on.**
- **Genre:** professional and expository writing differs from creative nonfiction.
- **Scale:** an essay's thesis is almost always in the introduction; sections may hold theirs back (V50 9:37).
- **Skill level:** point last is advanced and needs previews.
- **Stage:** drafting discovers the point at the end; the reader draft relocates it.

He never allows the middle (V19 53:46). Point last without a promise feels arbitrary. With a promise it feels inevitable (V50 10:01).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Default: the point goes at the end of the introduction and at the top of every section and paragraph.
- Allow point last only when all three hold:
  - the genre supports it;
  - the opening promises the destination and introduces the key concepts the final point will combine;
  - the writer chose it deliberately.
- Never leave the point mid-unit.

### D2. Front-load the important thing vs the end is the place of emphasis

**Pull toward the front.**
- The point goes in the index at the top of each unit (V18 21:12; V46 8:20; V27 37:51).
- The main clause comes first and long introductory clauses move to the end (V12 48:28 to 49:26).
- Subject and verb come early: the locomotive, the runway, the tunnel (V12 0:41; V27 21:35; V8 0:42 to 2:27).

**Pull toward the end.**
- The end of the sentence is its most valuable spot (V2 9:38 to 10:48).
- The stress position (V14 5:51 to 6:43, 14:44 to 16:03; V26 13:21).
- New information goes last (V15 12:00 to 12:29; V37 4:21 to 4:53).
- Keep weak words out of the final slot (V37 16:03).

**What is going on.** Scale. Units (paragraph, section, piece) front-load their point. Inside a sentence, the start carries the familiar anchor and the end carries the emphasis. The two meet at one location that he names several times:
- the end of the first sentence, which he calls the index position (V37 7:25);
- the end of the introductory segment (V27 33:44);
- the end of the introduction (V24 7:28; V50 8:23);
- the "X; however, Y" index whose second half governs the paragraph (V46 12:43).

"Main clause first" (V12) means that the subject and verb arrive early. It does not mean the new information comes first.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- The rule: start familiar, end strong, and put each unit's point at the end of its opening.
- When moving a point "to the top," place it at the end of the first sentence or of the introduction, not as the sentence's opening words.

*Illustration (mine).*
- Point buried: "After three quarters of flat sales, a pricing review and many customer calls, we found that annual plans are the problem."
- Point at the end of the opening: "Our sales stalled for one reason: annual plans. Three quarters of flat revenue, a pricing review and forty customer calls all point there."

### D3. Signposting is bad vs readers need signals

**Pull against signposting.**
- Metadiscourse is a leftover of oral rhetoric. It bores readers; it is like a driver pulling over every 15 minutes (V9 13:32 to 16:45).
- Flow without boring signposts (V26 0:38).
- Heavy metadiscourse is a symptom of private structure (V49 10:14 to 11:14).
- Transitions are overused (V32 17:37).
- "First, second, third" is not an organizing principle (V50 13:57).

**Pull toward signals.**
- Four signals of coherence: headings built from key concepts, a short introductory segment, a point sentence, repeated concepts. Give the map before the terrain (V27 31:36 to 37:51).
- The introduction previews the structure (V22 32:40 to 34:30).
- Enumerators pointing back to a named index are worth it for heavy lists, though he doesn't love signposts (V37 11:33 to 12:26).
- Time markers are some of the dullest phrases in English, yet they do a great deal of work (V39 13:36).
- Theme preview, with "first, second, third" at section scale (V48 18:16 to 21:39).
- "For example" marks a zoom-in (V32 17:01). Label relationships with transitions (V18 22:33).

**What is going on.** Content signals versus talk about the text.
- Good signals carry content: key terms in headings, a point sentence that names its concepts, an enumerator that indexes a named set, a time or place marker.
- Bad signposting describes the text's own moves ("this section will discuss") or patches over a private order.
- **Genre:** his own spoken scripts signpost heavily, which fits his exception for oral media (V9 14:50, 15:37).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Flag sentences whose subject is the document ("This section examines," "As discussed above"). Replace them with a content index or with the question the section answers.
- Keep enumerators only when they index a named set.
- Always keep time and place markers in narrative.
- When signposting is heavy, check whether the underlying order is private. If it is, reorder rather than signpost.
- Relax these rules for talk scripts.

### D4. Titles that open a curiosity loop vs titles that state the promise

**Pull toward curiosity.**
- The title is not a keyword parking lot. It should open a curiosity loop, for example by violating an expectation (V6 2:43 to 5:27).
- Use an intrigue trigger to reach cold readers (V46 1:04 to 2:16).
- Keep an off-putting topic word out of the title (V39 0:32).

**Pull toward the promise.**
- Chapter titles should state the takeaway. Vague noun phrases and inside references break trust (V18 2:38 to 7:12).
- The Vow: a promise clear enough that the wrong readers leave (V16 3:21 to 7:47).
- Headings are built from key concepts, with no riddles or inside jokes (V27 34:45 to 35:23).
- No inside-joke titles; add three key-term nouns (V46 1:24, 2:55).

**What is going on.**
- **Scale and function:** a main title must earn a cold click, so curiosity is legitimate there. It must still decode for a stranger and anchor to the key terms. His later format is an intrigue trigger plus three key-term nouns (V46 2:55).
- Chapter and section headings orient readers who are already inside, so they state the promise.
- A book's title and subtitle together make the Vow.
- **Era:** V6 (2024) is curiosity only. V46 (2026) combines curiosity with key terms.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- For the main title, write a curiosity headline plus descriptive key terms or a subtitle.
- Check that a stranger understands the main title and that the piece pays off the loop.
- For headings and chapter titles, state the takeaway or the problem in the section's own key terms. No riddles.

### D5. Cut digressions vs a question admits digressions

**Pull toward cutting.**
- Filter every story, idea and example, and cut the side roads (V18 13:23 to 14:32).
- One theme and two or three points; cut good tangents (V22 17:32 to 19:00).
- A "favourite colour" paragraph serves no function in the genre (V20 19:13 to 21:38).
- The value audit (V19 1:05:58).

**Pull toward digressions.**
- A driving question lets in episodes that would otherwise be digressions: wartime politics, the Hunger Winter, an ant experiment (V39 1:57 to 2:47).
- A character portrait that looks like a digression illuminates the question (V39 20:04 to 21:35).
- Two time scales, and research folded into the narrative (V21 22:33, 35:10).

**What is going on.** The same test gives different verdicts. Relevance is judged against the **question or problem**, not against the topic. Material that helps answer the question stays, however far afield. Material that serves only the writer's interest goes, however close to the topic.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- For every section, ask which part of the question it helps answer, and how the reader will see that link.
- If the link is real but invisible, add a bridge, for example V39's one-word hyperlink (V39 18:44).
- If there is no link, move the material to the scraps file.

### D6. Chronology as a writer pattern vs structuring the piece in time

**Pull against chronology.**
- A chronological literature review fails to introduce your research (V1 9:44).
- Chronological organization follows the order of your discovery (V19 26:31 to 27:22).
- Don't impose a rigid chronology (V50 13:22 to 13:57).
- A strict chronology of events makes a glorified journal entry (V21 2:32).

**Pull toward chronology.**
- Structure the piece in time and mark every time shift (V39 11:29 to 13:36).
- Order clauses by the real cause-and-effect sequence (V32 9:08 to 10:31).
- Chronology is fine for a textbook (V1 9:44).

**What is going on.** Whose chronology it is:
- the chronology of the events being told, which is legitimate for narrative and causal chains;
- the chronology of the writer's discovery, which is a writer pattern;
- chronology as a default filing order, such as studies by year.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Ask whose chronology it is.
- The story's own time: keep it, with explicit time markers.
- The research journey: replace it with the order of the reader's questions.
- Dates as filing: replace them with a pattern or an argument.

## E. Sentences and words

### E1. Active voice as the default vs the passive as a main tool

**Pull toward active.**
- Make active the default. Perhaps no more than 5% of sentences need the passive (V6 11:47; V15 13:34, citing David Foster Wallace).
- Characters as subjects and actions as verbs (V8 4:39 to 6:33; V12 tools 18, 19 and 29; V27 0:41 to 3:08; V37 3:13).

**Pull toward the passive.**
- In V8 his own fixes quietly use the passive when the agent is unknown and to keep the flow (V8 6:33, 11:15).
- The passive keeps the passage's topic and hands the right character to the next sentence (V9 6:01 to 9:17).
- Passives can show a character's powerlessness (V13 13:57).
- Five legitimate uses. Strunk and White cannot even identify the passive (V14 3:03 to 10:59).
- The three-step conversion for flow (V15 13:05 to 16:16).
- Neither voice is better in the abstract (V24 16:49).
- His concrete rewrite is in the passive (V26 10:24). The passive is the tool for handoffs (V27 13:56).
- He uses this approach in nearly everything he writes (V32 0:23; passive 5:46 to 7:46).
- The passive version of a simple bees-and-honey sentence can be the better one when the reader cares about the honey (V33 0:00 to 8:08).
- Old-before-new beat a human subject in his own demo (V37 13:39 to 14:29).
- He changed his mind about active-only. Voice is a choice about agency (V42 4:27 to 9:28).

**What is going on.**
- **Era:** the passive moves from a rare exception (V6, Aug 2024) to a main tool for controlling what comes first and last (V9 onward), to an explicit reversal of his old classroom rule (V42, May 2026).
- **Scale:** V6 judges the single sentence; V9 judges the passage.
- **Separate levers:** "characters as subjects" and "actions in verbs" are not the same thing as the active voice. Tool 29's "active verbs" means verbs rather than zombie nouns.
- **Agency ethics:** the evasive passive ("mistakes were made") is never endorsed (V14 5:51; V42 7:12).
- The 5% figure is repeated twice (V6, V15) and never withdrawn, but his later practice clearly exceeds it.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Never flag the passive as an error by itself.
- First confirm it really is passive: a form of "be" plus a past participle, with the receiver of the action as subject (V14 9:57).
- Then ask, in order:
  1. Does it keep the topic string or old information first, or hand off to the next sentence? Keep it.
  2. Is the agent unknown or irrelevant? Keep it.
  3. Does it hide a responsibility the reader needs to see, as in incident reviews or apologies? Make it active.
  4. Otherwise, is a concrete character available as subject? Try the active.
- Drafting heuristic: characters as subjects, actions as verbs. Final arbiter: flow, and what the reader cares about.

### E2. What goes in the subject slot: the character, the old information, or what the reader cares about

**Pull toward characters.**
- Put concrete characters in the subject and actions in the verb (V8 4:39 to 6:33; V12 tools 18 and 19; V27 0:41 to 3:08; V37 3:13).
- Could you draw them (V32 5:16)?

**Pull toward old information.**
- Old information first, handed off from the previous sentence (V15 12:00 to 12:29; V26 4:55 to 9:19; V32 8:32).
- In his demo, old-before-new won over a human subject (V37 13:39 to 14:29).

**Pull toward reader interest.**
- Put in the subject whatever this reader cares about. Use the reader-question test (V33 2:39 to 12:31).
- Characters the reader cares about (V24 15:13 to 18:11).
- Not "you," but the idea (V18 27:28 to 34:19).

**What is going on.** He never states a single ordering. The criteria usually agree. When they don't, V37's live demo is the only direct evidence, and there flow won. Reader 7's synthesis works:
- Pick the passage's cast by what the reader cares about and what the passage is about (V33; V9 1:55).
- Keep that cast in the topic position (V32).
- Make them concrete characters where possible (V27).
- When a sentence forces a choice, keep the familiar cast member first (V37).

**Resolution for the skill.**
1. Name the passage's cast of two to four: who is this passage about, for this reader?
2. Put cast members in the topic position.
3. Prefer concrete characters.
4. Where a human agent would be new information and a cast member old, keep the cast member first, using the passive if needed.
5. Check with the topic-only underline test (V27 18:19; V48 3:00).

Present the result as a judgment with reasons, as he does (V37 13:11).

### E3. Avoid repetition and cut redundancy vs strategic repetition (and synonyms)

**Pull toward cutting.**
- Doublets, redundant modifiers, redundant categories and implied words (V29 5:39 to 11:34).
- Cut every word that doesn't change the meaning (V6 1:08). Apply McCarthy's is-it-needed test (V22 2:24).

**Pull toward repeating.**
- Don't vary topics for variety (V9 5:35).
- "Avoid repetition" is a myth (V19 43:50 to 45:45; V24 12:20 to 15:13).
- Never vary sentence openings for variety (V26 5:48 to 9:19).
- Strategic repetition (V32).
- Key terms return by repetition or close synonym (V46 13:26). Repetition is a feature (V48 12:20).
- A recurring motif is the real music of prose (V42 2:12 to 3:31).

**On synonyms.**
- Allowed:
  - a synonym for the same entity (V22 11:17);
  - pronouns and synonyms count as repeating the topic (V26 8:40);
  - hyponyms, meaning members of a named category (V32 21:13);
  - close synonyms (V46 13:26) and topic-chain links (V48 13:14).
- Restricted:
  - train readers in your naming system early (V19 45:45 to 47:22);
  - named concepts should not drift (V22 23:32 to 28:15);
  - use the same or closely related words (V24 14:39);
  - no synonyms for previewed terms (V48 20:31).

**What is going on.** They are about different objects. Cut **redundant words**, whose meaning is already present. Keep **repeated referents**, the same topic or key term across sentences, because they carry coherence. Synonyms are fine as topic links when the identity is obvious. They are wrong for central or previewed key terms and named concepts, where a new word may signal a new thing. V48 (Aug 2026) is the most precise statement.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Never suggest varying the wording of a topic or key term to avoid repetition.
- Do cut doublets, redundant modifiers and categories, and implied words.
- For key terms, lock one name per concept. If a variant is needed, introduce it explicitly at first use.

*Illustration (mine).* A preview names "cost, speed and trust." The sections should then open "Cost...", "Speed...", "Trust...", not "Expense...", "Velocity...", "Confidence...".

### E4. Plain words vs $10 words vs community jargon vs unpacking compression

**Pull toward plain.**
- More complex wording of the same ideas gets rated worse. Make the reader feel smart. Use the plain word (V5 2:05 to 4:43).
- Simple writing persuades (V6 1:08 to 2:43).
- Puffy words make you sound pretentious (V12 5:57).
- A wide vocabulary is for compression, not display (V29 13:07).
- Unpack expert compression for outsiders (V47 12:59 to 16:21).
- McCarthy's minimalism (V22).

**Pull away from plain.**
- Forget the fifth-grade reading level. Use your readers' code words (V12 3:46 to 5:57).
- Don't fear $10 words or cheap ones. No rule can be universal when readers aren't (V12 7:30).
- Learn the community's secret language (V19 16:59 to 18:46; V22 30:25 to 32:22; V31 10:11 to 14:02; V35 13:27 to 18:54).
- Jargon can be a membership card; he changed his mind about it (V42 9:28 to 14:20).
- Rejecting the "curse of knowledge" should not lead to "write at a fifth-grade level" (V47 13:39).
- Le mot juste: a precise word compresses a phrase (V29 11:34).

**What is going on.** Audience. Words are chosen for **this** reader: precise, and familiar to them. Plain does not mean generic. Jargon is right for insiders and wrong for outsiders. The fifth-grade rule is wrong because it assumes a universal reader. The common enemy is the word chosen to impress (V5; V12 5:57; V29 13:07).

The sanity check comes from V42 13:46: if you can't say it simply, the obscurity may be hiding empty thinking. Throughout, the ideas keep their complexity; only the packaging is simplified (V5 3:55; V39 8:17).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Ask which community the reader belongs to.
- Keep insider terms that carry that community's meaning.
- Unpack the writer's own compressed shorthand where this reader doesn't share it.
- Flag words chosen to sound learned.
- Never apply a reading-grade target.

### E5. Short sentences vs length follows the load

**Pull toward short.**
- Prune (V6). Keep the subject short (V8 2:27).
- The punctuation X-ray of the Arthur/McCarthy article is spare (V22 1:34 to 3:19).
- Compression (V29).

**Pull toward length.**
- Length doesn't matter if the sentence is well shaped (V29 2:15, 4:44).
- Length after the verb is fine (V27 24:45).
- A sentence is long because it has a lot to carry. The Gary Provost sentence-length meme is wrong (V42 0:00 to 4:27).
- The locomotive: a detailed sentence is clear when subject and verb come first (V12 0:41 to 2:26).

**What is going on.** What "short" refers to. The short thing is the subject and the distance to the verb, not the sentence. You cut waste, not load.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Never flag length alone, and never suggest varying length for rhythm.
- Flag long subjects, late verbs, stacked interrupters and redundancy.
- Let a sentence run when it carries linked parts, well coordinated, after the verb.

### E6. Zombie nouns are bad vs nominalizations have uses

**Pull against.**
- Zombie nouns and the three-step fix (V8 2:52 to 8:06; V12 tool 29).
- Empty verbs signal a hidden action (V32 10:53; V24 20:59).
- Fix the overloaded subject (V37 16:40).

**Pull for.**
- Four legitimate uses: a subject that links back, a replacement for "the fact that," an object, and a concept so familiar it acts as a character. The rule is that nominalizations work as objects, not subjects (V27 5:34 to 11:43).
- He uses one as an object himself (V29 13:34).
- Noun phrases as packed suitcases, his image (V32 12:05 to 13:05).
- Named concepts moved around like chess pieces (V22 23:32 to 28:15).

**What is going on.** Era (a refinement in Jan 2026), position in the sentence (object versus subject), and audience (familiar field terms).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Flag a nominalization only when it hides an action a character performs, especially in the subject position with an empty verb.
- Keep it when it links back, works as an object, packs a long phrase, or names a field concept these readers know.

### E7. State the logical link vs transitions are overused

**Pull toward explicit links.**
- Label relationships with transitions unless they are painfully obvious (V18 22:33 to 23:07).
- Logical bridges, because readers assume a relation anyway (V24 18:11 to 21:04).
- Join clauses with words that show the logic (V8 6:33 to 8:06).
- Transitions between sections (V50 15:50).

**Pull against transitions.**
- Transitions can be overused (V32 17:37).
- "However" loses force through overuse (V34 8:51 to 9:42).
- Reserve "but" and "however" for the problem turn (V28 11:00).
- "And," "furthermore" and "also" signal stasis (V31 4:34).

**What is going on.** The kind of connector. Logical connectors that state a non-obvious relation ("because," "although," "so," "unless") earn their place. Additive filler ("furthermore," "moreover," "in addition") and a "however" on every sentence don't. Much linking should be done by structure instead: old-to-new handoffs and consistent topics. Turn words keep their force only when they are rare (emphasis economy).

**Resolution for the skill.**
- For each adjacent pair, ask whether the relation is obvious from the topic chain. If not, state it with the precise connector.
- Remove additive filler transitions.
- Count "however" and "but," and keep them for real turns, especially the problem turn in the introduction.

### E8. Punctuation doesn't matter vs punctuation carries emphasis and structure

**Pull toward "doesn't matter."**
- Worrying about comma versus semicolon doesn't matter (V10 0:00).
- Comma or semicolon, en or em dash: he says the choice doesn't matter (V31 10:11).
- Online readers may not care about comma splices (V34 4:36).

**Pull toward "matters."**
- The em dash is one of the most powerful tools there is. It has three uses (example, aside, mitigation) and sits on a loudness scale above commas and parentheses (V34 2:31 to 11:48).
- The semicolon belongs in antithesis (V12 13:51).
- Run-ons are real errors in writing (V2 10:48 to 12:33).
- The punctuation X-ray (V22 1:34).
- A typo breaks a serious reader's trust (V24 21:36).

**What is going on.** Correctness worry versus rhetorical effect; he himself distinguishes syntax from semantics (V34 8:51). He dismisses anxiety about correctness as a drafting preoccupation and a school habit. He treats punctuation as a tool for emphasis and structure in revision. Mechanics still get proofread at the very end.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Don't police punctuation as right or wrong while the writer is drafting.
- In revision, suggest punctuation that changes emphasis or shows structure:
  - dash pairs for emphasized interruptions;
  - ", which" for definitions;
  - parentheses for quiet asides.
- Proofread mechanics and house style last.
- Never advise dropping em dashes to avoid looking like AI (V34 0:00 to 2:31).

### E9. Grammar is beside the point vs learn the rules, and break them on purpose

**Pull toward "grammar doesn't matter."**
- Grammar wrecks sentences and is beside the point for greatness (V10 0:00, 2:27, 6:04).
- Readers don't care about grammar (V11 15:50).
- School rules existed for grading (V31 10:11 to 14:34).
- Grammarly-perfect can still be useless (V24 5:17). Clarity over correctness (V30 0:00).

**Pull toward grammar.**
- Some rules are real: pronoun case, agreement, run-ons, dangling participles (V2).
- Learn the rules to break them properly (V2 19:54). Learn the convention first, then break it (V36 12:09).
- A deliberate grammatical "mistake" to make a line strange (V12 2:45 to 3:46; V36 9:58 to 11:15).
- His own teaching uses grammatical analysis: agent, process and goal; finite verbs; non-restrictive clauses; transitive verbs (V6; V8; V34 10:44; V37).
- Identify the passive correctly (V14 7:44 to 10:59).
- A great sentence meets the reader's expectations in its context (V10 4:40).

**What is going on.** Goal versus instrument. He rejects rule-following as the measure of quality and as a drafting preoccupation. He uses grammatical knowledge to explain reader effects and to avoid false alarms. A break is only deliberate if you know what you are breaking. V10's claim that you already know the rules intuitively is about practice drills.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Use grammatical terms in diagnosis, to explain why a sentence is hard.
- Never justify an edit with "that's the rule." Every edit names its effect on the reader.
- Protect deliberate breaks that produce an effect. He casts AI as the rule-following teacher's pet that would smooth them away (V36 13:06).
- Fix real errors that mislead the reader in the final pass.
- A break that confuses the intended reader fails his own test (V10 4:40).

### E10. Add concrete sensory detail vs compress and select

**Pull toward detail.**
- Give sights, sounds and smells, not just the name of a place (V9 12:44 to 13:32).
- The ladder of abstraction, synesthesia, and piling up images (V12 tools 7, 11 and 15).
- Ground abstractions in something physical the reader could bump into (V22 13:48 to 15:28).
- Sensory impressions are cheap to carry in memory (V26 9:19 to 11:54).

**Pull toward restraint.**
- Compression (V29). McCarthy's is-it-needed test (V22 2:24).
- Keep only the details that bear on the theme and cut inventory (V21 5:47 to 7:07).
- No visuals for their own sake (V39 9:54).

**What is going on.** Selection versus volume. A concrete word often replaces an abstract phrase and makes the sentence shorter; he contrasts a delivered package with the abstract noun "delivery" (V26 10:24). Genre matters too: piling up images (congeries) is an immersive device for narrative settings (V12 25:40 to 27:40). Expository writing needs one telling detail.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Replace an abstraction with the single most telling concrete detail, chosen by the question or theme.
- Don't add sensory inventory.
- In immersive narrative openings, piling up can be a deliberate choice.

## F. Tensions about the method itself

### F1. "No universal rules" vs "this is how readers read"

**Pull toward relativity.**
- "There are no universal rules for writing because there are no universal readers" (V12 7:30; also V31 14:02).
- Meaning is not in the text. Rules assume all readers are the same (V33 8:53 to 14:11).
- Coherence lives in the relationship; there is no universal template (V49 7:34 to 10:14).
- You can't judge a sentence without its reader (V24 1:40 to 3:01).
- Generic advice fails in professional contexts (V30 10:12).

**Pull toward universals.**
- Reading is linear, and working memory holds three or four items (V26 1:32 to 4:55).
- These principles describe how readers read, not his taste (V27 24:06).
- The fractal index pattern is not his prescription; it is what readers see anyway (V46 9:53).
- Cognitive patterns, not style tips (V48 0:00).
- A corpus of 30,000 articles and an experiment (V5 0:55). The Carnegie Mellon study (V50 2:00).

**What is going on.** Two layers.
- **Universal:** processing constraints. Readers go left to right, have limited working memory, attend to the starts and ends of units, assume every sentence is relevant (V18 18:31), and look for an index.
- **Variable:** what fills those positions. That includes:
  - what counts as old information (he says so himself, V15 5:16);
  - what counts as evidence (V25 8:27 to 11:00; V50 18:24);
  - value words and code words;
  - genre expectations (V49 12:09);
  - what the reader cares about (V33).

So there are no universal **rules**, but there are universal **locations**, and their right contents depend on the reader.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Encode positions and processes as universal checks.
- Treat their contents as reader-dependent questions.
- Run no diagnostic without a named reader.

### F2. Clear writing comes from clear thinking vs "your ideas are fine; the structure confuses"

**Pull toward thinking.**
- Harari writes clearly because he is a clear thinker (V7 28:11).
- If you can't explain it simply, you may not understand it (V42 13:46, citing Feynman).
- Without a vision, prose is hollow. The chicken-order story shows fluency without communication, his phrase for it (V45 5:09 to 11:41).
- Bad writing comes from bad thinking (V12 37:58, via Orwell).

**Pull toward structure.**
- Ideas that sound brilliant in your head confuse readers because of the structure on the page (V49 0:00).
- The problem is knowing too much, not bad writing (V47 0:00).
- It is interference, not a lack of clarity (V35 10:31 to 13:02).
- Organization matters more than vocabulary (V37 0:19).

**What is going on.** Two different failure sources, with different fixes.
- **Unfinished thinking:** the writer can't state the point or answer a follow-up question. That is a phase-one problem.
- **Finished thinking in a private structure:** that is a phase-two relocation problem.

Both are real. The skill has to tell which one it is facing.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Triage with one request: state the point in one arguable sentence, then answer "why?" and "for example?" (V45 9:06 to 11:41; V50 17:24).
- If the writer can't, it is a thinking problem. Make no sentence edits; send them to tethered freewriting.
- If they can, it is a structure problem. Run the location diagnostics.

### F3. Style and substance are inseparable vs change the form and keep the content

**Pull toward inseparable.**
- Style is not an independent variable laid on top of substance. You can't have polished prose without clarified ideas (V45 8:20, 11:41).
- Style is an integrity between thought and word (V38 15:41 to 16:38).
- The subject you choose changes what the sentence is about (V33).
- The choice of agent changes the meaning of Darwin's closing passage (V42 6:16 to 8:28).
- Red-yellow-blue: order changes meaning (V18 20:19 to 21:12).

**Pull toward separable.**
- Keep the information in each sentence the same and just change the order (V15 10:09).
- Package ideas without simplifying them (V5 3:55).
- Compress without losing meaning (V29).
- Word order is a choice, and neither order is better (V26 2:51).
- Transform a draft without losing its meaning or voice (V50 0:00).

**What is going on.** This is not a contradiction. It is an important implication: **every change of form changes the meaning the reader receives**, through emphasis, aboutness and agency. V15's claim is that the facts stay while what the reader takes the sentence to be about changes, which is the whole point of reordering. V45's claim is that only the person who holds the vision and knows the reader can choose which form carries it. A tool that sees only the words will distort it (V45 12:21 to 14:53).

**Resolution for the skill.** This is the governing constraint for a skill that is itself an AI.
- Propose, explain and let the writer choose. Never silently rewrite.
- Say when a suggested reordering changes emphasis or agency.
- Before compressing a vague sentence, ask what the writer means. A vague sentence often hides a thinking gap (F2).

### F4. Some AI uses allowed vs no AI at any stage (and the skill is itself AI)

**Pull toward some uses.**
- Using AI to clean up your talk-it-out transcript is fine (V20 7:40).
- Voice memo, AI clean-up, then save as a fleeting note (V40 3:00).
- Read-only AI agents as a navigation layer over your notes: indexes, maps, a weekly review, and asking whether you've thought about something before (V40 15:54 to 23:23).
- AI for matching people and drafting introduction emails, and for turning his own gut-reaction voice note into a bullet list (V44 11:19 to 13:07, 22:48).
- A wild generator as a springboard, never as copy (V36 19:52 to 20:15).
- Use AI or a thesaurus to find the right verb; with so many verbs available, there is no excuse not to (V50 34:15).
- A navigation layer is acceptable (V45 17:18).

**Pull toward none.**
- AI produces language, not thought (V1 2:25, 8:56).
- It builds bad rules into its checking (V14 7:44; V15 0:00).
- AI writers all sound the same, and AI research is confirmation bias (V19 0:23, 40:04).
- AI clean-up doesn't sound like you, and readers lose trust (V27 0:00).
- AI entrenches one-size-fits-all rules (V33 13:04). Its voice is confident and hollow (V34 12:17).
- Pre-known content is what AI can replace (V35 8:11).
- Don't disguise AI writing (V36 5:02).
- No AI at any stage of communicative writing. Asking AI to clean up your notes or voice memo is as harmful as restyling its output, because both produce fluency without communication (V45 0:21, 7:52 to 11:41, 17:18).
- No master prompt produces coherence (V49 7:34).

**What is going on.**
- **Era:** the line tightens. Transcript clean-up is allowed in Aug 2025 (V20) and still used for private notes in Apr 2026 (V40). By Jul 2026 it is banned for communicative writing (V45).
- **Function:** his line runs between private or logistical uses and communicative uses.
  - AI may navigate, retrieve, index, surface gaps, handle logistics, and offer word options for a thought the writer already holds.
  - AI may not supply the ideas, write or restyle prose meant for readers, summarize sources in place of reading, or clean up the writer's thinking into prose.
- **Unresolved residue:**
  - The V44 introduction emails are messages to people, drafted by AI. He doesn't address them.
  - V50's AI verb-finding sits close to the line. It seems acceptable because the writer chooses among words for a thought they already hold.

**Resolution for the skill.** This is the most consequential tension for the project, and the user should see it explicitly.
- Operate as a diagnostic coach and navigation layer:
  - ask his questions;
  - run the location-based diagnostics;
  - say where friction lives and why;
  - explain the principle and the reader effect;
  - offer options with reasons.
- Keep the writer's words the writer's.
- When asked to "rewrite" or "clean this up," first establish the stage and the intended meaning. Then show at most a minimal, labelled illustration of the move on one sentence, and ask the writer to apply it in their own words.
- Never generate the writer's ideas or evidence. Never turn phase-one material into reader-facing prose.
- Word options (V50 34:15) are acceptable when the writer has stated the thought and picks the word.

### F5. Against tricks and hustle vs his own "hacks" and clickbait packaging

**Pull against.**
- Hustle-culture advice treats writing as a commodity; *The Gift* is his manifesto against it (V7 1:29 to 2:17).
- Abandon surface-level tricks (V9 1:08).
- Against hooks (V28). Against disguising AI (V36 5:02).

**Pull toward.**
- Video titles: "4 Writing Hacks" (V9), "EXACT" (V16, V50), "ultimate guide" (V19), "DELETE your writer's block" (V23).
- He admits, jokingly, that his titles are clickbait (V38 16:38).
- Bold emotional takes spread (V16 2:03).

**What is going on.** Packaging versus substance. The substance of V9 is reader psychology, which is exactly what V9 says in its first minute.

**Resolution for the skill.**
- Take the substance, not the packaging. Don't teach "hacks" as surface tricks.
- Curiosity in titles is fine only when the piece pays it off (C4, D4).

## 3. Minor tensions, briefly

- **Read aloud vs read silently.**
  - Read the blended paragraph aloud, and read three sentences aloud to compare passive and active versions (V30 14:03; V9 6:01 to 9:17).
  - Judge punctuation silently, because your voice supplies the emphasis (V34 2:31).
  - Resolution: read aloud for presence and flow; read silently for punctuation and structure.
- **One tool at a time vs 27-step and seven-step systems.**
  - Trying many tools at once causes overwhelm (V12 51:02; V19 1:14:43; V23 40:48).
  - Yet he gives full systems (V19; V24; V50).
  - Resolution: run the system top-down, one pass per issue, and give the writer the single most important next move rather than everything at once.
- **Invite with questions vs rhetorical questions are overused.**
  - Open paragraphs with the question the reader is asking; "let's look" (V22 20:49 to 23:32; V9 15:37).
  - Writers overuse rhetorical questions; use them sparingly at transitions (V50 15:50; V22 21:41).
  - Resolution: a question is allowed only if it is the one the reader is already asking.
- **Paragraph breaks.**
  - Against Substack's one-sentence lines (V22 20:27 to 21:00).
  - Yet a one-sentence paragraph is fine when its job fits in one sentence (V22 20:27).
  - DeLillo's page per paragraph is a drafting device (V10 10:08).
  - Resolution: break paragraphs by idea, never for visual rhythm.
- **Summarizable vs unsummarizable.**
  - He approvingly reports Taleb's view that a book that can be summarized won't last (V7 19:31).
  - Yet readers should get the gist from the cover, and a book should carry one transmissible idea (V16 1:35; V17 26:24).
  - Resolution: an admiration aside, not teaching. The skill follows the clarity side.
- **"Curse of knowledge."**
  - He uses the phrase (V27 33:17), then rejects it in favour of "compression" and "interference" (V47 12:59 to 13:39).
  - Resolution: use his later terms. (His attribution of the phrase to C. S. Lewis should not be repeated without checking.)
- **Ghostwriters.**
  - You need a model, not a ghostwriter (V17 0:00), yet his model book used a ghostwriter (V17 22:31).
  - Resolution: irrelevant to the skill.
- **Morning pages vs dream logs.** See A7.
- **Research-by-AI is confirmation bias vs AI Q&A over his own notes.**
  - V19 40:04 against V40 20:35.
  - Resolution: AI over the writer's own thinking is navigation; AI as a source of evidence is not (F4).

## 4. What stays genuinely unresolved

These are open in the corpus itself. The skill should hold them as judgment calls, not rules.

1. **Is thinking linguistic?** His slogan wobbles (V38 21:34 against V40 11:53 and V42 16:02). The practice is stable; the theory is open.
2. **The AI line in practice.** The introduction emails (V44) and verb-finding (V50 34:15) sit on or near his own line (V45 17:18).
3. **Priority among criteria for the subject slot** (E2). Only one live demo decides it (V37 13:39 to 14:29), and he invites alternatives (V37 13:11).
4. **Six arrows vs no templates.** He never reconciles V42 and V43 himself.
5. **Persuasion vs understanding.** V25 criticizes the view he stated in V19 and V20 without mentioning that he held it.
6. **The 5% passive figure.** Stated twice (V6, V15), never withdrawn, and contradicted by his later practice (V32, V33, V37).
7. **How much reader feedback to trust.** V47's claim that readers make poor critics, against V44's community of early readers. The sensor-versus-diagnostician reading is an inference, though a well-supported one.

## 5. A resolution procedure the skill can run

When two pieces of his advice seem to collide over a user's draft, ask these questions in order. Stop at the first one that settles it.

1. **Stage.** Is the writer venting, exploring, shaping for readers, or finishing?
   - If venting or exploring: no reader critique, no cutting, no sentence polish.
   - If shaping or finishing: the reader is the gate.
2. **Genre and function.** Is it an essay (understanding); a proposal, pitch, grant or white paper (action); peer-reviewed academic work; narrative or creative nonfiction; a book of one of the V17 types; or a spoken script?
3. **Reader.** Which community? Insider or outsider? Cold or committed? Uninformed, indifferent or resistant? One reader or several?
4. **Scale.** Title, introduction, section, paragraph, sentence or word?
5. **Level.** Is this about parsing and logic (be explicit), or feeling and meaning (leave room)? About correctness (last) or effect (revision)?
6. **Era.** If he explicitly reversed himself, use the later position. If the later video only sharpens the earlier one, keep both.
7. **If both still apply,** present the choice to the writer. Give the reader effect of each option and let them decide. That is what he models himself (V37 13:11; V33 8:08).

## 6. Notes for whoever builds the skill

- **Never state a rule without its reader effect and its exception.** Almost every tension above arose because a tool had been stated as a rule. His recurring point is that these are tools, not rules (V12 0:41; V14 7:04; V9 6:01).
- **Ask the stage first, every time.** Stage settles more of these tensions than any other lens (A1 to A16, B5, D1).
- **Keep the writer in charge of the words** (F3, F4). Diagnose, locate, explain, offer options; don't silently rewrite.
- **Advice the skill should never give,** because he has retired or rejected it:
  - vary sentence length for music (V42);
  - always use the active voice (V42);
  - write at a fifth-grade level (V12; V47);
  - outline before the thinking draft (V42; V47);
  - write for yourself as the purpose of a published piece (V27; V38; V42);
  - avoid repetition by varying topics or key terms (V19; V24; V26; V32);
  - "every paragraph needs a topic sentence" as a rule (V46 11:08, replaced by the index);
  - rest the draft and read it as a stranger (V50 2:00);
  - drop em dashes so the text doesn't look like AI (V34);
  - end with a summary conclusion (V46 16:18);
  - justify value by a gap in the literature (V31);
  - escalate discipline for creative block (V41);
  - clean up the writer's notes into prose with AI (V45).
- **The sequence that avoids most collisions**, from the corpus:
  1. Vent or explore with a tether.
  2. Reverse outline.
  3. Triage thinking versus structure (F2).
  4. Reader brief: goal, reader, problem, code words.
  5. Point sentence, placed at the end of the introduction.
  6. Introduction: common ground, problem, solution.
  7. Section indexes, ordered by the reader's questions.
  8. Point anatomy and the uneven U.
  9. Topic strings and flow.
  10. Certainty calibration.
  11. Compression.
  12. Mechanics.
  13. Early readers under a protocol.

  Each tension above is resolved by knowing where in this sequence the writer is.
