# The Writer Science System

A working system of writing synthesized from all 50 long-form videos on William Fitzpatrick's *Writer Science* channel (Feb 2024 – Sep 2026, ~207,000 words of transcript). Everything here is paraphrased from his teaching; his own terms are in **bold**. Codes like [V37] point to the source video in the index at the end.

Scope: this is a system for **pointed nonfiction** — writing that answers a question or solves a problem for a reader (essays, articles, newsletters, reports, proposals, academic papers, expert books). It is not a system for fiction, poetry or memoir.

---

## Part 0 — The five axioms

Everything else is derived from these.

1. **The reader decides what good writing is.** Writing is not a thing but a function: meaning arises from what a specific reader does with the text. A sentence cannot be judged without knowing its reader. [V22, V33, V49]
2. **There are no writing rules, only writing tools.** There are no universal rules because there are no universal readers. Passive voice, jargon, repetition, long sentences, em dashes, nominalizations, "I" and "you" are all tools, judged only by their effect on this reader. The master test for any choice: *does this move my reader from where they are to where I want them to be?* [V2, V12, V14, V33, V42]
3. **Value = solving a problem the reader already cares about.** Clear, organized, persuasive and useless is still useless. "New", "true", "interesting to me" and "I worked hard on it" are not value. Readers trade attention for value; if they cannot perceive a problem, they cannot perceive value. The goal of nonfiction is not to express your ideas but to change the reader's. [V12, V19, V27, V31, V42]
4. **Writing has two conflicting functions, so it needs two separate phases.** First you write to think (for yourself); then you write a different document to be read (for them). The patterns you use to *generate* text **interfere** with the patterns readers use to *interpret* it. Collapsing the phases causes both writer's block and unreadable prose. [V11, V19, V20, V35, V42, V47]
5. **You cannot edit your own draft by feel.** Rereading your draft is remembering, not reading (the **coffee stain problem**); you are always the "briefed reader" who skates over gaps. Resting it two days does not fix this. Replace intuition with knowledge of the **locations where readers look**. [V24, V49, V50]

## Part 1 — The pipeline at a glance

| Stage | Mode | What you produce | Reader in the room? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Capture | ongoing | fleeting notes, scraps, sentences, stories | No |
| B. Daily freewrite | **Artist** | "magma" — raw, goal-less pages | No — not even you |
| C. Writer's draft | **Artist** | a thinking draft driven by a question | No |
| D. Bridge | switch | reverse outline + reader brief | Arrives here |
| E. Reader draft — value | **Architect** | problem, point, structure ("the bones") | Yes, entirely |
| F. Reader draft — clarity | **Architect** | paragraph and sentence flow | Yes |
| G. Compress, calibrate, polish | Editor | final text | Yes |
| H. Test | Community | feedback from early readers | Real ones |

Editing order is always **top-down**: reader → point → problem → structure → key terms → sentences → mechanics last. [V24]

## Part 2 — Phase 1: Write to think (Artist)

**Purpose:** generate knowledge. Valuable expertise is like tangled Christmas lights: too complex to sort out in your head. You don't find the idea and then write it; you produce the idea by writing. If you know exactly what you'll say before you start, you are producing what AI can replace. [V35, V47]

### 2.1 The daily practice
- **Freewrite daily with no goal.** Not a first draft, not newsletter fodder. It opens a vent to the unconscious. Let it cool, then reread later for the ~10% with "shine". Words like good/bad don't apply here. [V41]
- **Timed sessions:** 10–20 minutes, no backspacing, fingers keep moving. Use **placeholders** — type the first word and a bracket note like [better word] — and move on. [V11, V20]
- **Tether:** write the *question* you're trying to answer at the top of the page and return to it when lost. [V20, V35, V42]
- When a sentence feels wrong, don't stop — write about *why* it feels wrong. Start with "what I'm trying to say is…". Address yourself as "you" to observe your thinking from a distance. Dictate if typing stalls. [V11]
- Alternate **working with words** (stream) and **working with ideas** (zoom out, write one-sentence summaries of what stands out, pick one, freewrite on it again). [V11]
- Diagnostic: self-commentary or reaching for backspace means someone else's judgment has crept into the room. [V42]

### 2.2 The inquiry method (how to think on paper) [V38]
1. Start with a **question, not a topic**. A topic gives you a pile of things to say; a question gives you a reason to say them and filters evidence. Convert: "climate policy" → "why did this town vote against solar panels it had already paid for?" [V38, V39]
2. Say out loud: *"I don't know. What evidence would I need to answer this?"*
3. Read when the writing gets hungry (**reading is food, not fuel**) — guided by the question, not as procrastination. [V35]
4. Explain each piece of evidence to yourself in writing.
5. **Evidence + reasoning = claim.** Let the claim emerge; don't start with a thesis and decorate it with evidence ("writing in reverse").

### 2.3 Handling block [V23, V41, V44]
- Block is fear, and fear shows up only around writing that matters. Use it as a **metal detector**: ask "what am I most afraid of in this paragraph?" and write about that.
- Watch for **virtuous procrastination**: research, tidying, tooling, "strategy".
- Don't prune early (**writing anorexia**) — you can only afford to cut when rich in material. When a perfect ending occurs mid-draft, use it where it occurs.
- If stuck mid-session: new document, type non-stop for 5–10 minutes starting "I'm stuck because…". If you've lost faith: reread slowly, then outline what you actually have.
- Never add today's missed quota to tomorrow. Drop the drill-sergeant voice.
- If blocked for months, ask whether the project belongs to a previous version of you.
- His position evolved: 2025 = small, kind, consistent habits (morning, 30–90 min, tell people the habit not the outcome). 2026 = discipline is the wrong frame for creative work; get out of the unconscious's way, and find **early readers** you respect — block and flat voice rarely survive a real relationship with a reader.

### 2.4 The capture system (optional infrastructure) [V12, V40]
Fleeting notes (frictionless, timestamped) → literature notes (your dialogue with a source, not highlights) → permanent notes (one idea, your own words, linked to a source and to an existing note). Keep a weekly "teachable moment" log and a dream log. AI may *navigate* the vault (index, hub notes, weekly review, "have I thought about this before?") but never writes or edits your notes.

**Exit condition for Phase 1:** you've had the "click" — you can state what you now think in a sentence or two and you're satisfied you have the pieces. Put the writer's draft aside. Do **not** line-edit it into the final piece.

## Part 3 — The bridge

### 3.1 Reverse outline [V23, V35, V47]
Go through the writer's draft section by section and label what each part **does** (its function, not its topic). If you can't label it, it's fluff or still tangled. Lay the labels out alone and look at the sequence.

### 3.2 The reader brief — answer before writing a word of the reader draft
Fitzpatrick won't look at a client's text until these are answered. [V24, V30, V42]

- **Who, specifically?** A reading community, not "everyone". Writing for several audiences = several simultaneous chess games.
- What motivated them to pick this up? What problem are they dealing with? What answer or decision are they after?
- What do they currently believe? Where does their knowledge stop? What must they think differently afterward?
- Which kind of reader: **uninformed** (needs background to see the problem), **indifferent** (needs consequences relevant to them), or **resistant** (needs their position acknowledged, then shown to be less stable than they think)?
- **Rhetorical planning wheel:** purpose · writer's role (relation to topic and to reader) · audience · context (what event caused this text; what should happen after). "The text must match the context." [V30]
- **Problem statement** (top of page, not for publication): *I am writing about [topic] because I want to find out [question] in order to help my reader [gain benefit / avoid cost].* Delete anything that doesn't serve it. [V12]
- **Code-word hunt:** take 4–5 texts your readers share and admire; mine them "like an anthropologist" for **value words** and in-group **code words**; keep a running list and build your value statements from it. ("It pays to hit the market first" beat "early entry produces compounding benefits" for HBR readers.) [V19, V22, V35]

## Part 4 — Phase 2a: Build for value (Architect)

Open a **new document**. Start from where the reader is, not from the route you took (**directions to the restaurant**). Readers want certainty up front and prefer to move from stability → instability; you thought your way from uncertainty → certainty. Reverse it. [V19, V47]

### 4.1 The point
- Find the one sentence readers must accept or act on. It's usually at the *end* of your writer's draft. Compare the point sentence in your intro with the one in your conclusion; take the sharper or synthesize them. [V24, V50]
- A real point is **significant, non-obvious and debatable** — someone else must be wrong. "Water is wet" fails; test for the reaction "hm, I'd like to know more". [V3, V25, V50]
- **Anatomy of a point:** point ← reason ← evidence. Test with "point *because* reason". Opponents attack the reason, so check the bridge (warrant) from evidence to claim — evidence never speaks for itself. What counts as evidence depends on the reader. [V25, V50]
- Place it **at the end of the introduction** (default). Point-last is advanced: if you use it, preview the point's key terms early and strike them together at the end like a chord. [V50]

### 4.2 The introduction: problem construction [V22, V27, V28, V46]
Never open with background, definitions, or the **martini glass**. Open with a problem.

1. **Common ground / status quo** — *invert your thesis* and open with its opposite: something the reader already believes (historical background, recent event, or common belief). State it plainly.
2. **Concession** — what that belief got right or helped people do. Sit on the same side of the table; don't come off the top ropes.
3. **Destabilizing condition** — signalled by *but / however*. Reserve these words for this job.
4. **Cost or benefit** — the consequence *for this reader*. Imagine them saying "so what?"; your answer is the consequence. A condition with no cost is not a problem. Make it personal ("this prevents you from…").
5. **Solution preview** — your point: promise and direction, not details. **Practical problem** → what to do. **Conceptual problem** → what to think instead (conceptual problems sit upstream of practical ones).

Avoid the **gap model** ("nobody has studied X"): new ≠ valuable. Instead show an error or anomaly in the model your readers currently use, and what it costs them. [V31]

If you fix only one thing, fix the introduction.

### 4.3 The title [V6, V46]
The title is the real first line and must work on cold readers. No keyword parking lots, no inside jokes. Use one of four **intrigue triggers**: pose a question/puzzle · signal others know something the reader doesn't · violate an expectation · open a sequence with an unknown resolution. Reliable format: striking title + colon + three nouns that are the piece's key terms.

### 4.4 Structure: index and discussion, at every scale [V18, V27, V46, V50]
- **Fractal rule:** every unit (sentence, paragraph, section, document) opens with a short, easily grasped **index** that states the point and announces the key terms, followed by a longer, more complex **discussion**. Readers perceive the opening of a unit as its index whether you intend it or not.
- A paragraph index is 1–3 sentences. One paragraph = one job; you should be able to label it.
- **Key terms:** every important concept in the discussion is announced in the index and recurs (same word, close synonym, or category member). Limit the cast. Think of concepts as **chess pieces**: tell a story, name the concepts, then redeploy them. Train readers in your naming system before using variants.
- **Order sections by the questions your point raises in the reader's mind**, in the order they arise — not the order you discovered things, not "first, second, third" by default.
- **Transitions:** round up and push off. An occasional question works as a palate cleanser if it's the question the reader is already asking.
- **Four signals of coherence:** clear section boundaries with headings built from key terms · a short intro segment · a point sentence at its end · repeated key concepts.
- **Skim test:** read only the index positions. They should storyboard the whole argument. If not, restructure.

### 4.5 Developing a point: the uneven U [V18, V50]
Five levels of specificity (5 = most abstract, 1 = raw evidence).
Start at **4** (a claim someone could dispute; level 5 is vacuous) → **3** explain → **2** set up the evidence → **1** evidence (quote, number, case) → **3** interpret it → **4** return to the concept → finish at **5**, pushing off to the larger question. End higher than you began. Never commit a **hit-and-run quotation**; never open a point with a bare statistic.

### 4.6 Objections and stance [V3, V19, V20]
- Plan the piece as a **two-player game**: I say this → they'll think that → I address it here.
- For each major claim, pre-empt the three standard objections: other causes · counter-examples/omitted evidence · different definition of terms.
- Enter the conversation politely: acknowledge prior work, show you speak the community's language, position yourself as a helping hand.

### 4.7 The conclusion [V46]
Not a summary. Return to the problem, show at a high level that it's solved, then **push off** to a broader conversation the reader cares about — ending at a higher level of generality than the introduction.

### 4.8 Value audit [V19]
- Circle the value words (opportunity: *enables, becomes* · cost: *prevents, disrupts* · urgency: *critical, crucial* · instability: *but, however, although* · community code words). They should be front-loaded, with at least one in each paragraph's opening. Long stretches with none are a warning.
- Run **"so what?"** on every major point. "True" and "interesting" aren't reasons to keep something.
- Name the function of each sentence (problem? evidence? background?). If you can't, cut it.

## Part 5 — Phase 2b: Build for clarity (paragraphs and sentences)

Clarity is organizational, not grammatical. Two principles do most of the work. [V8, V9, V15, V26, V27, V32, V37, V48]

### Principle 1 — Characters as subjects, actions as verbs
- The subject position is a **spotlight**; a paragraph is a scene with 2–3 characters. Put the people, organizations, or familiar concepts your reader cares about in the subject. Abstractions can be characters if you make them the agents of a story.
- Put the important action in the verb. **Zombie nouns** (nominalizations: -tion, -ment, -ence) plus **empty verbs** (*occurred, resulted in, took place, was undertaken*) mean the action is buried. Fix: (1) find the action and make it a verb; (2) ask *who does what?* and make that character the subject; (3) join the resulting clauses with logic words (*because, since, although, so*).
- Legitimate nominalizations: a subject linking back to the previous sentence ("this discovery…") · replacing "the fact that" · the object of a verb · concepts so familiar they act as characters.
- **Short subject, verb early** (the tunnel / locomotive): readers hold their breath until the verb. Move long introductory clauses to the end; keep verb next to object.
- **Reader-question test** for choosing the subject: phrase the question your sentence answers in the reader's voice; the subject of their question is the subject of your sentence. ("Bees make honey" vs "Honey is made by bees" — neither is better in the abstract.) [V33]

### Principle 2 — Old before new; topic then comment
- **Topic position** (start, up to the verb): old, familiar, simple, short — links backward. **Stress position** (end): new, complex, emphasis-worthy — leans forward. The end of one sentence sets up the start of the next (relay race; sentences shaking hands).
- Keep a consistent **topic string**. Don't vary openings for variety; "avoid repetition" is grade-school advice that confuses readers. Pronouns and synonyms count as repetition.
- The **passive voice** is the main tool for getting the right thing into topic or stress position. Use it to focus on the thing acted on, when the actor is unknown or irrelevant, to land a word in the stress position, or to control agency. Other tools: *since* (marks its clause as known), ", which…" clauses and *-ing* reductions (subordinate effects and definitions), noun-phrase "suitcases".
- **Four topic-progression patterns** — choose by purpose [V48]:
  1. **Constant topic** — same topic, new comments: describe/characterize one entity. (Can feel static.)
  2. **Linking** — each topic picks up the previous comment: processes, causal chains, methods. (Risk: railroad tracks carry you off-subject.)
  3. **Whole-to-parts** — first comment names the whole, later topics are its parts: taxonomy, breakdowns. (Must state the umbrella explicitly.)
  4. **Theme preview** — first comment announces A, B, C; then take each in turn: weaving strands. (No synonyms for previewed terms.)
- **Diagnostic:** underline the first 7–8 words of every sentence (up to the verb). From those alone, could a reader predict what the passage is about? Are they concrete — could you draw them? Would *your* readers see them as one related set? If not, rewrite so subjects name your key characters. (**Movie-poster test**: who'd be on the poster?)

### Concreteness [V9, V12, V22, V26]
Use language the reader "could stub their toe on". Ground every abstraction in something visible (bulk-processing economy → "smokestack economy"). Move up and down the **ladder of abstraction**. Prefer an **intuition pump** (a scenario that lets readers work the point out themselves) to a plain example. **AAB** for examples: two similar, then one contrasting. Build trust with a simple concrete story before the argument (the **Bilbo moment**).

### Scene, not lecture [V22, V30, V44]
Anchor the text in the **classic scene of joint attention**: you and one reader, side by side, looking at the same thing. Distant audience → one companion; abstract topic → something visible; show the problem, then show the solution. Prefer "as we've seen", "notice that", questions, to **metadiscourse** ("this section will discuss…"). Heavy signposting is a symptom of **private structure**. Aim to sound like a partner, not a preacher; watch habitual "you" as subject — it drags the reader onstage and reads as finger-wagging. [V9, V18, V49]

## Part 6 — Compress, calibrate, polish

Only now — concision applied earlier is harmful. [V23, V29]

**Compression** (goal: less cognitive load, not shorter sentences) [V29]
1. Cut filler: *actually, basically, really, various, particularly, generally, certain…*
2. Cut doublets: *each and every, first and foremost, full and complete* — keep the stronger word.
3. Cut redundancy: modifiers (*future plans, free gift*), categories (*large in size*), implications spread across phrases.
4. Replace phrases with the exact word: *the reason for → why; despite the fact that → although; in the event that → if.*
5. Write in the affirmative: *not many → few; not allow → prevent; not include → omit.* Watch implicit negatives (*unless, fail, lack, without*) stacking with *not*. Keep the negative when the warning is the point.
- McCarthy's question for every mark, word, sentence, paragraph: *do you need that?* Try the **punctuation X-ray**: view only your punctuation; little between periods = simply built sentences. [V22]

**Certainty calibration** [V3, V50] — scale: over-hedged ↔ calibrated ↔ strident. Stacked intensifiers (*obviously, never, every*) usually cover a gap in the argument; stacked hedges (*might arguably, somewhat, perhaps*) tell readers nothing is at stake. Fix two slots: the **verb** (one precise verb instead of modal stacks) and the **subject noun** (narrow its scope). Hedging norms are cultural: peer-reviewed fields expect more; elsewhere hedges undermine you.

**Emphasis economy** [V34] — *however*, em dashes and signposts lose force with overuse. Interruptions by loudness: em dashes > commas > parentheses. Em dashes are for emphasis (exemplification, aside, mitigation); put definitions in a ", which…" clause.

**Mechanics last** — grammar, punctuation, house style, platform conventions. [V24]

## Part 7 — Test with real readers [V19, V44]

- Find **early readers**: people you respect and want to be respected by. Groups of 3–4 for honest talk; seminars of 5–12 for workshops.
- Untrained feedback ("unclear", "dense", "disorganized") describes friction, not its cause — don't act on it literally. [V47]
- **Letter of response** (2 pages, to the author): first state as precisely as possible *what the piece is trying to do*; then what you'd steal; only then what's missing.
- **Two readings:** first as a normal reader (record a gut-reaction voice note, put it away); a day later, analytically — locate *where in the text* each effect happened (body-scan reading). Look for where things are the same and where they differ.
- **Silent-author rule:** the author says nothing while the piece is discussed — just as in publication.
- Success indicators: readers act on the piece; you can predict objections; people who disagree still find it useful; it gets shared.

## Part 9 — Genre modules

- **Essay (reader's definition):** helps specific readers understand better something they *want* to understand. Deals in arguments of judgment, which exist only by community consensus — so the reader is the batter, not the icing. [V25]
- **Impersonal essay (Zadie Smith's six arrows):** title in the centre; write the opening and closing lines first; then 1 introduction/promise → 2 development → 3 devil's advocate → 4 wind down → 5 reprise with variations → 6 conclusion. Turn passion outward; be a window onto the world, not a confession. Use in the Architect phase only. [V43]
- **Creative nonfiction from ordinary life:** aim beyond the facts. Opening image contains all characters and themes; keep a **web of signification** (one image family); bring research in sparingly and inside the narrative; plant concepts early and call them back. [V21]
- **Narrative idea/science writing:** drive with one question; **foreground** (what you saw: people, rooms, props) vs **background** (research — now nearly free, so least valuable); mark every time shift plainly; set up payoffs; never stop the story to define a term; assume the reader doesn't care and build a bridge from what they do care about. [V39]
- **Academic:** literature review as "evidence of an organizing mind" — story not plot, cast studies as major/minor characters and allies/adversaries, mind-map before matrix; synthesize, prioritize, cross disciplines. Simple syntax, personal language in abstract/intro, impersonal in methods/results, present tense for your own findings. [V1, V4, V5]
- **Expert nonfiction book:** pick the type by goal (personal brand builder, new authority, lead getter, booking agent, legacy, gift). Position with the **3Vs**: **Vow** (a promise sharp enough that wrong readers leave), **Victim** (one specific person in pain, named in their words), **Villain** (what they already hate but haven't named). Purpose chain: your goal → who must read → their pain → table of contents as a bridge → filter everything. Chapter titles are promises, not noun phrases. [V16, V17, V18]
- **Dialogue / literary restraint:** no **author-splaining**; cut adverbs on speech verbs and emotion labels; leave gaps; use ellipsis — omit the most charged exchange. [V13]

## Part 10 — His position on AI [V19, V36, V45]

Never for communicative writing at any stage — not drafting, not "cleaning up" your notes, not research. Style is not a coat of paint over substance; AI sees only the words that index your thought, never the thought, so it returns a funhouse mirror. The diagnostic: if the reader talked back, could you elaborate? Acceptable: navigating your own notes, transcribing/bulleting a private voice note, logistics, divergent "surprise me" play. The human moat: emergent voice, embodied metaphor, deliberate rule-breaking, stakes, and whimsy.

## Part 11 — Advice he has retired

| Conventional advice | His replacement |
|---|---|
| Vary sentence length for "music" | A sentence is long because it has much to carry; music = thematic/key-term echo |
| Always use active voice | Voice is chosen by topic continuity, stress, and agency |
| Avoid jargon / write at 5th-grade level | Language is social; use your community's code words deliberately |
| Outline before you write | Artist first; the outline is already in the first draft — find it |
| Write for yourself, ignore readers | True for Phase 1 only; a "bad export from fiction" otherwise |
| Avoid repetition; vary openings | Strategic repetition of topics and key terms creates coherence |
| Every paragraph needs a topic sentence | Index (1–3 sentences) because that's where readers look — function, not rule |
| Write first, revise later / cut ruthlessly always | Drafting, research and revision are recursive; cut only when rich |
| Discipline beats block | Get out of the way; find readers you respect |
| Rest the draft and read it as a stranger | Impossible; use structural diagnostics |
| Hook the reader | Give them a problem |
| Conclusions summarize | Conclusions resolve and push off higher |

**Tensions he leaves open (and how the system resolves them):**
- *Hedge vs be bold:* depends on community norms and genre; always back claims with reason + evidence the reader accepts.
- *Anti-template vs his own fixed sequences:* his sequences describe reader *functions* and locations, not forms; and they apply only in Phase 2.
- *"Readers give useless feedback" vs community feedback:* untrained readers report symptoms; the letter-of-response protocol makes feedback usable.
- *Rewrite from scratch vs salvage:* in practice, salvage the points from the writer's draft and rebuild the structure around them.
- *"Writing is thinking":* his core slogan through early 2026; by Apr 2026 he is privately questioning whether thinking happens in language at all. The practice doesn't change.

## Part 12 — One-page checklist

**Before drafting for readers**
- [ ] I have a writer's draft and had the "click"; I can state my point.
- [ ] Reverse outline done; every part has a function label.
- [ ] Reader brief answered; problem statement at top of page; code-word list made.

**Bones**
- [ ] Title uses an intrigue trigger and works cold.
- [ ] Intro: common ground → (concession) → *but* → cost to this reader → solution preview.
- [ ] Point sentence sits at the end of the introduction; it is debatable; point ← reason ← evidence holds.
- [ ] Sections ordered by the reader's questions; each opens with an index that names its key terms.
- [ ] Skim test passes.
- [ ] Each point runs the uneven U (4→1→5); no hit-and-run evidence.
- [ ] Three standard objections pre-empted.
- [ ] Conclusion resolves the problem and pushes off higher.
- [ ] Value words front-loaded; every point survives "so what?".

**Flow**
- [ ] First 7–8 words of each sentence: a small, concrete, consistent cast.
- [ ] Characters are subjects; actions are verbs; no empty verbs.
- [ ] Short subjects, early verbs; old → new; ends hand off to next beginnings.
- [ ] Right topic-progression pattern for each passage.
- [ ] Abstractions grounded; metadiscourse minimal.

**Finish**
- [ ] Filler, doublets, redundancy cut; phrases → words; negatives → affirmatives.
- [ ] Certainty calibrated at verb and subject noun.
- [ ] *However*, em dashes, signposts used sparingly.
- [ ] Mechanics and house style.
- [ ] Sent to early readers with the letter-of-response protocol.
